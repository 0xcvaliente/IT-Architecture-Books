# 1 The enterprise architecture problem

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain how locally sound solutions can create cost and risk for the enterprise as a whole.
- Define enterprise architecture and set the boundary of the enterprise for a given decision.
- Construct a benefit mechanism from an intervention to an outcome, with observable intermediate steps.
- Evaluate a benefit claim by identifying rival explanations and the data needed to test them.
- Describe how an enterprise architecture function is organised: its roles, placement, operating rhythm and measures of effectiveness.

## Good solutions can make a poor enterprise

A well-designed solution can still create an enterprise problem. Three examples from Helios show the pattern:

- Retail Distribution introduces a new customer database that fits its workflow well, but uses identifiers that group reporting cannot match to the other units.
- A new cloud service is secure in isolation, but depends on an account-removal process that nobody owns.
- A low-cost order application saves licence fees, but keeps an expensive manual reconciliation between two divisions in place.

Each decision was reasonable for the team that made it. The cost appears only when the decisions are viewed together, across units, systems and years. Enterprise architecture exists to make those relationships visible before they become expensive.

## What enterprise architecture is

The international standard for architecture description distinguishes an **architecture**, the abstract set of fundamental concepts and properties of a system or enterprise, from the descriptions that express it {REF:iso42010}. Applied to a whole organisation, **enterprise architecture** describes how capabilities, processes, information, applications, technology and decision rights fit together, and uses that description to choose and sequence change. Ross, Weill and Robertson give a more strategic definition: the organising logic for business processes and IT infrastructure, reflecting the integration and standardisation requirements of the organisation's operating model {REF:eastrategy}. Both definitions point to the same job: deciding what the organisation must share, what it can leave local and how to get from here to there.

The **enterprise** is whatever boundary the decision needs. It may be a group of companies, a public agency, a single division or an ecosystem of partners. State which entities the architect can influence and which are external authorities, such as regulators or suppliers. For Helios, the enterprise is the group and its three units; the customers, suppliers and regulators are outside it but shape its choices. {FIG:enterprise} shows the basic structure: local capabilities in each unit, a few shared decisions beneath them, and enterprise outcomes above.

## Architecture informs decisions

An enterprise model has value when it changes a decision: whether to consolidate two applications, fund a shared capability, accept an exception or delay a migration. A small, accurate model used in an important investment review is worth more than a complete repository that nobody consults.

Different audiences need different content:

| Audience | What they need | Helios example |
|---|---|---|
| Board | Choices, costs, consequences and uncertainty | Options for the customer platform, with costs and risks |
| Capability owner | Process and data implications | How a shared identifier changes the entitlement check |
| Delivery team | Boundaries, standards and transition duties | Which system is authoritative for each customer fact |
| Operations | Service dependencies and recovery ownership | What fails if the workforce identity service is down |

One giant diagram cannot serve all four.

Drawing the model gives the architect influence, and authority only where it has been delegated. Be explicit about where the role advises, where it sets an agreed standard and where an accountable executive decides. A technically sound recommendation can fail because nobody addressed the decision rights and incentives around it.

## Organising the enterprise architecture function

Enterprise architecture is also a function inside the organisation, with people, meetings and a place in the reporting lines. Van der Raadt and van Vliet describe the **enterprise architecture function** as the organisational functions, roles and bodies involved in creating, maintaining, ratifying, enforcing and observing architecture decisions. These work together at enterprise, domain, project and operational levels {REF:vanderraadt}. Designing the function is itself an architecture decision.

### Roles

Most organisations distinguish three architect roles, although the titles vary:

- **Enterprise architects** work across the whole organisation. They maintain the principles, the capability map, the target state and the roadmap, and advise on investment.
- **Domain architects** cover one area, such as data, integration, security or technology, or one business unit. They turn enterprise direction into domain standards and reference designs.
- **Solution architects** design a specific change, such as replacing an order system, within those standards, and record the project's decisions.

Helios starts small: a head of architecture acting as enterprise architect, two domain architects (one for data and integration, one for security and platforms), and solution architects who sit in the delivery teams of each business unit.

### Placement

Where the function reports shapes what it sees and what it can influence. Reporting to the CIO keeps it close to technology but can make it look like an IT specialism. Reporting to a strategy or transformation office keeps it close to investment decisions but risks losing touch with delivery. Many groups choose a **federated** arrangement: a small central team for enterprise-wide work, with domain and solution architects embedded in the business units. Helios chooses this arrangement, with the central team reporting to the CIO and attending the transformation board, because its main decisions cut across units while its delivery stays local.

### Operating rhythm and the architecture board

An **architecture board** is a cross-unit body that reviews significant designs, approves standards and exceptions within the authority delegated to it, and sends investment and risk decisions to their accountable owners (Chapter 8). The function also needs a predictable rhythm, tied to the decisions it serves. For Helios:

| Cycle | Activity | Decision it feeds |
|---|---|---|
| Fortnightly | Architecture board reviews designs that meet the review triggers | Approval of designs and time-limited exceptions |
| Monthly | Portfolio and risk update: inventory changes, overdue exceptions | Platform and security priorities |
| Quarterly | Roadmap review against delivery and benefits | Release of the next funding stage |
| Annually | Review of principles, standards and the capability map | Strategy and budget round |

### Measuring the function

Judge the function by its effect on decisions rather than by its output of documents. Useful measures include the share of significant investment decisions that used current architecture information, the time taken to review a design, the number of exceptions past their expiry date and the reuse of shared services. Survey research on enterprise architecture management supports this emphasis: intention to use the architecture and the organisational and project benefits it produced worked as measures of success, while satisfaction with it did not {REF:lange}. Chapter 14 shows how to measure such effects with care.

## Define the baseline honestly

Helios's forty-three applications are an initial inventory count, which is far from a complete model of how the group works. For each application, record its business purpose, owner, users, cost, the facts it is authoritative for, its interfaces, its criticality, its lifecycle status and how confident the team is in each fact. An application with no known owner is a question to investigate, not a retirement candidate.

Show the current state and the desired state separately. For example, if the group wants a shared customer identity but each division has its own identifiers today, draw both, together with the uncertain mapping between them. A future-state label on a current-state diagram hides the work the transformation must do.

## From slogan to mechanism

The board's phrase "reduce duplication" could mean at least four different things:

- fewer paid licences for applications that do the same job;
- fewer inconsistent copies of the same customer data;
- less repeated engineering effort across teams;
- fewer redundant steps in business processes.

Each has a different mechanism and a different measure. For example, retiring a CRM might cut subscription cost but add work for a division whose process the replacement supports poorly. A shared customer model might improve reporting without any CRM consolidation at all.

Three kinds of statement are easy to confuse. A **definition** says what a term covers. A **mechanism** explains how one change could lead to another. A **causal claim** says what would happen to an outcome if an intervention were made, compared with an alternative. Agreeing on the definition of a capability map does not show that producing one reduces operating cost. Chapter 16 develops these distinctions further.

A **benefit chain** makes the mechanism explicit, one observable step at a time:

1. A governed customer identifier makes previously ambiguous matches resolvable.
2. Resolvable matches reduce manual reconciliation.
3. Less reconciliation frees staff capacity.
4. A decision about staffing, contracts or new work turns that capacity into a financial benefit.

Each step needs an owner and something that can be observed. The existence of a master-data tool establishes none of them. Research on the value of enterprise architecture proposes similar intermediate mechanisms, such as better alignment and better information availability; Chapter 14 examines what that research can and cannot show {REF:eavalue}.

## Worked example: a mechanism and its rivals

Define the intervention narrowly: a verified customer-link service, plus a staffed correction process, for two divisions. Define the outcome as the proportion of eligible service requests that are reassigned because the customer or service site was misidentified. The unit of observation is the individual service request, even though the funding decision is made for the programme.

Start with the size of the problem. Last quarter, 410 of 5,200 eligible maintenance requests were reassigned, a rate of 7.9%. A review of 100 of those reassignments found that 46 were caused by a wrong customer or site. If that sample is representative, about 189 requests, or 3.6% of all eligible requests, were reassigned for identity reasons. Even perfect identity resolution could therefore remove at most about 3.6 percentage points of reassignment. The other 4.3 points have different causes, and the business case must not claim them.

### When the prediction fails

Work backwards from a failed prediction. Suppose verified links increase but misrouting does not fall:

- If routing does not actually consume the links, the fix is an integration change.
- If routing uses the links but selects the legal party when it needs the service site, the fix is a change of meaning in the data.
- If routing is correct but resolution is still slow, the binding constraint may be technician availability, which needs a capacity decision.

Buying a larger customer-matching platform would address none of these.

Compare a cheaper alternative too. Helios could keep its local identifiers and set up a small cross-division correction desk with an agreed response time. At low volumes it might give an adequate service and reveal how much ambiguity really exists, but its labour cost, coverage and handoff risk continue. The automated option needs implementation and stewardship, error monitoring and a fallback for unresolved matches. Choose between them on volume, consequence and observed correction effort.

Before collecting data, record the eligible workflows, the observation period, the treatment of unresolved cases and the quality checks. Keep every eligible request in the denominator, even those that cannot be matched, so that nobody can improve the figures by excluding difficult cases. Agree a decision rule in advance: expand only when the measured improvement and a sustainable support burden justify the next commitment.

## A first decision for Helios

The board asks whether to mandate one CRM across the group immediately. A defensible first response proposes a short, time-boxed analysis before any group-wide procurement. In six weeks, the team will deliver:

- an assessment of how the divisions' service processes differ, and which differences are required by contracts or customers;
- a proposal for who is authoritative for each shared customer fact;
- one feasible consolidation option and one staged alternative, each with costs.

Meanwhile, the team fixes the handoffs that cause the most misrouted requests, because those fixes are useful whatever the CRM decision. If the divisions turn out to share essentially the same process and one product supports it, consolidation may be justified. If their differences are material, common data contracts and governance may create more value first.

## Key points

- Locally sound decisions can create enterprise-level cost and risk. Enterprise architecture makes the relationships between decisions visible.
- Enterprise architecture describes how capabilities, processes, information, applications, technology and decision rights fit together, and uses that description to choose and sequence change.
- A model is valuable when it changes a decision; different audiences need different views.
- Benefits must be argued through explicit mechanisms with observable steps, and bounded by the size of the problem.
- Every benefit claim needs rival explanations and a decision rule agreed before the data arrives.
- An architecture function needs clear roles, a deliberate placement, a rhythm tied to decisions and measures of its effect on those decisions.
