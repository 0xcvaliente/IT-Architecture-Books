# 16 Research methods and academic synthesis

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Classify research designs and state what kind of claim each can support.
- Appraise a study's question, method, sample, results and limits.
- Synthesise several sources into a conditional recommendation that separates findings, inference and proposal.
- Write a critical synthesis with a source matrix and a counterargument.

## Kinds of claim

Helios's sponsors, vendors and consultants all cite research. This chapter gives you a way to judge what each source can and cannot support before it shapes a decision.

Arguments about enterprise architecture mix several kinds of claim, and each needs different support:

- A **definition** says what a term includes. For example, "an operating model is the level of process integration and standardisation an organisation needs".
- A **mechanism** explains how one change could lead to another: verified customer links could reduce misrouted requests.
- An **association** reports that two things vary together: organisations reporting stronger EA capabilities also report more benefits.
- A **causal effect** states what would happen to an outcome if an intervention were made, compared with an alternative.
- A **design proposal** recommends what an organisation should do, under stated conditions.

Agreement on a definition does not establish a mechanism, and an association does not establish a causal effect. Much confusion in architecture debates comes from moving silently from one kind of claim to the next.

## Research designs and what they support

The studies cited in this book use several designs. Each supports some kinds of claim well and others poorly.

| Design | Example in this book | Good for | Weak for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conceptual model from literature | Tamm et al., EA benefits (Chapter 14) | Proposing mechanisms to measure | Estimating effect sizes |
| Formal or simulation model | March, exploration and exploitation (Chapter 15) | Showing what follows from assumptions | Showing the assumptions hold |
| Comparative measurement | MacCormack et al., mirroring (Chapter 7) | Testing a relationship across matched cases | Generalising beyond the sample |
| Survey | Lange et al.; van de Wetering et al. (Chapter 14) | Associations among measured constructs | Causal direction; behaviour as opposed to perception |
| Qualitative case study | Jöhnk et al. (Chapter 8); Dang and Pekkola (Chapter 12); Kotusev (Chapter 2) | How and why processes unfold in context | Frequency and typicality |
| Quasi-experiment | Difference-in-differences (Chapter 14) | Estimating effects under stated assumptions | Settings where the assumptions fail |
| Standard or method | TOGAF, ArchiMate (Chapter 2) | Shared definitions and procedures | Showing that the procedure works |

No design is best in general. A larger sample does not automatically defeat a smaller study if the two answer different questions.

## Appraising a source

Read each source as an argument. A practical appraisal follows these steps:

1. **Question.** What exactly does the study ask?
2. **Constructs.** How are its key ideas defined and measured?
3. **Method.** Which design does it use, and why?
4. **Sample and selection.** Who or what was studied, and how were they chosen?
5. **Results.** What did it find, in its own terms?
6. **Limits.** What do the authors say limits the findings, and what else might?
7. **Transfer.** What would need to be true for the findings to apply to your case?

### Worked appraisal

Apply the steps to the survey by van de Wetering and colleagues {REF:dynamic}.

- **Question:** do EA-driven dynamic capabilities, innovativeness and organisational structure affect organisational benefits?
- **Constructs:** each was measured with questionnaire items, rated by respondents.
- **Method:** partial least squares structural equation modelling, plus fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis to find combinations of conditions associated with high benefits.
- **Sample and selection:** data from 299 CIOs and IT managers. Check in the paper how they were reached, because the recruitment route decides whom the results can describe.
- **Results:** EA-driven dynamic capabilities were associated with benefits, partly through innovativeness.
- **Limits:** the authors note self-reported measures and a single point in time. Nothing in the design makes the respondents a random sample of organisations.
- **Transfer:** for Helios, the study suggests that the capability to sense, mobilise and transform matters more than owning an architecture repository. It cannot say how much benefit Helios would gain, or whether the capabilities cause the benefits or the reverse.

The appraisal does not reject the study. It records exactly what the study can support, which is what a synthesis needs.

## Independence of evidence

Count independent sources of evidence rather than citations. For example, a survey analysed in two ways still rests on the same respondents and measures. Two papers discussing the same case share their evidence. A practitioner guide that cites a study adds no new data to it. Record where each source's data came from, so that a long reference list does not overstate the support for a conclusion.

## A synthesis structure

Separate three layers in every synthesis:

1. **Source findings:** what each inspected source argues or observes, within its own scope.
2. **Inference:** your argument about how those findings may apply to the case.
3. **Proposal and test:** the conditional commitment you recommend, and the observation that would show whether it holds.

Take the question: "Under what conditions could shared customer information improve Helios's service outcomes?" The operating-model perspective (Chapter 4) asks what the business must share. The EA benefits model (Chapter 14) proposes intermediate mechanisms. The mirroring study (Chapter 7) suggests examining technical and organisational boundaries together. March's model (Chapter 15) highlights the risk of standardising too early. These perspectives complement each other, but they are not four independent proofs that one investment will succeed.

A synthesis might conclude: Helios should first share customer identity while keeping local fulfilment, because process equivalence is unproven; it should fund stewardship, because shared meaning otherwise has no owner; and it should use a bounded pilot, because adoption uncertainty could change the expansion decision. Each clause rests on a different kind of support, and each can be challenged.

### Rival explanations

Write at least one rival explanation for your preferred design. If you recommend federated coordination because divisions need different processes, test whether the differences reflect legal or customer obligations, temporary workarounds or habit. If you recommend a shared platform to reduce duplicated engineering, check whether its contract and funding actually let teams stop doing that work.

Where sources seem to disagree, first compare their units, constructs, outcomes and time periods. A shared platform could make routine deployments more consistent while slowing unusual local changes; both findings can be true. State the trade-off and the circumstances in which it matters.

> **Check your understanding.** Take one paragraph of your own writing that cites a source. Mark each sentence as source finding, inference or proposal. If a citation sits at the end of a sentence that is really your inference, rewrite the sentence.

## The research assignment

Write a critical synthesis of 1,500 to 2,000 words on a question about the Helios programme. Use at least four relevant sources, including two research papers that you have read in full. Include:

- a source matrix with columns for question, design, sample, key finding, limits and relevance to Helios;
- a recommendation that separates source findings, inference and proposal;
- one counterargument to your recommendation, stated as strongly as you can;
- IEEE-style references numbered in order of first citation.

Use official documentation for claims about products and standards, and research for claims about effects. If a source you want is unavailable, choose an accessible primary source that addresses the same question. Assessment rewards traceable reasoning and honest limits, not the number of citations.

## Key points

- Distinguish definitions, mechanisms, associations, causal effects and design proposals; each needs different support.
- Every research design has strengths and blind spots. Match the design to the claim.
- Appraise sources systematically, including how the sample was selected and what would need to be true for the findings to transfer.
- Count independent sources of evidence rather than citations.
- A good synthesis separates source findings, inference and proposal, and states a rival explanation and a test.
