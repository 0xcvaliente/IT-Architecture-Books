# 2 Enterprise architecture frameworks, methods and modelling

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Distinguish a taxonomy, a method, a modelling language and a practice, and explain what each contributes.
- Classify enterprise artefacts with the Zachman Framework 3.0 and use the result to find gaps.
- Tailor the TOGAF Architecture Development Method to a specific decision and write architecture principles in a standard format.
- Model a service view in ArchiMate 4 using its domains, aspects and relationships, and explain what changed from ArchiMate 3.2.
- Evaluate framework claims against empirical studies of how enterprise architecture is practised.

## Four kinds of help

In one week the Helios architect receives three requests. The board wants options for the customer platform. The data steward asks what "customer" should mean across the group. A delivery team wants to know which systems depend on the workforce identity service. Each request needs different content, a different level of detail and a different audience. Frameworks help the architect decide what to describe, in what order to work and how to draw it.

The word "framework" covers several different kinds of help, and confusing them causes trouble:

- A **taxonomy** classifies the things an enterprise description could contain. The Zachman Framework is the classic example.
- A **method** sets out the steps for producing and using architecture. The TOGAF Architecture Development Method is the most widely taught.
- A **modelling language** defines element types, relationships and notation so that models mean the same thing to every reader. ArchiMate is the open standard for enterprise models.
- A **practice** is what architects in a real organisation actually do: which artefacts they keep, which meetings they attend and which decisions they influence.

An international standard ties these together. ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010 separates an **architecture**, which is abstract, from an **architecture description**, the tangible work product that expresses it {REF:iso42010}. Stakeholders have **concerns**; a **viewpoint** sets the conventions for addressing particular concerns, and it governs one or more **views**, each of which applies those conventions to a particular system or enterprise. For example, a "board investment" viewpoint might specify a table of options, costs and risks; the Helios customer-platform options paper is a view built to that viewpoint.

| Kind | Question it answers | Example | What it leaves to you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taxonomy | What could we describe? | Zachman Framework | Order of work and depth |
| Method | In what order do we work? | TOGAF ADM | Which steps matter here |
| Modelling language | How do we express it precisely? | ArchiMate | Which views to draw |
| Practice | What do architects actually do? | Empirical studies | Fit to your organisation |

## The Zachman Framework: a classification scheme

John Zachman's 1987 paper started from an analogy with building and manufacturing {REF:zachman}. A building is described differently for the owner, the architect and the builder, and each description is complete for its purpose. Zachman argued that information systems need the same family of descriptions. His original grid had three columns, for data (what), function (how) and network (where), and he noted that who, when and why descriptions were also needed. Sowa and Zachman later extended the scheme to all six questions, with rows for the planner, owner, designer, builder and subcontractor {REF:sowa}. Older texts still use those row names.

The current version, 3.0, appeared in 2011 under the subtitle "The Enterprise Ontology" {REF:zachman3}. Zachman presents it as an **ontology**: a structured classification of the descriptions an enterprise can have, which deliberately says nothing about how architecture work should be done. Its grid has six columns and six rows, 36 cells in all:

- The **columns** are the six interrogatives, each named for the kind of description it holds: what (inventory), how (process), where (distribution), who (responsibility), when (timing) and why (motivation).
- The **rows** are audience perspectives, from the most abstract to the most concrete: the executive's scope contexts, the business owner's business concepts, the architect's system logic, the engineer's technology physics, the technician's tool components and, in the bottom row, the operating enterprise itself.

Each cell is a distinct kind of description. For example, the cell where the why column meets the business-concepts row holds the business's goals and rules, while the cell where the what column meets the system-logic row holds a logical data model.

### Worked example: classifying Helios artefacts

The Helios architecture team lists six documents it already holds and classifies each one.

| Helios artefact | Column | Row |
|---|---|---|
| List of business units and service regions | Where (distribution) | Scope contexts (executive) |
| Definition of "customer organisation" | What (inventory) | Business concepts (business owner) |
| Service-request handling process | How (process) | Business concepts (business owner) |
| Customer-link data model | What (inventory) | System logic (architect) |
| Integration schedule for nightly extracts | When (timing) | Technology physics (engineer) |
| Database table definitions for CRM North | What (inventory) | Tool components (technician) |

The table shows a pattern. Helios has several what and how descriptions but nothing in the why (motivation) column. No document records the goals or rules that justify consolidating customer data, so every debate about the CRM restarts from opinion. That single gap is the useful output. Completing all 36 cells for an enterprise of Helios's size would cost far more than it returns, and the bottom row is the running enterprise rather than a model of it.

Zachman is therefore best used as a checklist. Ask which cells a decision depends on, check that those cells are described at the right level, and stop there.

## TOGAF and the Architecture Development Method

The TOGAF Standard, published by The Open Group, is a framework for developing and governing enterprise architecture {REF:togaf}. Its 10th Edition separates a stable core, the **fundamental content**, from a library of **series guides** on particular topics. At its centre is the **Architecture Development Method (ADM)**, a cycle of phases for developing and changing an architecture {REF:togafadm}. {FIG:adm_cycle} shows the cycle.

FIGURE adm_cycle | The TOGAF Architecture Development Method: a preliminary phase, eight phases in a cycle, and requirements management at the centre.

The phases are:

1. **Preliminary.** Establish the architecture capability: scope, principles, governance and how the method will be tailored.
2. **A. Architecture vision.** Agree the scope of one cycle, the stakeholders, the business goals and a high-level target.
3. **B. Business architecture.** Describe the baseline and target business: capabilities, processes, organisation and information needs.
4. **C. Information systems architectures.** Describe the data and application architectures that support the business.
5. **D. Technology architecture.** Describe the technology services and platforms the applications need.
6. **E. Opportunities and solutions.** Group the changes into work packages and identify transition architectures.
7. **F. Migration planning.** Sequence the work into a costed, prioritised roadmap.
8. **G. Implementation governance.** Check that delivery projects conform to the architecture.
9. **H. Architecture change management.** Monitor changes in the business and technology, and decide when a new cycle is needed.

**Requirements management** sits at the centre because requirements change throughout the cycle. Each phase reads them and adds to them.

### Tailoring the ADM

The ADM looks sequential on paper, but TOGAF itself expects organisations to adapt it: to iterate within and between phases, to vary the depth of each phase, and to fit the method to their own governance {REF:togafadm}. A literal pass through every phase for every decision would be slow and would produce many unused documents.

Tailoring starts from the decision the cycle must support. For Helios's first decision, whether and how to share customer identity across two divisions, a ten-week tailored cycle might look like this:

| Phase and depth | Helios deliverable | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Preliminary: light, 1 week | Principles, decision rights for customer data | Reuse existing governance where it works |
| A: full, 2 weeks | Scope: two divisions; named sponsor and owners | Scope disputes are the main risk |
| B and C: full, 4 weeks | Customer facts, owners, matching process, affected applications | This is where the uncertainty lies |
| D: light, 1 week | Confirm hosting on the existing platform | No new technology is proposed |
| E and F: full, 2 weeks | Options, transition states, pilot plan and costs | The board decides on this output |
| G and H: set up, then ongoing | Pilot governance and review triggers | Needed once the pilot starts |

The tailored cycle spends most of its time where Helios is uncertain and moves quickly where existing decisions still hold. It also combines phases B and C, because the customer question is both a business question and a data question.

### Architecture principles

The Preliminary phase usually produces a short set of **architecture principles**. TOGAF recommends a four-part format for each one {REF:togafprinciples}:

- **Name:** a short, memorable label.
- **Statement:** the rule, stated plainly.
- **Rationale:** the business benefit of following the rule.
- **Implications:** what the organisation must do or accept as a result, including costs.

Here are two Helios principles in that format.

| Name | Statement | Rationale | Implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| One authority for each shared fact | Every business fact shared between units has one named authoritative source and owner. | Conflicting copies cause most of Helios's reconciliation work. | New integrations must name their source; owners need funded time; copies are read-only. |
| Share data before processes | Units share customer data and identifiers first; common processes follow only where analysis shows they fit. | Unit processes differ for contractual reasons that have not yet been analysed. | The CRM decision waits for process analysis; the identity work starts now. |

A good principle can be broken, so it guides real choices. "We value high-quality data" cannot be broken and so guides nothing. The first principle, by contrast, rules out a proposal in which two CRMs both edit the customer's legal name.

TOGAF warns that too many principles reduce the flexibility of the architecture, and notes that many organisations prefer a small set of high-level principles, between 10 and 20 {REF:togafprinciples}. It describes a good set as understandable, robust, complete, consistent and stable. Consistency deserves attention: strict adherence to one principle may require a looser reading of another, so the set should say which wins. Give each principle an owner, and review the set when strategy changes.

## ArchiMate: a modelling language

ArchiMate is The Open Group's modelling language for enterprise architecture. The current version, the ArchiMate 4 Specification, was published in April 2026 {REF:archimate4}. It gives each kind of element a defined meaning and a symbol, and it restricts which relationships are allowed between them. Two people reading the same ArchiMate model should draw the same conclusions, which is rarely true of informal box-and-line diagrams.

### Domains and aspects

ArchiMate 4 organises its elements into **domains**:

- The **common domain** holds generic elements used everywhere: the behaviour elements service, process, function and event; the active structure elements role, collaboration and path; and the grouping and location elements.
- The **business**, **application** and **technology** domains, together called the core domains, hold the elements specific to each. Examples are business actors and business objects; application components and data objects; and nodes, devices, system software, artifacts and physical elements such as equipment and facilities.
- The **strategy** domain models capabilities, value streams, resources and courses of action.
- The **motivation** domain models stakeholders, drivers, assessments, goals, outcomes, principles, requirements, meaning and value. It explains why the architecture is the way it is.
- The **implementation and migration** domain models work packages, deliverables and **plateaus**, which are relatively stable states of the architecture, such as a baseline, a transition state and a target.

Elements also belong to one of three **aspects**. **Active structure** elements perform behaviour, such as a role or an application component. **Behaviour** elements are the work done, such as a process or a service. **Passive structure** elements are acted upon, such as a business object or a data object.

The most visible design choice in version 4 is that behaviour is no longer split by domain. There is one process, one function, one service and one event, and the same process can be performed by people, by software or by both together {REF:archimate4,archimate4changes}. A modeller no longer has to decide early whether a step is manual or automated, which suits work in which people and machines collaborate. Roles are generic in the same way: any internal active structure element, including an application component, can be assigned to a role.

Relationships come in groups. **Structural** relationships (composition, aggregation, assignment and realisation) say what an element is made of or what it makes real. **Dependency** relationships (serving, access, influence and association) say how elements use each other. **Dynamic** relationships (triggering and flow) show order and transfer between behaviours. Specialisation completes the set. Relationships can now also carry a **multiplicity**, which limits how many instances may sit at each end; for example, a rule that every service is realised by at least one process.

### Worked example: a Helios service view

{FIG:archimate_layers} models how Helios handles a maintenance service request today.

FIGURE archimate_layers | A Helios service-fulfilment view drawn in ArchiMate 4, arranged by business, application and technology domain. Every relationship is labelled and drawn in ArchiMate line style; element symbols are simplified to boxes.

Read the view from the top. A service coordinator role is **assigned to** the process "Handle service request", which **realises** the service "Field maintenance" that **serves** the customer, a business actor. Two services from the application domain serve that process: case management, realised by CRM North, and customer lookup, realised by the customer link hub. Both components are served by a container hosting service, realised by the shared cloud platform. In ArchiMate 4 all four services are the same kind of element; the view places each in the domain of the elements that realise it.

The view answers a precise question: what does the maintenance process depend on? If the customer link hub fails, the coordinator loses customer lookup but keeps case management. If the container hosting service fails, both application services are lost at once, a shared dependency that Chapter 9 returns to. In the terms of ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010, this is a view built to a layered viewpoint suited to an operations audience; the specification's appendix offers example viewpoints for other purposes, including capability maps, value streams and migration. The board would need a different view of the same model, for example one showing capabilities and work packages.

### What changed from ArchiMate 3.2

Many organisations hold repositories drawn in ArchiMate 3.2 {REF:archimate}, so architects will meet both versions for some years. The main differences are these:

- **Layers became domains.** The framework is now drawn as concentric hexagons rather than a matrix of layers and aspects. Lankhorst explains that "layer" was often misread as a level of abstraction or a strict hierarchy {REF:archimate4changes}.
- **Behaviour is merged.** The separate business, application and technology processes, functions, services and events of 3.2 are now one generic set in the common domain, and the implementation event is replaced by the generic event.
- **Roles and collaborations are generic.** The business role is replaced by a single role element, and the business, application and technology collaborations by a single collaboration.
- **Some elements were removed.** The three interaction elements, constraint, contract, gap and representation no longer exist. Suggested replacements include a process with several participants for an interaction, a requirement for a constraint, a business object for a contract, and an assessment between two plateaus for a gap {REF:archimate4changes}.
- **Relationships can have multiplicity.**
- **The language is smaller.** Lankhorst counts 42 concepts in version 4 against 60 in version 3.2, a reduction of about 30%.

The aspects, the relationship groups (including composition, which version 4 keeps as a structural relationship) and most structure elements, such as business actors, application components, nodes and data objects, carry over unchanged. Converting a 3.2 model is therefore mainly a matter of retyping behaviour elements and replacing the removed ones; for example, a 3.2 "business process" and an "application process" both become a process in version 4.

## Capability-based planning

Many organisations plan change by project or by application. **Capability-based planning** plans and funds change by business capability instead, and it treats projects as the means of delivering capability increments {REF:togafcbp}. For example, Helios's capability "Customer identity resolution" could improve in three increments:

1. Verified links between customer records in two divisions.
2. Verified links across all three divisions, with a correction service.
3. Customer self-service correction of contact and site details.

Each increment needs work from several projects: data cleansing, an integration change, training and new support roles. Funding the increment rather than the projects keeps attention on the business ability the board is paying for. It also connects the strategy domain to the implementation and migration domain: each increment corresponds to a plateau in the roadmap. Chapter 3 builds the capability map on which such planning depends.

## How enterprise architecture is practised

Frameworks describe how enterprise architecture could be done. Empirical research asks how it is done. Kotusev has studied this question through case studies and interviews with practitioners. In an in-depth case study of one organisation whose practice was described as TOGAF-based, he concluded that none of the TOGAF-specific recommendations proved useful, and that the practice hardly resembled any established EA methodology {REF:kotusevtogaf}. In an analysis of the artefacts used in 27 diverse organisations, he identified 24 common artefacts that proved useful in practice and described their usage and purpose. He argued that the familiar picture of enterprise architecture as layered current-state and future-state descriptions linked by a roadmap does not match how practitioners work {REF:kotusevartifacts}.

These studies are qualitative and cover a limited number of organisations, so they show what happened in those cases rather than what must happen everywhere. They still support three practical conclusions for Helios:

- Treat frameworks as libraries of ideas to select from.
- Judge architecture work by the decisions it changes rather than by the number of artefacts produced.
- Keep the artefacts that people actually consult, and keep them current.

> **Common mistake.** Announcing that an organisation "uses TOGAF" and measuring progress by deliverables completed. Progress is better measured by whether investment, design and governance decisions now use architecture information.

## Choosing and combining frameworks

Most organisations combine elements from several sources. Helios might choose:

- Zachman as a periodic checklist for gaps in its descriptions;
- a tailored ADM cycle for each major decision, using the phase names as a shared vocabulary;
- ArchiMate for the repository, with a few agreed viewpoints for the board, data owners and operations;
- the C4 model for software views inside delivery teams, where a lighter notation suits developers {REF:c4}.

The combination works if each element has a stated purpose and an owner. It fails if the organisation adopts all of them in full and then has no time left to make decisions.

## Key points

- Frameworks provide four different kinds of help: a taxonomy of what to describe, a method for the order of work, a modelling language for precise expression and, through research, evidence of what practice looks like.
- Zachman's grid of six perspectives and six interrogatives is an ontology of descriptions. Use it to find the gaps that matter for a decision.
- The TOGAF ADM is a cycle of phases around requirements management. Tailor its depth to the decision, and write principles with a name, statement, rationale and implications.
- ArchiMate 4 models the enterprise in domains and aspects with defined relationships, and uses one set of behaviour elements across the business, application and technology domains. Viewpoints select what each audience needs to see.
- Empirical studies show that practitioners adapt frameworks heavily. Architecture is judged by the decisions it improves.
