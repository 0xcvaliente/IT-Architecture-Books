# 11 Roadmaps, dependencies and transition architectures

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Design intermediate states that keep the business operating during a transformation.
- Calculate earliest finish times, slack and the critical path, and explain how shared resources change them.
- Allocate a programme envelope to work packages that respect both package totals and annual limits.
- Revise a roadmap after a funding cut, stating clearly what changes and what is lost.

## A roadmap is a sequence of operable states

A **target architecture** describes a desired arrangement. A **roadmap** explains how the organisation reaches it through intermediate states while continuing to operate. TOGAF calls these intermediate states **transition architectures** and develops them in phases E and F of the ADM {REF:togafadm}; ArchiMate models them as plateaus (Chapter 2). Every transition creates temporary ownership, data movement, support and retirement work.

"Replace the CRM in year two" is therefore incomplete. A usable roadmap entry says what migrates, which integrations change, how existing customers continue to be served during the change, and what has to be true before the old system can be switched off.

Begin with outcomes and constraints, then identify work packages and dependencies. Dependencies come in several kinds:

- **Technical:** the integration needs the shared identifier service.
- **Informational:** the migration needs an agreed meaning of "customer organisation".
- **Contractual:** the export needs a right to the supplier's data format.
- **Organisational:** the rollout needs trained stewards.
- **Regulatory:** the data transfer needs an approved legal basis.

Buying the target platform satisfies only one link in such a chain.

## The Helios funding envelope

The following allocation is the starting scenario for the €9 million programme envelope. It is separate from the €12 million annual operating baseline. Some programme work will later change operating costs, but no saving is assumed merely because money has been allocated.

| Work package | Three-year allocation | Intended outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Customer identity and data foundations | €1.0m | Governed cross-system customer identifiers, matching and stewardship |
| Workforce identity and access | €0.5m | Staff accounts created and removed through the group identity service |
| Integration capability | €1.2m | Reusable contracts, monitoring and recovery support |
| Legacy modernisation | €2.4m | Safe transition away from selected unsupported systems |
| Analytics improvement | €0.9m | Reproducible, governed reporting for agreed decisions |
| CRM and process pilot | €1.2m | Evidence about shared customer processes before wider consolidation |
| Adoption and assurance | €0.9m | Training, service ownership, testing and transition assurance |
| Contingency | €0.9m | Controlled response to identified uncertainty |

The packages sum to €9.0 million, and the contingency sits inside that total. It is released only through a documented decision. The two identity packages are deliberately separate. Customer identity resolution links records about customer organisations across systems; workforce identity and access controls how staff sign in and lose access when they leave. They have different owners, benefits and risks, and Chapter 10 charges only the first to the customer-handoff business case.

The profile follows the dependencies rather than spreading money evenly. ## The critical path

The **critical path method** schedules a project as a network of activities and finds the longest chain of dependent activities, which determines the earliest completion date {REF:cpm}. Activities on that chain have no **slack**: any delay to them delays the whole project.

For example, consider the customer identity pilot, with durations in weeks and finish-to-start dependencies:

- A: agree identity semantics, 4 weeks.
- B: implement shared identifiers, 6 weeks, after A.
- C: define integration contracts, 3 weeks, after A.
- D: pilot a customer handoff, 4 weeks, after both B and C.
- E: assess results and decide on rollout, 2 weeks, after D.

FIGURE roadmap | Dependency network for the customer identity pilot. The highlighted longest path, A–B–D–E, sets the earliest completion at 16 weeks.

A **forward pass** gives each activity's earliest finish: A at week 4, B at 10, C at 7, D at 14 (it must wait for B) and E at 16. A **backward pass** from week 16 gives the latest finish that does not delay completion: E at 16, D at 14, B at 10, C at 10 and A at 4. Slack is the difference. C can finish as late as week 10, so it has three weeks of slack; every other activity has none. The critical path is A–B–D–E.

### A listing for the two passes

The following program performs both passes. It assumes that the tasks are listed so that every predecessor appears before the tasks that depend on it.

~~~
"""Critical-path schedule for the Helios customer identity pilot (weeks)."""

TASKS = {  # name: (duration, predecessors), listed in dependency order
    'A': (4, []),          # agree identity semantics
    'B': (6, ['A']),       # implement shared identifiers
    'C': (3, ['A']),       # define integration contracts
    'D': (4, ['B', 'C']),  # pilot a customer handoff
    'E': (2, ['D']),       # assess results and decide rollout
}

def schedule(tasks: dict[str, tuple[int, list[str]]]) -> tuple[dict, dict, int]:
    early: dict[str, int] = {}                 # earliest finish (forward pass)
    for name, (dur, preds) in tasks.items():
        early[name] = max((early[p] for p in preds), default=0) + dur
    end = max(early.values())
    late: dict[str, int] = {}                  # latest finish (backward pass)
    for name in reversed(list(tasks)):
        succs = [s for s, (_, preds) in tasks.items() if name in preds]
        late[name] = min((late[s] - tasks[s][0] for s in succs), default=end)
    return early, late, end

early, late, end = schedule(TASKS)
for name, (dur, _) in TASKS.items():
    start, slack = early[name] - dur, late[name] - early[name]
    print(f'{name}: start {start:>2}  finish {early[name]:>2}  slack {slack}')
critical = [n for n in TASKS if late[n] == early[n]]
print('Completion:', end, 'weeks; critical path:', '-'.join(critical))

shared = dict(TASKS, C=(3, ['B']))             # one team must do B, then C
print('With one shared team for B and C:', schedule(shared)[2], 'weeks')
~~~

Running it prints:

~~~
A: start  0  finish  4  slack 0
B: start  4  finish 10  slack 0
C: start  4  finish  7  slack 3
D: start 10  finish 14  slack 0
E: start 14  finish 16  slack 0
Completion: 16 weeks; critical path: A-B-D-E
With one shared team for B and C: 19 weeks
~~~

The last line shows why a precedence schedule is a lower bound rather than a promise. If one specialist team must build the identifiers and then define the contracts, C cannot start until week 10. D then starts at week 13 and the pilot completes at week 19. C's three weeks of slack existed only because the calculation assumed unlimited people. Real roadmaps must check resource constraints, and the people most often shared are the scarce experts in identity, data and integration.

## Design each intermediate operating state

For the move from separate identifiers to governed group identifiers, Helios defines three states:

| | State 1: today | State 2: pilot | State 3: adopted |
|---|---|---|---|
| Authority for customer facts | Each division's CRM | Each division's CRM | Party register for legal facts; CRMs for local facts |
| Cross-division links | Spreadsheets, informal | Link hub publishes verified links | Link hub, with correction service |
| Who may write links | Nobody formally | Party stewards | Party stewards and the correction service |
| Exit condition | Pilot approved | Pilot results meet the agreed thresholds | Spreadsheets retired, all consumers migrated |

For each state, also name the allowed writers for every fact, the supported integrations, the access controls, the reconciliation rules and the operational owner. A temporary bridge needs a retirement condition and funded removal work; otherwise the temporary state quietly becomes the permanent architecture.

Retire systems on evidence rather than on a calendar date. Before switching something off, show that consumers have migrated, retained records remain accessible under policy, interfaces are no longer called and the contract can be ended. For example, a system can have no interactive users and still supply a critical monthly extract.

## Respond to a 20% funding cut

Change card A reduces the envelope to €7.2 million, a cut of €1.8 million. Cutting every package by 20% would be easy arithmetic and poor architecture. Some packages contain work that cannot be divided, such as a security control or a dependency that other packages need. Cutting assurance while keeping every delivery promise increases risk without reducing scope.

One defensible scenario defers the €0.9 million analytics improvement and €0.9 million of legacy modernisation that has no fixed hosting deadline, provided that continued operation of those systems is acceptable. Legacy Orders, which does have a deadline, stays in scope.

The cut must also be applied to the annual limits, which still add up to €9.0 million. The deferred work was planned for years 2 and 3, so the revised schedule, in € millions, looks like this:

| Work package | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Revised total | Initial total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer identity and data foundations | 0.6 | 0.4 | 0.0 | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| Workforce identity and access | 0.4 | 0.1 | 0.0 | 0.5 | 0.5 |
| Integration capability | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.2 | 1.2 | 1.2 |
| Legacy modernisation | 0.6 | 0.6 | 0.3 | 1.5 | 2.4 |
| Analytics improvement | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.9 |
| CRM and process pilot | 0.3 | 0.6 | 0.3 | 1.2 | 1.2 |
| Adoption and assurance | 0.3 | 0.3 | 0.3 | 0.9 | 0.9 |
| Contingency, held in reserve | 0.3 | 0.3 | 0.3 | 0.9 | 0.9 |
| **Annual total** | **3.0** | **2.8** | **1.4** | **7.2** | **9.0** |

The architect should therefore ask the board to revise the annual limits to €3.0 million, €2.8 million and €1.4 million. A proportional cut of 20% to every year (€2.4 million, €2.8 million and €2.0 million) looks fairer but is infeasible for this plan: year 1 holds the identity foundations and the Legacy Orders deadline, and needs its full €3.0 million. If the board insists on proportional limits, €0.6 million of year-1 work must move later, which in practice means starting the CRM pilot a year late or running year 1 without contingency. Exercise 17.1 works through that choice.

The revision keeps the contingency, the pilot, the foundations and the assurance work. It also keeps the €2.5 million customer-handoff investment from Chapter 10 intact, so that investment's NPV is unchanged. The cost is real: group reporting stays inconsistent for longer, and some legacy systems keep running. If a deferred legacy system turns out to be unsafe to keep, the board needs another scope reduction or an explicit funding decision.

A reduced programme must make fewer promises or change how it keeps them. Recalculate benefits, contracts, annual cash needs and dependencies, and record the rejected alternatives with the reasons for choosing the revised sequence.

## Key points

- A roadmap is a sequence of operable intermediate states, each with named authorities, owners and exit conditions.
- The critical path sets the earliest completion; slack shows where delay is harmless. Shared scarce resources can remove slack and lengthen the schedule.
- A programme allocation must satisfy work-package totals and annual limits at the same time, with contingency inside the envelope.
- Funding cuts should remove scope deliberately, protect the dependencies that other work needs, and state openly which promises change.
