# 14 Measuring enterprise outcomes and architecture value

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Design a measurement contract that links an architectural change to a business outcome.
- Recognise Simpson's paradox and regression to the mean in enterprise data, and calculate a standardised rate.
- Calculate a difference-in-differences estimate and state the assumptions needed to read it causally.
- Evaluate research on the value of enterprise architecture and apply it to a disputed benefit claim.

## Measure the consequence the programme promised

Helios's board will ask what the programme has achieved, and this chapter shows how to answer with evidence that survives challenge. Architecture activity is easier to count than enterprise value. Diagrams drawn, standards published, review meetings held and applications migrated describe work done; better service, lower cost and reduced risk have to be measured separately. Keep activity measures for managing the work, and connect the programme to the outcomes stakeholders care about.

For Helios, a useful chain is: verified customer links enable accurate routing; accurate routing reduces reassignment; less reassignment shortens the time to resolve a service request. Each link can fail. More reliable links will change little if technician availability is the real constraint.

Write a **measurement contract** for each important measure:

- name, purpose and owner;
- unit of observation and population;
- numerator, denominator and exclusions;
- when and where the observation is taken, and over what time window;
- known limitations and the version of the definition.

If the denominator changes, a trend may reflect reclassification rather than improvement. Pair measures to guard against distortion: faster case handling with rework, lower cloud cost with demand and reliability, retirement counts with remaining interfaces. Campbell warned that the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is exposed to pressures that distort both the indicator and the process it is meant to monitor {REF:campbell}. Each pair follows the mechanism by which a misleading success could appear.

## Simpson's paradox

Aggregated rates can point the opposite way from every group within them. Simpson showed in 1951 that conclusions drawn from a combined table can differ from those drawn from its parts {REF:simpson}; the effect is now called **Simpson's paradox**.

For example, before a change Unit A handles 100 requests with 10 failures and Unit B handles 900 with 45 failures. Their failure rates are 10% and 5%, and the pooled rate is 55 / 1,000 = 5.5%. After the change, A handles 900 requests with 72 failures and B handles 100 with 4. Both units improve: A falls to 8% and B to 4%. Yet the pooled rate rises to 76 / 1,000 = 7.6%, because the workload has shifted towards the unit with the higher failure rate.

A **standardised rate** answers a like-for-like question: what would the later unit rates imply under the earlier workload mix of 10% A and 90% B? The answer is 0.1 × 8% + 0.9 × 4% = 4.4%. The following program reproduces all of these figures, using exact fractions so that rounding cannot creep in, and also computes the difference-in-differences used later in the chapter.

~~~
"""Pooled, stratified and standardised error rates, and a difference-in-differences."""
from fractions import Fraction

BEFORE = {'A': (10, 100), 'B': (45, 900)}      # unit: (errors, requests)
AFTER = {'A': (72, 900), 'B': (4, 100)}

def pct(x: Fraction) -> str:
    return f'{float(x) * 100:.1f}%'

def rates(data: dict[str, tuple[int, int]]) -> dict[str, Fraction]:
    return {unit: Fraction(errors, n) for unit, (errors, n) in data.items()}

def pooled(data: dict[str, tuple[int, int]]) -> Fraction:
    return Fraction(sum(e for e, _ in data.values()), sum(n for _, n in data.values()))

for label, data in [('Before', BEFORE), ('After', AFTER)]:
    by_unit = ', '.join(f'{unit} {pct(r)}' for unit, r in rates(data).items())
    print(f'{label}: {by_unit}; pooled {pct(pooled(data))}')

total = sum(n for _, n in BEFORE.values())
mix = {unit: Fraction(n, total) for unit, (_, n) in BEFORE.items()}  # earlier mix
standardised = sum(mix[unit] * r for unit, r in rates(AFTER).items())
print('After, standardised to the earlier mix:', pct(standardised))

pilot, comparison = (10, 6), (8, 7)            # error rate (%) before and after
change_p, change_c = pilot[1] - pilot[0], comparison[1] - comparison[0]
print(f'Pilot {change_p} pp, comparison {change_c} pp, '
      f'difference-in-differences {change_p - change_c} pp')
~~~

Running it prints:

~~~
Before: A 10.0%, B 5.0%; pooled 5.5%
After: A 8.0%, B 4.0%; pooled 7.6%
After, standardised to the earlier mix: 4.4%
Pilot -4 pp, comparison -1 pp, difference-in-differences -3 pp
~~~

Report both figures. The pooled 7.6% is what the later population actually experienced. The standardised 4.4% isolates the change in unit performance from the change in mix. Neither explains why demand moved between units, or whether the architecture caused the unit improvements.

## Regression to the mean

**Regression to the mean** occurs when a group is selected because of an extreme measurement: its next measurement tends to be closer to its long-run average, even if nothing changes {REF:rtm}. It can make natural variation look like real change.

For example, Maintenance's reassignment rate has averaged 6.8% over the past eight quarters, ranging from 5.9% to 7.9%. Helios starts its pilot in Maintenance immediately after the worst of those quarters, the 7.9% quarter described in Chapter 1, because that is where the pain is most visible. The next quarter shows 6.6%. The apparent improvement is 1.3 percentage points, but some return towards the 6.8% average was expected anyway. If the 7.9% quarter were pure bad luck, full regression to the average would explain up to 1.1 of those points, so 1.1 is an upper bound. In practice regression is usually partial: part of a bad quarter reflects lasting causes, such as a hard case mix, that persist into the next quarter. The expected next value lies between the extreme and the average, closer to the average the less the quarterly rate is correlated from one quarter to the next. Choosing pilots because of a recent bad result is natural, so guard against this effect: use several quarters of baseline data, compare with units that were not selected, and be cautious about the first measurement after selection.

## Attribution needs a credible comparison

A before-and-after improvement can coincide with staffing changes, seasonality, a different mix of customers or altered reporting. Record concurrent changes and preserve the baseline. Where possible, compare similar workflows or roll out in stages, and explain how the groups differ. Randomised rollout is often impractical; without it, causal confidence is lower, but the comparison still teaches something.

### Difference-in-differences

In the participating division, reconciliation errors fall from 10% to 6% during the pilot. In a comparison division, they fall from 8% to 7% over the same period. The participating division changes by −4 percentage points and the comparison by −1, so the **difference-in-differences** is −3 percentage points. Subtracting the comparison's change removes influences that affected both divisions equally.

{FIG:outcome_comparison} shows the logic. The dashed line is the path the participating division would have followed without the pilot, if it had moved in parallel with the comparison division.

FIGURE outcome_comparison | Difference-in-differences: the dashed line shows the participating division's assumed path without the pilot, parallel to the comparison division.

That assumption, called **parallel trends**, is the crux. The estimate is causal only if, without the pilot, the gap between the two divisions would have stayed constant {REF:did}. Several things can break it:

- the participating division adds experienced staff at the same time;
- the comparison division also starts using the new customer-matching service;
- the pilot division was chosen after an unusually bad period, so regression to the mean inflates its improvement;
- the definition of an error changes in one division but not the other.

Check prior trends where data exists, but remember that similar past trends make the assumption more plausible without proving it.

The example also omits sample sizes. Ten errors in 100 cases and 1,000 errors in 10,000 cases are both 10%, but they carry very different amounts of information. Repeated records from the same customer or operator may not be independent. Before attaching an uncertainty estimate, define the unit of observation and how observations depend on each other.

If no credible comparison exists, report the descriptive change together with its competing explanations. That is still useful for a bounded, reversible decision.

## What research says about the value of enterprise architecture

Three strands of research help Helios design its own evaluation.

- **A model of how value arises.** Tamm, Seddon, Shanks and Reynolds reviewed the literature and proposed that enterprise architecture creates benefits through four **benefit enablers**: organisational alignment, information availability, resource portfolio optimisation and resource complementarity {REF:eavalue}. Alignment can be improved by planning alone, but the two resource enablers depend on the plans actually being carried out. The model is conceptual, so it tells Helios which intermediate outcomes to measure rather than how large the benefits will be.
- **Survey evidence on success factors.** Lange, Mendling and Recker built a model of EA management success from the literature and interviews, and tested it with a survey of 133 practitioners {REF:lange}. Four success factors were supported: the quality of EA products, the quality of the EA infrastructure, the quality of EA services and **organisational anchoring**, the extent to which EA is embedded in the organisation's decisions and processes. Anchoring was the focal concept: it mediated the effects of infrastructure quality and service quality on the two success measures, intention to use EA management and organisational and project benefits. Satisfaction with EA proved irrelevant for determining or measuring success. A related survey by van de Wetering and colleagues linked EA-driven dynamic capabilities to organisational benefits, partly through innovativeness {REF:dynamic}. Its 299 respondents were CIOs and IT managers, and all measures were self-reported at one point in time.
- **Studies of practice.** Kotusev's case studies, discussed in Chapter 2, show practitioners relying on specific artefacts used in planning and decision processes, rather than on framework procedures {REF:kotusevartifacts}.

The strands agree on one practical point: value depends on whether architecture is used in real decisions. Most of the quantitative evidence, however, is cross-sectional and based on respondents' perceptions, so it shows association rather than effect. Helios therefore needs its own evidence: observed changes in decisions, operations and cash, measured with the methods in this chapter.

## Worked example: a disputed benefit claim

Helios's sponsor claims that the programme has made the group "adaptable", because leaders report more confidence in changing systems. Confidence, the ability to change and the benefit realised from a change are three different things, and each needs its own observation:

1. **Confidence:** ask the relevant roles the same defined questions before and after the programme, recording role and response rates.
2. **Ability to change:** rehearse a real change, such as adding a service-site relationship, and record effort, dependencies and defects.
3. **Realised benefit:** observe the affected service population over a stated period, including exceptions and concurrent staffing changes.

Consider rival explanations for a positive survey. Better leadership may both sponsor the architecture work and improve service independently. Better-performing divisions may have more resources to invest in architecture, reversing the assumed direction of influence. Enthusiastic respondents may rate both capability and performance highly. Only different kinds of evidence can tell these accounts apart; more responses of the same kind cannot.

Decide in advance what each result will change. A positive survey with a failed rehearsal calls for work on capability before any announcement of savings. A successful rehearsal with no business improvement points to a constraint elsewhere. An invoice reduction after a licence is removed supports that particular cash saving, net of replacement costs, and nothing more general. A sound report might say: "The participating team completed the specified change with fewer cross-team handoffs; the available comparison does not isolate the programme's effect on service outcomes."

## Technical measures keep their own scope

Software delivery measures help teams improve a particular service. The best known come from DORA, the DevOps Research and Assessment programme, which began as an independent research company and became part of Google Cloud in 2018. Its multi-year surveys, summarised by Forsgren, Humble and Kim, related software delivery performance to organisational performance {REF:accelerate}. DORA's current guide describes five measures in two groups {REF:dora}:

- **Throughput:** change lead time, deployment frequency and failed deployment recovery time.
- **Instability:** change fail rate and deployment rework rate.

The guide stresses context. It warns against comparing very different applications, such as a mobile app and a mainframe system, and against turning the measures into targets, which invites the distortion Campbell described. For Helios, a shorter change lead time for the customer lookup service supports the Digital Services objective when slow delivery was a real constraint, but it may change little if prioritisation, procurement or customer adoption dominates the elapsed time. Use such measures where they explain the relevant process rather than as a universal score for the architecture team.

The acronym has a second meaning in enterprise work. The EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act, Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, has applied to banks, insurers and other financial entities since January 2025, and sets requirements for ICT risk management, incident reporting, resilience testing and the management of ICT third-party providers {REF:eudora}. It is unrelated to the delivery measures. An architect working for a financial group must treat it as a legal obligation, with the supplier and recovery analysis of Chapter 9 as part of the evidence.

For each measure, state who reviews it and what action it can trigger. A growing queue of unresolved customer links could lead to more stewardship capacity, better validation at source or a narrower rollout. A measure that stays red without ever changing a decision has become a ritual.

## Key points

- Measure the outcome the programme promised, with a written contract for each measure and paired measures to resist distortion.
- Aggregated rates can reverse the pattern within groups (Simpson's paradox). Report actual and standardised figures.
- Units chosen because of an extreme result tend to improve anyway (regression to the mean). Use longer baselines and comparison groups.
- Difference-in-differences removes shared influences but relies on the parallel-trends assumption.
- Research suggests EA creates value when it is embedded in decisions, but most of the evidence is perceptual and cross-sectional. Each organisation must measure its own effects.
