# 7 Technology strategy and shared platforms

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Write a technology standard with a scope, rationale, owner and exception route.
- Evaluate a shared platform as a service, including the failures it can spread.
- Compare cloud strategies and the four meanings of portability.
- Explain Conway's law and the mirroring hypothesis, and use them to diagnose a delivery bottleneck.

## Four levels of technology guidance

Architecture guidance comes at four levels, and mixing them up causes needless conflict:

- A **principle** states a direction, such as "one authority for each shared fact".
- A **standard** is an agreed requirement or preferred technology within a stated scope, such as "customer-facing services use the workforce identity service for staff sign-in".
- A **reference design** is a reusable arrangement with stated conditions, such as a pattern for publishing customer events.
- A **project decision**, often recorded as an architecture decision record, applies these to one context.

If the levels are confused, a local experiment that uses a different library can look like a breach of an enterprise principle, when it is only a different implementation of the same design.

## A standard should solve an identified problem

A technology standard can reduce support variety, improve interoperability or provide a control that has already been tested. It should state its scope, rationale, permitted use, owner and exception route. "Use the preferred cloud" is incomplete because it says neither which workloads it covers nor what would justify another choice.

For example, Helios writes its workforce identity standard like this:

> **Standard TS-04: workforce identity and access.** **Scope:** all customer-facing and internal applications in production. **Requirement:** staff sign in through the workforce identity service, and accounts are created and removed by its lifecycle events. **Rationale:** acquired applications with local accounts left former staff with access. **Owner:** identity service owner. **Evidence for release:** an automated test shows that a removed account is refused. **Exceptions:** time-limited, approved by the standard owner, with compensating controls.

Standardise the properties that need coordination. For Helios, a common sign-in contract, a logging structure and a deployment evidence policy create more value than forcing one programming language on fundamentally different products.

## Treat a shared platform as a service

A **platform team** provides capabilities that other teams use: identity integration, deployment pipelines, monitoring, secrets management, network access or data processing. Its users need documentation, support, predictable change and an escape route for legitimate needs. A mandatory platform with long provisioning delays pushes teams towards unsupported alternatives. {FIG:platform} shows the contracts between product teams and platform services.

FIGURE platform | Shared platform services support product teams through contracts and named operating responsibility.

Measure the platform by what its users experience:

- time to obtain a working environment;
- the share of standard deployments that succeed first time;
- recovery time after platform incidents;
- the effort a team needs to meet the baseline controls.

Counting platform features or registered teams does not show that delivery improved.

A platform also creates shared dependencies. If every application depends on one deployment control plane, one identity configuration or one shared network component, a single failure can affect them all. This is a **common-mode failure**. Standardisation reduces variety but can increase common-mode risk, so the architecture should evaluate both effects. For each platform service, identify its failure domain, the applications inside it and the recovery path.

## Cloud strategy is a set of workload decisions

A vendor-neutral architecture begins with responsibilities and guarantees rather than identical implementations on every provider. Azure and AWS both provide managed compute, storage, databases, identity and monitoring, but the semantics, configuration and operating responsibilities of their services differ. Both providers publish well-architected frameworks that organise workload reviews around pillars such as reliability, security, cost optimisation, operational excellence and performance efficiency {REF:azurewa,awswa}. They are useful checklists of questions; the answers still come from the workload.

Helios compares three strategies:

- **Preferred provider.** New workloads use one provider unless an exception is approved.
- **Selective multi-provider.** A second provider is used for specific workloads with a clear reason.
- **Dual-provider operation.** Critical workloads are built to run on either provider.

A second provider removes some dependencies but adds skills, contracts, identity integration, monitoring and data transfer costs. It does not create disaster recovery by itself; recovery comes from tested procedures. Helios chooses a preferred provider for ordinary new workloads, allows exceptions for material constraints, and requires tested data export and a documented exit plan for strategic dependencies.

## Portability has four meanings

"Portable" can mean four different things:

- **Code portability:** the application can run elsewhere.
- **Data portability:** the data can be extracted with its meaning and relationships intact.
- **Operational portability:** deployment, identity, monitoring, skills and recovery work in the new environment.
- **Commercial portability:** licences and contracts allow the move at an acceptable cost.

A container image addresses only the first. For example, for a critical customer service, a useful exit rehearsal exports representative data, rebuilds a small workload in a separate environment and runs a real business journey through it. A policy demanding theoretical portability without funding any test gives weak assurance. Avoiding every managed service in the name of portability can also impose large operating costs with no realistic exit benefit.

## Lifecycle and technical debt

Standards need review dates and a process for retiring obsolete technologies. Record each technology's support status, upgrade dependencies and the business consequence of delay. **Technical debt** is the future cost of change created by past design shortcuts; connect it to a specific cost or risk, rather than using it as a label for any technology an architect dislikes.

Fund platform maintenance alongside product change. A mandated shared component with no capacity for patching, upgrades or support creates an unmanaged responsibility. The roadmap must include the work needed to keep the foundation usable.

## Organisation structure and system structure

In 1968 Melvin Conway stated the thesis that "organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations" {REF:conway}. The observation, now called **Conway's law**, has a simple mechanism: two components can only fit together well if the people designing them talk to each other, so the interfaces in a system tend to follow the lines of communication between teams.

MacCormack, Baldwin and Rusnak tested a related idea, the **mirroring hypothesis**, by comparing five matched pairs of software products {REF:mirroring}. Each pair contained an open-source product, developed by a loosely coupled, distributed community, and a commercial product of similar size and function, developed by a tightly coupled firm. In every pair the open-source product was significantly more modular, and the differences were large: up to a factor of eight in how far a change to one component could spread to others. The sample is small and the products' functional equivalence was not tested directly, so the study supports a relationship in software products rather than a rule that changing an organisation chart will improve any system. Skelton and Pais turn the same idea into a design approach: they define team types, including platform teams that provide internal services to teams aligned with business streams, and shape team boundaries to match the system boundaries the organisation wants {REF:teamtopologies}.

### Worked diagnosis: a shared database

Helios observes a problem: three application teams wait for a central database team to approve their changes, because their applications write directly to shared tables. {FIG:enterprise_dependencies} shows the technical dependencies and the approval dependencies side by side.

FIGURE enterprise_dependencies | Technical dependencies (solid) and approval dependencies (dashed) for three Helios applications and a shared database.

Before redesigning anything, the architect examines the ten most recent changes:

- 8 of the 10 needed approval from the central team.
- Waiting times for the ten changes were 0, 0, 2, 3, 6, 9, 9, 11, 12 and 15 working days, a median of 7.5 days; the two zeros needed no approval.
- 5 of the changes wrote directly to a shared table.
- 6 of the 8 approvals were for columns used by a single application, with no shared meaning.

A cross-tabulation shows how these counts fit together:

| Change touched | Needed central approval | No approval | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shared table, column with shared meaning | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| Shared table, column used by one application | 3 | 0 | 3 |
| The application's own table | 3 | 2 | 5 |
| **Total** | **8** | **2** | **10** |

The five shared-table changes are the first two rows. The six approvals for single-application data are the 3 + 3 in the second and third rows.

The pattern suggests that most of the delay comes from an approval rule rather than from genuine coupling. Six approvals concerned data that only one team uses, so delegating those decisions to the owning team would remove most of the waiting without any new technology. The three single-application columns in shared tables could move to their owning applications' tables, and the two changes to data with shared meaning deserve a technical response.

Two technical alternatives are available for those:

- **A shared data service.** Give the shared data a published contract, a funded backlog and a service owner. Teams use supported operations without individual requests. This reduces repeated work but makes the service's capacity an enterprise dependency.
- **Ownership by capability.** Each team owns its authoritative data and publishes events or APIs for others. This removes shared writes but adds integration, duplication and reconciliation work. If a rule genuinely needs one immediate transaction across the data, splitting it across teams can make things worse.

Conway's law explains why either option needs an organisational change as well. Product teams that still write to shared tables still depend on each other; a new API owned by an overloaded central team keeps the old bottleneck. Ten changes are a small sample, so treat the diagnosis as a hypothesis and keep the counterexamples, the changes where central review caught a real problem.

## Key points

- Separate principles, standards, reference designs and project decisions. A good standard states its scope, rationale, owner and exception route.
- A shared platform is a service with users. Measure it by their experience, and identify the common-mode failures it introduces.
- Cloud strategy is a set of workload decisions, and portability has code, data, operational and commercial meanings that need separate tests.
- Conway's law and the mirroring hypothesis link system structure to communication structure. Diagnose technical and organisational dependencies together before choosing a fix.
