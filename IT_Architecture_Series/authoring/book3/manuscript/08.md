# 8 Governance, decision rights and exceptions

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain IT governance as the allocation of decision rights, using five decision domains and six governance archetypes.
- Explain how ISO/IEC 38500 and COBIT 2019 separate governance from management, and apply them to a board's oversight of a programme.
- Design a decision-rights matrix that separates the right to give input from the right to decide.
- Design proportionate review triggers and a governed exception process.
- Evaluate governance mechanisms against the tensions they must manage, using research on bimodal IT.

## Governance decides who may decide

Helios's architecture work stalls whenever nobody knows who may approve a change of customer meaning or accept a risk. This chapter gives the group a way to allocate those decisions and to review changes in proportion to their consequences.

Weill and Ross define **IT governance** as specifying the decision rights and accountability framework that encourages desirable behaviour in the use of IT {REF:itgov}. Governance is not the same as management. Management makes and carries out decisions; governance determines who makes which decisions, who must be consulted and who is held accountable.

Their book, which draws on a study of about 250 enterprises, organises IT decisions into five related **decision domains**. Each has a Helios example:

- **IT principles:** high-level statements about how IT is used, such as "share data before processes".
- **IT architecture:** the organising logic for data, applications and infrastructure, such as the group customer-identifier standard.
- **IT infrastructure:** shared technical services, such as the cloud platform and the workforce identity service.
- **Business application needs:** the applications that units need, such as Maintenance's request for site-level scheduling.
- **IT investment and prioritisation:** how much to spend and on what, such as the allocation of the €9 million envelope.

They also describe six **archetypes**, typical patterns of who holds the rights for a domain:

| Archetype | Who decides | Where it might fit at Helios |
|---|---|---|
| Business monarchy | Senior business executives, sometimes with the CIO | Approving the transformation envelope |
| IT monarchy | IT executives | Technical standards for the cloud platform |
| Feudal | Each business-unit leader, for their own unit | Local features in a unit's own applications |
| Federal | Group executives and business-unit representatives together, with IT | The meaning of the group customer identifier |
| IT duopoly | IT executives with one group of business leaders | One division's application priorities |
| Anarchy | Individual users or small groups | Personal spreadsheets and tools |

The archetypes are descriptive. None is right everywhere, but each suits some domains better than others. An IT monarchy for infrastructure standards can work well; an IT monarchy for business application needs usually produces systems nobody asked for. Anarchy is rarely chosen deliberately, yet it is what an organisation gets when nobody holds a decision right explicitly.

### Input rights and decision rights

Weill and Ross also separate **input rights**, the right to be consulted, from **decision rights**, the right to make the call. They summarise an organisation's arrangements on a single page: a matrix of domains against archetypes, showing who provides input and who decides in each {REF:governance}. For Helios's year-one design:

| Domain | Input from | Decision by |
|---|---|---|
| IT principles | Business units, IT | Business monarchy: group executive committee with CIO |
| IT architecture | Business units, data owners | Federal: architecture council with unit representatives |
| IT infrastructure | Product teams | IT monarchy: CIO and platform owners |
| Business application needs | Users, architects | Feudal within shared standards: each unit |
| Investment and prioritisation | All units, architects | Business monarchy: board and executive committee |

## Standards for IT governance: ISO/IEC 38500 and COBIT 2019

Weill and Ross describe how decision rights are allocated. Two widely used standards describe what the governing body and management should do with them.

### ISO/IEC 38500

ISO/IEC 38500 gives principles for the governance of IT to the governing body of any organisation, such as a board of directors {REF:iso38500}. Its model separates governance from management. The governing body **evaluates** proposals and plans for the current and future use of IT, **directs** management through strategies and policies, and **monitors** performance and conformance against them. Management prepares the proposals and runs the IT.

The 2015 edition defined six principles: responsibility, strategy, acquisition, performance, conformance and human behaviour. Many textbooks still teach these. The current edition, ISO/IEC 38500:2024, replaces them with the eleven governance principles of ISO 37000 {REF:iso38500wp}:

- **Primary:** purpose.
- **Foundational:** value generation, strategy, oversight and accountability.
- **Enabling:** stakeholder engagement, leadership, data and decisions, risk governance, social responsibility, and viability and performance over time.

According to the working group's white paper, the 2024 edition's model also adds **engage stakeholders** as an element alongside evaluate, direct and monitor, and stresses that governance is exercised throughout the organisation, with management working inside the framework the governing body sets. For Helios, the board evaluates the options paper and the €9 million envelope, directs through the architecture principles and the funding gates, and monitors through gate results, the benefits register and overdue exceptions. Engaging stakeholders means hearing the business units and key customers before each gate rather than after it.

### COBIT 2019

COBIT 2019, published by ISACA, is a framework for the governance and management of enterprise information and technology {REF:cobit}. Its core model holds 40 objectives and keeps governance and management apart:

- **Governance objectives** sit in one domain, Evaluate, Direct and Monitor (EDM). Its five objectives include ensured benefits delivery and ensured risk optimisation.
- **Management objectives** sit in four domains: Align, Plan and Organise (APO); Build, Acquire and Implement (BAI); Deliver, Service and Support (DSS); and Monitor, Evaluate and Assess (MEA). Their 35 objectives include managed enterprise architecture (APO03) and managed data (APO14).

COBIT does not prescribe one governance system for every organisation. It tailors the system through eleven **design factors**: enterprise strategy, enterprise goals, risk profile, I&T-related issues, threat landscape, compliance requirements, role of IT, sourcing model for IT, IT implementation methods, technology adoption strategy and enterprise size. Each factor raises or lowers the priority of particular objectives and the capability level to aim for. At Helios, a risk profile shaped by acquired applications with local accounts, and an I&T-related issue of inconsistent customer data, would raise the priority of managed security services and managed data.

The three sources answer different questions. Weill and Ross ask who holds each decision right. ISO/IEC 38500 asks what the governing body must do. COBIT supplies a detailed catalogue of governance and management objectives and a method for tailoring it. An architect can use COBIT's managed enterprise architecture objective to check their own practice, and the evaluate–direct–monitor model to frame what the board needs from architecture: options to evaluate, principles through which to direct and evidence to monitor.

## A decision-rights matrix

The governance matrix sets the pattern. Day-to-day work needs a more specific matrix for recurring decisions. The following proposal for Helios names the decision, who provides input, who is accountable and what information the decision needs:

| Decision | Input from | Accountable decision-maker | Information expected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local implementation within standards | Product engineering, operations | Product owner, within delegated scope | Tests and a decision record |
| Meaning of the shared customer identifier | Domain owners, data stewards | Group data owner | Meaning and consumer-impact analysis |
| Strategic application consolidation | Business units, finance, architects | Investment sponsor or board | Options, costs, transition and benefits |
| Exception to a platform standard | Delivery, platform and risk specialists | Owner of the standard | Reason for the exception and compensating controls |
| Acceptance of material residual risk | Security, operations, legal, business | Authorised business risk owner | Consequences and treatment options |

The matrix makes the decision path visible before a dispute blocks delivery. It also prevents two common errors. A committee can review information and recommend, but someone must still be accountable. An architect can advise on a risk, but does not acquire the authority to accept a financial or legal risk merely because it appears on an architecture diagram.

## Review in proportion to consequence

A small, reversible implementation choice should not wait for the same forum as a change to the meaning of customer data. Define **review triggers**, the conditions that send a change for wider review:

- impact across business units;
- a new use of sensitive data;
- spending above a set threshold;
- a new strategic supplier dependency;
- a changed recovery promise;
- an exception to a mandatory control.

Teams should know when they may proceed on their own. Consult early on uncertain choices, and use a release gate to confirm conditions that can be verified. If the only architecture review happens just before deployment, it finds expensive problems too late. If every experiment needs detailed approval, teams cannot gather the information a good decision needs.

Record the scope of each approval. Approving a target architecture does not approve every later configuration. Tie release evidence to the version being deployed.

## Exceptions are governed decisions

An **exception record** states the affected standard, the reason, the scope, the duration, the owner, the risk, the compensating measures and the review trigger. It also states the path to compliance, or the condition under which the standard itself should change. An exception without an owner or an end date becomes invisible permanent policy.

For example, an acquired application cannot use the workforce identity service before its first migration deadline. A time-limited exception might allow restricted interim access with extra monitoring and a funded plan to integrate within six months. Whether that is acceptable depends on the actual risk and obligations. The record must describe controls that are actually operating; a planned control is a condition of approval until it works.

Repeated similar exceptions tell you something about the standard. The platform may not meet a common need, the migration schedule may be unrealistic, or the policy may be out of date. Governance should learn from the pattern rather than keep issuing waivers.

## Funding and incentives

Suppose the group mandates a common integration platform while each division pays only for its own delivery team. The platform team asks divisions to fund shared reliability work, and each division prefers another to pay, because all will benefit. A platform funded centrally without visible demand has the opposite problem: it may chase adoption counts rather than useful service.

Compare two funding models:

- **Central allocation** can fund capabilities whose benefits are shared and hard to attribute, but it needs an accountable sponsor and explicit outcome measures.
- **Consumption charges** reveal demand and cost, but a badly chosen unit can discourage the behaviour the architecture needs. Charging per published event, for example, might encourage teams to suppress events that reconciliation depends on.

A charging design must not contradict the architecture's correctness requirements.

## Governance tensions: research on bimodal IT

Some organisations split IT into a traditional mode, optimised for stability, and an agile mode, optimised for speed. Jöhnk and colleagues studied the governance of two such **bimodal IT** organisations, a public-sector IT function and the IT function of a multinational automotive company, through fourteen semi-structured interviews and internal documents {REF:bimodal}. They classified governance mechanisms as **structural** (roles and units), **procedural** (processes and rules) or **relational** (collaboration and trust), and as either **impeding** mechanisms that create or worsen challenges or **coping** mechanisms that help resolve them. For example, a diluted and distributed strategic vision impeded coordination, while synchronising agile release planning with the capacity of traditional IT helped.

They interpret the challenges as five paradoxes, pairs of goals that pull against each other and must be balanced rather than resolved once:

- **Strategic vision:** flexibility versus predictability.
- **Alignment:** aligning business with IT versus aligning the two IT modes with each other.
- **Organisation:** simplicity versus complexity of structures.
- **Distinction:** comparability between the modes versus differentiation.
- **Collaboration:** integration versus autonomy.

Helios is not a bimodal organisation, but it faces the same kinds of tension. The collaboration paradox appears whenever a division's autonomy meets a shared contract. The distinction paradox appears in group reporting, which needs comparable measures while divisions legitimately differ. The study is exploratory, based on two contrasting cases, so it provides sensitising questions rather than a recipe.

### Worked example: a change of meaning

Helios needs to add a service-site role to its customer event contract. Retail can use its existing buyer identifier; Maintenance needs a separate site identifier because entitlement depends on location. The implementation adds one field, but the request changes the meaning of the contract, so it is not eligible for automatic approval.

| Arrangement | How the decision works | What could defeat it |
|---|---|---|
| Central committee | One forum resolves meaning, priority and transition | Meeting delay; local specialists absent; approval without funded delivery |
| Delegated contract owner | The owner approves compatible additions under published rules | Delegation beyond expertise; affected consumers left out; no escalation route |
| Independent local change | Each division publishes its own interpretation | The same field acquires incompatible meanings; consumers absorb the cost |

A defensible choice delegates genuinely compatible changes to the contract owner but escalates this change of meaning to the group data owner, with both business roles represented. It uses all three kinds of mechanism:

- **Structural:** name the decision owner and the escalation authority.
- **Procedural:** require example records, consumer-impact tests, a migration window and a versioned decision.
- **Relational:** bring the service staff who resolve disputed cases into the design discussion.

The choice also needs a failure path. If the meaning cannot be agreed before the delivery deadline, keep the existing contract and publish a provisional interface to a bounded pilot, if its controls and funding are authorised. Do not silently repurpose a field in production. The sponsor then chooses whether to delay, narrow the scope or fund a separate bridge; the architect makes those consequences visible.

## Measure governance as a service

Track decision lead time by category, rework caused by late findings, overdue exceptions and consumer incidents after approved changes. Read them together. A shorter approval queue is weak evidence of success if teams now spend longer repairing mismatches, and a longer deliberation can be justified when it prevents a costly error. Classify requests by consequence, so that a change in the mix of work is not mistaken for a change in governance quality.

Publish decisions and their rationale in a form that teams can use. Meeting minutes nobody can find invite repeated debate; a concise standard, an example and a decision record reduce coordination effort while keeping accountability clear.

> **Common mistake.** Giving "the architecture board" every decision. Boards are good at reviewing and recommending. Accountability for accepting cost and risk belongs to named business owners.

## Key points

- IT governance allocates decision rights and accountability. Weill and Ross's five domains and six archetypes give a vocabulary for describing who decides what.
- ISO/IEC 38500 frames the governing body's work as evaluate, direct and monitor, with stakeholder engagement and eleven principles in its 2024 edition. COBIT 2019 separates five governance objectives from 35 management objectives and tailors them with design factors.
- Separate input rights from decision rights, and record both in a matrix before disputes arise.
- Match review to consequence with explicit triggers, and keep exceptions owned, bounded and visible.
- Funding models create incentives; a charging unit must not discourage behaviour the architecture depends on.
- Research on bimodal IT shows that governance balances persistent tensions with structural, procedural and relational mechanisms, some of which help and some of which hinder.
