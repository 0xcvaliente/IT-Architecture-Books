- Explain the difference between exploration and exploitation, and apply it to a portfolio of experiments.

## Exploration competes with current delivery

March distinguished **exploration**, the search for new possibilities, from **exploitation**, the refinement of what an organisation already knows {REF:march}. His models show why the two compete: exploitation pays back sooner and more reliably, so organisations tend to drive out exploration, even though their long-run adaptation depends on it. The models describe a mechanism, and each organisation still has to set its own innovation budget.

At Helios, improving the reliability of an existing integration is exploitation, and testing a new way to help technicians diagnose faults is exploration. The categories describe the purpose of the learning rather than the novelty of the technology. Introducing a familiar product into an unfamiliar business process can still be substantial exploration.

Manage exploration as a portfolio of **bounded experiments**, as shown in {FIG:innovation}. Each experiment states:

- the decision it will inform;
- its cost limit and duration;
- the data it will collect and the baseline it will be compared with;
- its stop conditions;
- the possible next actions: stop, revise or scale.

Protect time for learning, but make further investment depend on what is learned. A series of demonstrations with no decision record consumes money without reducing uncertainty.

## An AI-assisted service case

Helios proposes an assistant that drafts a service summary from the maintenance records an agent is authorised to see. Start by fixing the task and the authority boundary. In the pilot, the assistant may suggest text. It may not change entitlement, dispatch technicians or send anything to a customer without a person's action.

Separate the stages of the work: retrieving records, generating text, validating it, human review and the final business action. Access checks must apply to the retrieved records and to the output. A fluent summary can still state something unsupported, omit a safety condition or mix records from two customers. Wrong retrievals and excessive permissions need fixes in the design; a disclaimer on the screen leaves them in place.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework organises the work under four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage {REF:airmf}. For this case, **Govern** assigns accountability for the assistant; **Map** describes the context of use and who could be harmed; **Measure** evaluates the relevant errors; **Manage** decides how to respond, including how to withdraw the feature.

### Evaluate against a baseline

Compare the assistant with the current workflow and, where useful, with a simpler template that fills in fields from the record. Use representative cases, including ambiguous and incomplete records, and keep the evaluation cases separate from any examples used to tune the system. Record the model version, prompt, retrieval settings and data version, so that the result can still be interpreted after something changes.

Define error categories by consequence:

- an unsupported claim;
- content about the wrong customer;
- an omitted safety-relevant condition;
- a misleading order of events;
- disclosure of information the agent may not see.

For example, in a 200-case evaluation, agents took a median of 9 minutes per summary with the assistant and 14 minutes without it, including the time to correct drafts. Three drafts mixed in content from another customer's records. That is 1.5% of cases, and it triggers the pre-agreed stop condition for wrong-customer content until retrieval is fixed.

Small evaluations also have a statistical limit. If a severe error never occurs in 200 cases, the approximate 95% upper bound on its true rate is 3 / 200 = 1.5%, so the evaluation cannot show that the rate is below 1 in 1,000. Rare, serious errors need controls that prevent them as well as tests that look for them. Review can fail too, when users over-trust fluent text or lack time to check it, so the operating design needs escalation, traceability and a way to withdraw the feature.

## Sustainability needs a defined boundary

A claim that one design is greener than another needs a basis for comparison. The Green Software Foundation's **Software Carbon Intensity** (SCI) specification, published as ISO/IEC 21031, expresses emissions per **functional unit** R, such as one API call or one completed service request {REF:sci}. In its simplest form:

SCI = (E × I + M) per R

where E is the energy used, I is the carbon intensity of the electricity, and M is the share of the hardware's embodied emissions from manufacture.

For example, suppose one completed service request uses E = 0.02 kWh, the electricity intensity is I = 250 g CO2e per kWh, and the request's share of embodied hardware emissions is M = 1.5 g CO2e. The operational part is 0.02 × 250 = 5 g, so the SCI is 6.5 g CO2e per request. All three inputs are teaching assumptions; a real estimate would measure E and take I from the grid region.

Now suppose an optimisation halves the energy per request while demand triples. The answer depends on what happens to the hardware, so the assumption must be stated:

- **Hardware grows with demand.** If Helios adds servers in proportion to the extra requests, each request still carries 1.5 g of embodied emissions. The SCI falls to 0.01 × 250 + 1.5 = 4.0 g per request, a 38% improvement in intensity. Total emissions, however, rise from 6.5 g to 3 × 4.0 = 12.0 g for every request in the starting volume, an 85% increase.
- **Existing hardware absorbs the demand.** If the same servers handle three times the load, their embodied emissions are shared among three times as many requests, so M falls to 0.5 g. The SCI becomes 0.01 × 250 + 0.5 = 3.0 g, and the total rises to 3 × 3.0 = 9.0 g, a 38% increase.

In both cases operational energy rises by 0.5 × 3 = 1.5 times. Intensity and total impact answer different questions, so report both whenever demand can change, together with the hardware assumption. A lower emissions estimate also leaves the design's reliability and data-residency requirements in force.

## Decide what to scale

Scale only what the evidence supports. A successful drafting pilot authorises drafting, and automatic diagnosis or dispatch would need evidence of its own. Wider use changes the data population, the consequence of an error, the permissions, the cost and the support needed. Treat each material change in scope as a new decision with proportionate evidence.

## Learning versus standardisation

March's model of mutual learning describes individuals learning an organisation's code of practice while the code learns from individuals; if everyone converges on the code too quickly, the organisation stops learning {REF:march}. The model is stylised, so its application to Helios is an inference to test.

Suppose the architecture group standardises one customer-matching method after a successful pilot. Reuse cuts repeated experimentation and simplifies support. Then an acquired business turns out to identify customers by service site and contract, unlike the pilot's legal-party view. Enforcing the old rule without investigation would create systematic mismatches. The organisation needs a way for contrary local results to change the standard, without turning every preference into an exception.

Compare two policies:

- A **strict reuse** policy permits only the approved method and measures compliance. It risks preserving a mistaken assumption.
- A **bounded learning** policy requires the approved method by default, but permits a time-limited experiment with a documented hypothesis, isolated data, explicit costs and a decision about whether to change the standard. It costs coordination effort and risks uncontrolled variation if the bounds are weak.

To tell useful variation from accidental divergence, measure false matches, missed matches and the downstream harm of each, on the same eligible cases for both methods. A single overall accuracy figure can hide a dangerous minority of false merges. If the alternative is better only for site-based contracts, the standard may need a conditional rule rather than replacement. Record the counterexample and its scope in the architecture record, so that the debate is not repeated after staff turnover.

## Key points

- Exploration and exploitation compete for the same resources. Manage exploration as bounded experiments, each tied to a decision.
- AI pilots need a clear authority boundary, separated stages, consequence-based error categories and a baseline. Small samples cannot establish that rare, severe errors are rare.
- Sustainability claims need a functional unit and a boundary. Intensity can fall while total emissions rise.
- Standards should be able to learn: a bounded route for contrary local results keeps them from fossilising mistaken assumptions.
