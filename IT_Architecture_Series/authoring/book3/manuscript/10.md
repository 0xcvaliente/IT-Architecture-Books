# 10 Investment economics, benefits and uncertainty

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Calculate net present value, internal rate of return and simple and discounted payback for an architecture investment, stating the timing convention used.
- Apply real and nominal discount rates consistently, and compare options by total cost of ownership.
- Build a benefits register that separates cash, capacity and risk benefits without double counting.
- Evaluate a staged investment as a real option, using the value of the information a pilot provides.
- Test whether a recommendation survives changes to its assumptions, including a funding cut.

## A business case is a model of consequences

The board will release the €9 million only against business cases it can check. This chapter builds the case for the customer-handoff investment and tests how far it bends before it breaks.

Architecture changes cash flows through delivery cost, operation, transition, retirement, revenue and losses. A credible business case says who experiences each effect and when. Three rules prevent the most common errors:

- **Compare with a realistic baseline.** "Do nothing" still includes running costs, necessary maintenance, supplier renewals and known end-of-life events. A baseline that assumes free continuation forever makes every change look expensive; one that assumes certain disaster makes every change look attractive.
- **Count only incremental future cash flows.** Money already spent cannot be recovered by any choice, so it stays out of the comparison. Existing assets can still have future disposal, contract or transition consequences, and those belong in it.
- **Count the group's cash.** Moving a cost from one business unit to another saves the group nothing. Reducing staff effort creates capacity; it becomes a cash saving only when an actual payment stops.

## The Helios customer-handoff investment

The worked example in this chapter is the investment that makes cross-unit handoffs reliable. It draws on three roadmap work packages from Chapter 11: customer identity resolution and data foundations (€1.0 million), integration capability (€1.2 million) and €0.3 million of the adoption and assurance package, a total of €2.5 million within the €9 million envelope.

The separate workforce identity and access package (€0.5 million) is deliberately left out. It makes sure that staff accounts are created and removed through the group identity service, which reduces the risk of former employees keeping access. That is a risk benefit with its own justification, and charging it to the handoff case would make the handoff case look worse and the access case invisible.

The expected net cash benefit is €1.2 million a year, made up of:

- €0.5 million less spent on contractors who reconcile customer records by hand;
- €0.3 million of licence and support fees for two matching tools that will be retired;
- €0.4 million less paid in service credits to customers after failed handoffs.

To keep the arithmetic clear, the model treats the €2.5 million as spent at the start and the benefits as arriving at the end of years 1, 2 and 3, with nothing after year 3. In the roadmap (Chapter 11) most of this spending falls in years 1 and 2 and a small part, mainly integration work, in year 3. Because later spending is discounted, booking it all at the start makes the cost side slightly conservative; the board should know that the model simplifies the timing. All values are in euros; the discount rate is 8%.

## Net present value

**Net present value (NPV)** converts future cash flows to their value on a single date by discounting them, then subtracts the investment {REF:npv}:

NPV = −I + CF1/(1 + r) + CF2/(1 + r)² + CF3/(1 + r)³

where I is the investment at time zero, CFt is the net cash flow at the end of year t and r is the annual discount rate. At 8%, the three benefits are worth €1,111,111, €1,028,807 and €952,599 today. Their sum is €3,092,516, so the NPV is €592,516. The investment creates value under these assumptions, and the sensitivity analysis below shows how firmly.

### Internal rate of return and payback

The **internal rate of return (IRR)** is the discount rate at which the NPV is exactly zero {REF:irr}. For this investment it is 20.71%, well above the required rate of 8%, which agrees with the positive NPV. IRR is easy to communicate but has limits: it says nothing about the size of the gain, and cash flows that change sign more than once can have several IRRs. Use it alongside NPV.

**Payback** is the time until cumulative benefits repay the investment {REF:payback}. Its answer depends on a timing convention, which must be stated:

- **Year-end cash.** The NPV model places each year's benefit at the end of the year. Cumulative benefits are €1.2 million after year 1, €2.4 million after year 2 and €3.6 million after year 3, so the €2.5 million is repaid only when the year-3 cash arrives: a payback of 3 years. Discounted payback is also 3 years, because the discounted benefits reach €2,139,918 after two years and €3,092,516 after three.
- **Even accrual.** If benefits arrive evenly through each year, payback can be interpolated within year 3. Simple payback is 2 + 0.1 / 1.2 = 2.08 years. Discounted payback is 2 + 360,082 / 952,599 = 2.38 years.

The discounted figure is a hybrid: it interpolates within year 3 while still discounting that year's benefit as if it arrived at the year end, which is how most spreadsheets compute it. Interpolated payback is a common reporting convention, but it contradicts a model that books each year's benefit at the year end. Report the convention that matches the cash-flow model, here 3 years, and show the other only with its label. Payback is a useful liquidity check, but it ignores everything that happens after the cut-off.

### A listing to reproduce the figures

The following program computes the NPV under seven scenarios, including the two funding-cut scenarios discussed at the end of the chapter, the IRR by bisection, and payback under both timing conventions. It uses only the Python standard library.

~~~
"""NPV, IRR and payback for the Helios customer-handoff investment (EUR)."""

def npv(rate: float, investment: float, flows: list[float]) -> float:
    """Investment at time 0; flows[t - 1] arrives at the end of year t."""
    return -investment + sum(cf / (1 + rate) ** t for t, cf in enumerate(flows, 1))

def irr(investment: float, flows: list[float]) -> float:
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0                      # bisection: NPV falls as the rate rises
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if npv(mid, investment, flows) > 0 else (lo, mid)
    return mid

def payback(investment: float, flows: list[float], rate: float = 0.0,
            even: bool = False) -> float:
    """Cash arrives at year ends, or accrues evenly through each year if even=True."""
    left = investment
    for t, cf in enumerate(flows, 1):
        pv = cf / (1 + rate) ** t
        if pv >= left:
            return t - 1 + left / pv if even else t
        left -= pv
    return float('inf')

cost, base = 2_500_000, [1_200_000] * 3
scenarios = {  # name: (rate, investment at time 0, flows at the end of years 1, 2, ...)
    'Base case, 8%': (0.08, cost, base),
    'Lower rate, 4%': (0.04, cost, base),
    'Higher rate, 12%': (0.12, cost, base),
    'Benefits 20% lower': (0.08, cost, [960_000] * 3),
    'Benefits one year later': (0.08, cost, [0] + base),
    'Cut: 0.6m deferred': (0.08, cost - 600_000, [-600_000] + base),
    'Cut: 0.6m removed': (0.08, cost - 600_000, [800_000] * 3),
}
for name, (rate, inv, flows) in scenarios.items():
    print(f'{name:<24} NPV EUR {npv(rate, inv, flows):>12,.0f}')
print(f'IRR {irr(cost, base):.2%}')
for even, timing in [(False, 'year-end cash:'), (True, 'even accrual:')]:
    print(f'Payback, {timing:<15} simple {payback(cost, base, 0, even):.2f}, '
          f'discounted {payback(cost, base, 0.08, even):.2f} years')
~~~

The bisection assumes that NPV falls as the rate rises, which holds for an investment followed only by positive cash flows. It is used here for the base case only.

## Sensitivity: does the answer survive?

{FIG:investment} shows the first five scenarios. The case survives a higher discount rate and a one-year delay in benefits, but it is sensitive to the size of the benefits. The break-even annual benefit at 8% is €2,500,000 / 2.5771 = €970,084, which is 19.2% below the estimate. A 20% shortfall therefore turns the NPV slightly negative, to −€25,987. Delaying the benefits by one year, with the investment still at time zero, reduces the NPV to €363,441; a real delay would usually add a year of transition cost as well.

Sensitivity analysis shows what the conclusion depends on; it does not say how likely each scenario is. The useful output here is a question for the board: how confident are we that benefits will reach at least €0.97 million a year? The three benefit lines deserve different answers. The licence fees are contractual and easy to verify, the contractor saving depends on a staffing decision, and the service-credit reduction depends on how often handoffs fail in future. Keep full precision in calculation and round only the reported result. A difference of a few thousand euros means little when migration costs are uncertain by hundreds of thousands.

## Real and nominal rates

Cash flows and discount rates must use the same convention. **Nominal** amounts are in the euros of the year they occur; **real** amounts are in constant, today's prices. The Fisher relation links the two rates: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation) {REF:realrates}.

The base case treats the €1.2 million benefits as nominal: fixed euro amounts, as with a contract fee that does not rise. Suppose instead that €1.2 million is in today's prices and the benefits rise with inflation of 2.5% a year. Two consistent calculations give the same answer:

- Inflate the benefits to nominal amounts (€1,230,000, €1,260,750 and €1,292,269) and discount at the nominal 8%.
- Keep the benefits in today's prices and discount at the real rate, 1.08 / 1.025 − 1 = 5.37%.

Both give an NPV of €745,623. Discounting today's-price benefits at the nominal 8% gives €592,516 and understates the NPV by about €153,000, roughly a quarter of the base-case value. Check every business case for this mistake.

## Total cost of ownership

**Total cost of ownership (TCO)** counts all the costs of an option over its life: implementation, migration, licences, operation, support, training, upgrades and exit {REF:tco}. Chapter 5 compared options for Legacy Orders. Replatforming takes 8 months and repurchasing takes 12, and Legacy Orders keeps running, at €0.6 million a year, until the new service takes over. A fair comparison therefore covers the same five years from the decision for both options and includes the legacy running cost during each implementation.

| Option | One-off cost | Year 1 running cost | Years 2 to 5, each | Five-year TCO, undiscounted | Five-year TCO, present value |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Replatform | €0.60m | €0.55m | €0.45m | €2.95m | €2.49m |
| Repurchase | €0.90m | €0.60m | €0.40m | €3.10m | €2.68m |

The year-1 figures combine both systems. Replatforming pays legacy costs for 8 months (€0.40 million) and the new platform for 4 months (€0.15 million). Repurchasing pays legacy costs for the whole year. One-off costs are at the start and running costs at each year end, discounted at 8%.

Repurchase has the lower running cost once it is live, but the higher one-off cost and a longer period of paying for the old system. Over five years replatforming is about €0.19 million cheaper in present value, roughly 7%. A naive comparison that ignores the transition, assuming each new service runs from day one at its own cost (€0.45 million and €0.40 million a year for all five years), gives present values of €2.40 million and €2.50 million, a gap of only €0.10 million. The omitted legacy cost therefore nearly doubles the difference. The difference is still within the uncertainty of the estimates, which tells the team that cost leans towards replatforming but will not decide the choice alone; deadline risk, process fit and exit terms will.

## Benefits need owners and clean definitions

A **benefits register** records, for each benefit, its baseline, intended change, mechanism, measure, owner, timing and the data that will show it was realised. Benefits management research finds that organisations focus on implementing technology rather than on realising the benefits they expected, and recommends planning benefits explicitly from the start {REF:benefits}.

Classify each benefit:

- **Cash benefits** stop or reduce a payment, for example a licence fee after verified retirement.
- **Capacity benefits** free staff time, for example service agents who no longer reconcile records. They become cash only through a decision about staffing, contracts or new work.
- **Risk benefits** reduce exposure, for example fewer people with stale access after the workforce identity work. Describe the exposure and the mechanism; an invented probability multiplied by a dramatic loss is a weak estimate.

Avoid double counting. If reduced contractor hours are already in the lower operating cost, count them once. If customer retention improves, count the incremental contribution margin rather than gross revenue, and explain what else could have caused the improvement.

## Staging and real options

A staged programme buys information before an expensive commitment. In finance, a **real option** is the right, without an obligation, to take a later action on a real investment, such as expanding, delaying or abandoning it. Benaroch and Kauffman showed how option-pricing ideas can value flexibility in IT investments, such as the choice of when to deploy {REF:realoptions}. For most architecture decisions, a simple decision tree captures the essential point: flexibility has value only when new information can change what you do next.

### Worked example: a pilot before expansion

Helios is considering expanding the shared customer service to all three divisions. All values below are in euro millions at the same valuation date, so they are already discounted.

- If adoption is high, expansion is worth +1.0; if adoption is low, it is worth −0.6.
- Without further information, the probability of high adoption is 0.5.
- Keeping the current arrangement is worth 0.

Expanding now is worth 0.5 × 1.0 + 0.5 × (−0.6) = 0.20.

A pilot in one division costs 0.05, taken from the CRM and process pilot work package. The pilot gives a favourable result with probability 0.8 when adoption would be high and 0.2 when it would be low. Overall, a favourable result occurs with probability 0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.5. By Bayes' rule, the probability of high adoption after a favourable result is (0.5 × 0.8) / 0.5 = 0.8; after an unfavourable result it is 0.2.

- After a favourable result, expansion is worth 0.8 × 1.0 + 0.2 × (−0.6) = 0.68, so Helios expands.
- After an unfavourable result, expansion is worth 0.2 × 1.0 + 0.8 × (−0.6) = −0.28, so Helios does not expand and gets 0.

The pilot policy is worth 0.5 × 0.68 + 0.5 × 0 − 0.05 = 0.29, against 0.20 for expanding now. The **expected value of the information** is 0.34 − 0.20 = 0.14, so any pilot costing more than 0.14 would not be worth running. {FIG:pilot_decision} shows the tree.

FIGURE pilot_decision | A pilot creates value only when its result can change the next decision. Squares are decisions and circles are chance nodes; solid lines are choices, dashed lines are chance outcomes and values are in euro millions.

Two variations show where the value comes from. If the pilot reported "favourable" with probability 0.5 whatever the true adoption, it would carry no information: expansion would still be worth 0.20 after either result, and paying 0.05 would leave 0.15. If Helios had already signed an irrevocable contract for full expansion, the pilot could not change the action and its information value would be zero. A polished demonstration can therefore be a poor experiment.

The model leaves things out. A pilot may delay benefits, teach the team how to implement, or fail to represent the other divisions. A low-adoption outcome may be a loss the board cannot accept even when the expected value is positive. State these concerns openly and rework the model where they matter, rather than hiding them in a higher discount rate.

## Revisiting the case under the funding cut

Change card A cuts the programme envelope from €9.0 million to €7.2 million. The deferral scenario in Chapter 11 removes analytics improvement and part of legacy modernisation, and leaves the three packages behind the customer-handoff investment intact. Under that scenario the NPV is unchanged at €592,516.

Now suppose the cut fell instead on integration capability, taking €0.6 million from its €1.2 million. "Cut" can mean two different things, and the listing models both:

1. **Deferral.** The €0.6 million of work is postponed by a year and spent at the end of year 1. Because reliable handoffs need that work, the benefits also start a year later, in years 2 to 4. The cash flows are −€1.9 million at time zero, −€0.6 million at the end of year 1 and +€1.2 million at the end of years 2, 3 and 4. The NPV falls from €592,516 to €407,886.
2. **Removal.** The €0.6 million is never spent. It would have paid for the monitoring and recovery support that stops failed handoffs, so the €0.4 million a year of service-credit savings is lost. The cash flows are −€1.9 million at time zero and +€0.8 million at the end of years 1, 2 and 3. The NPV falls to €161,678.

Both versions save €0.6 million of programme budget, and both destroy value: €184,630 through delay, or €430,838 through lost benefits. Removal also leaves the case far more fragile, because a modest further shortfall in benefits would turn it negative. The architect's job is to make that consequence visible, so that the board can choose where the cut falls with its eyes open.

## Key points

- Compare options against a realistic baseline, count only incremental future cash flows, and count the group's cash rather than a unit's.
- NPV is the main measure; IRR and payback add useful perspectives but have known limits. Payback depends on a timing convention that must match the cash-flow model.
- Use nominal rates with nominal cash flows and real rates with real cash flows. Mixing them can move the NPV by a quarter or more.
- TCO counts every cost over the same period for each option, including the old system's running cost during the transition. Benefits registers separate cash, capacity and risk benefits and avoid double counting.
- Staging is a real option: a pilot is worth paying for only when its result can change the next decision. Funding cuts must be traced through to the cash flows they change.
