# 4 Operating models and strategic alignment

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain the four operating models defined by process integration and process standardisation.
- Analyse which capabilities need integration, standardisation or both, and justify each choice.
- Compare central, federated and local operating designs for a specific decision.
- Evaluate the alignment between business strategy and IT using the strategic alignment model.
- Distinguish an operational backbone from a digital platform, and classify an organisation's components accordingly.

## Sharing data and sharing processes are different choices

Two of Helios's divisions could share customer identity while running quite different fulfilment processes. Equally, they could follow the same process template while keeping their customer data apart. These are two separate choices:

- **Process integration** is the extent to which units share data, so that work in one unit depends on accurate, timely data from another.
- **Process standardisation** is the extent to which units perform the same work in the same way, with common definitions and procedures.

Integration buys coordination: a customer who calls Retail about a maintenance contract can be recognised and helped. Standardisation buys efficiency and predictability: one process is cheaper to train, support and improve than three. Each has costs, and each can be chosen capability by capability.

## Four operating models

Ross, Weill and Robertson define an **operating model** as the level of business process integration and standardisation an organisation needs to deliver its goods and services to customers {REF:eastrategy}. Crossing the two dimensions gives four models, shown in {FIG:operating_models}:

- **Diversification** (low integration, low standardisation). Independent units with few shared customers or data. Each unit chooses its own processes and systems.
- **Coordination** (high integration, low standardisation). Units share customer, product or supplier data, but each keeps its own processes.
- **Replication** (low integration, high standardisation). Similar units run the same standard processes, but have few reasons to share data with each other.
- **Unification** (high integration, high standardisation). One business with standard processes and shared data across the organisation.

The same authors define enterprise architecture as the organising logic for business processes and IT infrastructure that reflects the integration and standardisation requirements of the operating model {REF:eastrategy}. In their account, an organisation builds a **foundation for execution**, a set of digitised core processes and shared data, in stages. Organisations move from business silos, through standardised technology and an optimised core, towards business modularity. A related research briefing argues that IT should be focused on the operating model rather than on a strategy that may change every year {REF:operatingmodel}.

### Applying the grid to Helios

Helios today looks like a diversified group. Acquisitions brought their own systems, customer identifiers and processes, and little data crosses unit boundaries. The board's objectives point upwards on the grid: reliable cross-unit handoffs and a coherent customer view both need integration. The case for standardisation is weaker. The units' fulfilment processes differ, and nobody has yet shown whether those differences are accidental or legitimate.

That reasoning suggests Coordination as the year-one target. It also shows a limit of an enterprise-wide label: different capabilities need different positions on the grid.

The grid describes how units relate to each other, so it applies only to capabilities that more than one unit performs or depends on. A capability performed by one unit alone, such as technician scheduling in Maintenance, has no position on it; its design is a local decision. For the cross-unit capabilities, the question is how much the units need to share data and how much they need to work the same way:

| Cross-unit capability | Integration and standardisation needed | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Customer identity resolution | High integration, low standardisation | Units must recognise shared customers; local processes can stay |
| Group financial reporting | High integration, high standardisation | One set of definitions is a legal and board requirement |
| Workforce identity and access | Low integration, high standardisation | The same minimum controls apply in every unit; little business data is shared |
| Service fulfilment (visits, deliveries, subscriptions) | Low integration, low standardisation | Each unit fulfils a different product; the handoffs between them rely on shared customer identity rather than shared fulfilment |

Read the table as a set of hypotheses for owners to confirm. Its value is that each row can be argued on its own evidence, instead of one debate about "centralisation".

## Strategic alignment

Henderson and Venkatraman's **strategic alignment model**, first published in 1993, explains why alignment is more than IT taking orders from the business {REF:alignment}. It has four domains, and each is described by three components:

| Domain | Components | Helios example |
|---|---|---|
| Business strategy | Business scope, distinctive competencies, business governance | Which customers and services the group offers; reliable cross-unit service as a competence |
| IT strategy | Technology scope, systemic competencies, IT governance | Shared customer identifiers and integration as competencies; who decides on IT |
| Organisational infrastructure and processes | Administrative structure, processes, skills | Three business units, their service processes and their staff |
| IT infrastructure and processes | IT architecture, processes, skills | Forty-three applications, platform operations, architects and engineers |

The model links the domains in two directions. **Strategic fit** links the external choices, where to compete and with what distinctive capabilities, to the internal arrangements that carry them out. **Functional integration** links the business side to the IT side. Alignment requires both: a sound IT strategy that the IT infrastructure cannot deliver fails on fit, and an excellent IT department pursuing goals the business does not share fails on integration.

The authors describe four **alignment perspectives**. Each starts from one strategy domain, works through a second domain and changes a third:

| Perspective | Starts from | Works through | Changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategy execution | Business strategy | Organisational infrastructure | IT infrastructure |
| Technology transformation | Business strategy | IT strategy | IT infrastructure |
| Competitive potential | IT strategy | Business strategy | Organisational infrastructure |
| Service level | IT strategy | IT infrastructure | Organisational infrastructure |

In strategy execution, the business designs its organisation and IT follows. In technology transformation, the business strategy calls for a new IT strategy, which reshapes the IT infrastructure. In competitive potential, new IT capabilities open new business strategies. In service level, IT strategy shapes the IT infrastructure so that IT serves the organisation well.

For example, Helios's board is currently following a strategy-execution path: improve cross-unit service, then design the processes and systems to support it. The competitive-potential path matters too. Once customer identity is resolved across units, the Digital Services unit could offer a single maintenance-plus-monitoring contract to industrial customers, a product the group cannot sell today. An architect who presents the customer identity work only as a cost-saving measure hides that option from the board.

Alignment is therefore a recurring test. At each major investment, ask whether the capability, process, information model and responsibilities still support the current strategy. If Helios later decides to sell a division, separability becomes more valuable than integration, and the architecture should show the cost of each direction rather than pretending one target suits both.

## Operational backbone and digital platform

Ross, Beath and Mocker extend the operating-model argument to digital offerings {REF:designed4digital}. From field research in large companies, they describe five building blocks of digital business: shared customer insights, an operational backbone, a digital platform, an accountability framework and an external developer platform. Two of them matter most for enterprise architecture:

- An **operational backbone** is the integrated set of core systems, data and processes that runs the business reliably and efficiently, such as transaction processing, back-office services and authoritative core data.
- A **digital platform** is a set of reusable business, data and technology components from which teams can quickly assemble, test and change digital offerings.

The two need different things. The backbone values stability, integration and standard processes, and changes deliberately. The platform values speed, reuse and experimentation, and changes continually. The backbone is necessary for a digital business, because offerings built on unreliable core data inherit its errors, but it does not by itself let a company innovate quickly.

At Helios, ERP Core, the party register, the CRMs and the billing process form the backbone, and the Coordination choice above is mainly a decision about it. The platform is still small: a customer lookup service offered by the link hub, events announcing verified customer links, the workforce sign-in service and shared monitoring. Digital Services could build the maintenance-plus-monitoring contract from those components without changing the backbone's processes. Two design rules follow. Platform components read and change backbone data only through published contracts, and the platform's faster release cycle must never become a route for unreviewed changes to the backbone.

## Who decides, funds and operates

An operating model has to be expressed as responsibilities. For Helios's year-one design:

- **Group** sets customer-identity standards, minimum security controls and consolidated reporting definitions.
- **Business units** own their service processes, local product features and approved exceptions.
- **Shared platform teams** provide workforce identity, customer matching, integration and monitoring services under published contracts.

Two questions often go unanswered. The first is funding. If every division expects another to pay for the customer-mapping service, a sound shared capability may never be maintained. The second is priority. If the central team sets all priorities without understanding local needs, divisions build unofficial alternatives.

Match the decision level to the consequence. A change to a local screen can stay with a product team. A change to the meaning of the enterprise customer identifier affects reporting and integrations across units, so it needs broader agreement. Centralising both decisions slows the small one; decentralising both lets incompatible facts spread.

## The cost of standardisation

A common process reduces variation, simplifies training and makes shared data comparable. It can also remove useful local differences or push exceptions into manual work. Examine the variation before removing it. Some is accidental legacy. Some reflects a distinct product, customer promise or contract.

For example, Maintenance closes a service case when the technician finishes the work, while Digital Services closes a case only after the customer confirms the fix. Mapping both events to a single "closed" status makes the reports look standard while hiding two different meanings. There are two honest options: agree a common business definition of closure and change one process, or keep two distinct events and map them openly for each measure that needs them.

Standardisation also has transition costs: training, data conversion, a period of running two processes, and changes in accountability. These can exceed the licence savings in the first years. Count them in the business case and measure whether the common process is actually used. An approved process diagram is not proof that daily work follows it.

## Worked comparison: three operating designs

Consider three designs for Helios's service operations:

- **Central consolidation.** All divisions move to one CRM and one closure process.
- **Federated coordination.** Divisions keep their CRMs and fulfilment processes, and standardise customer identity, selected event contracts and group measures.
- **Local autonomy.** Operational decisions stay local; group reporting is reconciled at month end.

| Dimension | Central consolidation | Federated coordination | Local autonomy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Process ownership | One common process owner | Local owners within shared contracts | Local owners |
| Customer meaning | One governed operational definition | Shared matching, explicit local meanings | Local definitions, reconciled for reports |
| Main dependency | Central product and process capacity | Contract and platform coordination | Local delivery capacity |
| Main risk | The common process fits no unit well | Shared contracts lack funded owners | Group promises exceed what reconciliation can deliver |

Central consolidation makes one process easier to enforce and can remove duplicate configuration. It concentrates change decisions, and it requires a genuinely common process, which means an authorised business change as well as a field mapping.

Federated coordination corresponds to the Coordination operating model. It allows different processes but needs durable ownership of shared contracts. A shared customer-matching service without funded stewardship becomes an unreliable dependency for everyone. Each division must agree how disputed matches are resolved and how quickly it publishes the events others depend on.

Local autonomy suits a strategy in which divisions have separate customers and little cross-selling. It costs delayed group visibility and manual reconciliation, which may be acceptable for monthly reporting but not for a promised cross-division service journey.

Helios chooses federated coordination for year one. The group has a clear need for shared visibility, but no one has shown that the divisions' fulfilment processes are equivalent. The decision states what would change it: if analysis shows that process differences are accidental and one product supports all obligations, central consolidation becomes the better option; if a division is to be sold, a narrower integration boundary is better.

Keep funding, process ownership, data authority and deployment as separate questions. A centrally funded customer identity service can support locally owned applications. A shared cloud account says nothing about common process meanings. Show each relationship explicitly so that reviewers can see exactly where people agree and disagree.

## Key points

- Process integration and process standardisation are separate choices. Together they define four operating models: Diversification, Coordination, Replication and Unification.
- Enterprise architecture expresses the operating model as shared data, processes and infrastructure. Different capabilities can sit in different parts of the grid.
- Strategic alignment needs both strategic fit and functional integration, and technology can open new strategies as well as support existing ones.
- An operational backbone runs the core business reliably; a digital platform lets teams assemble new offerings quickly. They need different change processes and meet only through published contracts.
- Operating choices must be turned into explicit responsibilities for deciding, funding and operating shared services.
- Helios's year-one choice is federated coordination, with stated conditions under which it would change.
