# How to study enterprise architecture

This book teaches the organisational side of IT architecture. It asks which business capabilities an organisation should improve, how applications and data should support them, who may make each consequential decision, and how a transformation programme can create value that people can see and measure. It suits advanced undergraduate and postgraduate study, and practitioners who already design solutions and now need to work across a whole organisation.

Enterprise architecture draws on several disciplines. You will use ideas from strategy, information systems research, finance, governance, change management and evaluation methods. Each chapter introduces the ideas it needs, so you can study the book on its own. A working knowledge of how applications, databases and integrations behave will help, but the book explains the technical points it relies on.

## How the chapters work

Every chapter follows the same pattern:

- **Learning objectives** state what you should be able to do when you finish.
- **Worked examples** apply each idea to one continuing case, usually with numbers you can check.
- **Exercises** mix recall, calculation, design and critique. Answers and marking guidance are in the answers chapter at the end of the book.
- **Key points** summarise the chapter in a few lines for revision.

Chapters 1 and 2 set out the problem and the main frameworks. Chapters 3 to 9 build an enterprise model: capabilities, operating model, applications, data, technology platforms, governance and risk. Chapters 10 to 15 turn that model into decisions about investment, sequencing, change, sourcing, measurement and innovation. Chapter 16 shows how to read research critically, and Chapter 17 is a capstone project that brings everything together.

## The continuing case

**Helios Services Group** has three business units: Industrial Maintenance, Retail Distribution and Digital Services. Acquisitions have left it with forty-three applications, several customer identifiers for the same organisations and service reports that disagree with each other. Annual IT operating expenditure is €12 million. The board has approved a separate three-year transformation envelope of €9 million, released in stages as results come in.

Helios, its people, its budgets and its datasets are fictional teaching examples, as are the other organisations in the book. The numbers are realistic in scale, but they are assumptions for practice rather than benchmarks or supplier prices.

The board wants four things:

- more reliable handoffs between units when a customer needs service;
- a coherent view of each customer across the group;
- less duplication of applications and effort where duplication brings no benefit;
- a platform on which teams can launch new digital services.

Two kinds of identity run through the case, and the book keeps them apart. **Customer identity resolution** decides which records in different systems describe the same customer organisation, site or contact. **Workforce identity and access** controls how Helios's own staff sign in to applications and lose that access when they change role or leave. They have different owners, data, risks and budgets.

Business units remain accountable for their own results, and some of their processes differ for good reasons. "One system for everything" is therefore a hypothesis to test rather than an agreed requirement. Your job, built up chapter by chapter, is to propose a three-year roadmap that is affordable, keeps the business running at every intermediate step and can change when new facts arrive. Two change cards test that last property. The first cuts the envelope by 20%. The second adds an acquired business with a different customer data model.

## A ten-week study route

Plan on five days a week at four to five hours a day, or about 200 to 250 hours in total, including reading, modelling, calculation and revision. Keep a dated copy of your enterprise model before each change card so that you can explain how and why your decisions changed.

| Week | Chapters | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 and 2 | Problem statement and a tailored method for Helios |
| 2 | 3 and 4 | Capability map, heat map and operating-model choice |
| 3 | 5 and 6 | Application dispositions and a fact-authority table |
| 4 | 7 and 8 | Platform policy and decision-rights matrix |
| 5 | 9 and 10 | Service risk register and investment model |
| 6 | 11 and 12 | Dependency roadmap and adoption plan |
| 7 | 13 and 14 | Supplier evaluation and measurement plan |
| 8 | 15 and 16 | Bounded experiment and critical research synthesis |
| 9 | 17 | Three-year transformation dossier |
| 10 | 17, change cards | Revised programme and board defence |

## Sources, numbers and judgement

References are numbered in IEEE style in the order of first citation and listed at the end of the book. Different kinds of source support different kinds of claim. For example, a research paper can show what was observed in particular organisations. A standard defines terms and methods. A practitioner book or official guide describes how a method is meant to be used. None of them can show that a particular Helios investment will succeed. That argument has to be built from Helios's own data, and the book shows you how.

Treat every worked number as something to recompute. Several chapters print short Python programs that reproduce their calculations; they use only the standard library and run as printed. Discount rates, costs and benefit estimates are teaching inputs for practice. Where a design depends on law or contract, such as data retention or exit rights, the book frames it as a question to settle with the organisation's specialists.
