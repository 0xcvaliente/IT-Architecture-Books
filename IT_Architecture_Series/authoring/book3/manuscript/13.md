# 13 Sourcing, procurement and supplier ecosystems

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Compare build, buy and hybrid sourcing choices over the whole lifecycle of a responsibility.
- Apply mandatory conditions before weighted scoring, and test how sensitive a ranking is to its weights.
- Translate architectural assumptions into questions for a contract.
- Design an exit test that checks meaning and relationships as well as file transfer.

## Source a responsibility rather than a feature list

Start with the business responsibility to be supported. A customer platform may need account relationships, customer identity resolution, staff sign-in, service history, data export, recovery and operational support. A feature checklist that leaves out data authority or exit can select a product that shines in a demonstration and then fits the enterprise poorly.

Build, buy and hybrid are families of choices:

- **Build** gives control over selected design decisions but needs lasting engineering and operating capacity.
- **Buy** transfers some implementation work to a supplier but keeps configuration, integration, governance and supplier management with the buyer.
- **Hybrid** buys a core service and builds the interfaces or domain behaviour that make Helios's work distinctive. For example, Helios could buy a case-management product and build its own customer-matching rules.

Research offers two useful lenses. Transaction cost economics asks which transactions an organisation should govern through markets and which it should govern internally, matching each kind of transaction to a suitable governance structure {REF:williamson}. In IT terms, the more a service depends on knowledge specific to Helios, such as its customer-matching rules, the more costly and risky it is to buy it through a simple market contract. A review of IT outsourcing research by Lacity, Khan and Willcocks drew lessons from 191 studies, covering why organisations outsource, how they manage risk, and the client capabilities and relationship practices associated with success {REF:lacity}. A consistent message is that outsourcing changes the buyer's work rather than removing it: the client still needs strong capabilities in contracting, integration and supplier management.

## Compare the whole lifecycle

Compare total cost of ownership over the whole lifecycle (Chapter 10). Include discovery, licences, implementation, migration, integration, operation, support, training, upgrades, data retention and exit. For each cost, record who pays it and which assumptions it depends on: transaction volumes, contract renewal terms, exchange rates or organisational change. A subscription with a low first-year cost can still cost more than a build over the whole lifecycle.

Dependence is also a cost. **Vendor lock-in** means being unable to move away from a supplier without substantial cost. A survey of cloud adopters by Opara-Martins, Sahandi and Tian found that the problem grows as organisations move more of their computing to the cloud. The authors recommended attention to contracts, standard data formats and interfaces, and awareness of dependencies from the start {REF:lockin}. {FIG:sourcing} shows the procurement sequence that keeps these concerns in view.

## Apply mandatory conditions before weights

Helios evaluates three suppliers for a customer workflow service. A tested export that keeps the required identifiers and relationships is **mandatory**. Supplier C cannot demonstrate it, so C is ineligible under the current requirement. However low its price, C cannot win by trading its price against the missing export, because a mandatory condition is not a weighted criterion.

For the eligible suppliers A and B, the panel scores three criteria from 1 to 5, where higher is better:

| Criterion | Weight | A score | B score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functional fit | 0.40 | 4 | 3 |
| Operability | 0.35 | 3 | 5 |
| Cost suitability | 0.25 | 4 | 3 |

The weighted totals are 0.40 × 4 + 0.35 × 3 + 0.25 × 4 = 3.65 for A and 0.40 × 3 + 0.35 × 5 + 0.25 × 3 = 3.70 for B. A difference of 0.05 on a five-point scale of judgements is fragile. If the weights change to 0.50, 0.25 and 0.25, A scores 3.75 and B scores 3.50, and the ranking reverses.

### Finding the tipping point

A's scores differ from B's by +1, −2 and +1 on the three criteria. Since the weights sum to 1, the difference in totals is (1 − w) − 2w = 1 − 3w, where w is the operability weight, however the remaining weight is split between the other two criteria. The ranking therefore tips at w = 1/3: below it A leads, above it B leads. The following program applies the mandatory condition and then checks three operability weights.

~~~
"""Supplier comparison: apply the mandatory condition first, then weighted scores."""

SUPPLIERS = {  # name: (passes the export test, scores for fit, operability, cost)
    'A': (True, (4, 3, 4)),
    'B': (True, (3, 5, 3)),
    'C': (False, (5, 4, 5)),
}

def ranking(weights: tuple[float, float, float]) -> list[tuple[str, float]]:
    assert abs(sum(weights) - 1) < 1e-9, 'weights must sum to 1'
    totals = {name: sum(w * s for w, s in zip(weights, scores))
              for name, (eligible, scores) in SUPPLIERS.items() if eligible}
    return sorted(totals.items(), key=lambda item: -item[1])

for weights in [(0.40, 0.35, 0.25), (0.50, 0.25, 0.25)]:
    print(weights, [(name, round(total, 2)) for name, total in ranking(weights)])

for w in [0.30, 1 / 3, 0.36]:          # vary the operability weight
    rest = 1 - w
    result = ranking((rest * 0.6, w, rest * 0.4))
    print(f'operability {w:.3f}:', [(name, round(total, 3)) for name, total in result])
~~~

Running it prints:

~~~
(0.4, 0.35, 0.25) [('B', 3.7), ('A', 3.65)]
(0.5, 0.25, 0.25) [('A', 3.75), ('B', 3.5)]
operability 0.300: [('A', 3.7), ('B', 3.6)]
operability 0.333: [('A', 3.667), ('B', 3.667)]
operability 0.360: [('B', 3.72), ('A', 3.64)]
~~~

Supplier C never appears, whatever the weights. The decision between A and B rests on one question: how much does operability matter, relative to everything else? That question deserves direct evidence. A realistic recovery and support exercise with both suppliers is worth more than adding twenty minor criteria to the matrix. Record the basis for each score, and keep unresolved assumptions as conditions on the award rather than rounding them into a confident total.

## Contract and architecture must describe the same service

Turn each important architectural assumption into a question for procurement and legal specialists:

- Which data can be exported, in what structure, how quickly and at what cost?
- What happens to the data and the service after termination?
- Which interfaces are supported, and how are breaking changes announced?
- Who may approve subcontractors or changes to how data is processed?
- What evidence supports the supplier's recovery claims?

The architect does not draft jurisdiction-specific clauses. The architect's contribution is to state the technical and operational consequence the contract must address. A legal right to export is of little use if the export cannot be loaded and reconciled in the receiving system; a technical export is of little use if Helios lacks the right to use the data after exit.

Service credits deserve the same scrutiny. A credit may compensate for an outage under agreed terms without restoring the business service, so assess detection, response, restoration and data correction separately. The buyer's own responsibilities matter too. Configuration, identity, application design and backup choices often determine the service that users actually experience.

## Test exit before dependence becomes deep

Define an exit test early, while dependence is still shallow. For example, Helios's test for the customer workflow service is:

1. Export a representative dataset: 5,000 customer organisations with their sites, contracts and two years of case history.
2. Load it into a separate test environment.
3. Reconcile record counts, keys, missing values and relationships against the source.
4. Rebuild one required report and compare it with the version produced by the current system.
5. Run three real business journeys, such as opening a case for an existing site.
6. Record the effort, the elapsed time, unsupported fields and any rights needed to keep using the data.

A small, real export reveals dependence that a general statement about portability hides. Repeat the test when the supplier releases major changes.

Look at concentration across suppliers as well. Several apparently separate products may depend on the same identity provider, cloud region or implementation partner. Operational independence has to be checked dependency by dependency, whatever the supplier list suggests. Deliberately shared services can also simplify control, provided the shared dependency is recognised and managed.

## Key points

- Source a business responsibility over its whole lifecycle. Buying changes the buyer's work rather than removing it.
- Apply mandatory conditions before weighted scoring. A low price cannot compensate for a failed mandatory condition.
- Weighted scores are judgements. Find the weight at which the ranking tips, and gather direct evidence about that criterion.
- The contract and the architecture must describe the same service, including export, exit, change and recovery.
- Test exit early with real data, and look for hidden concentration across suppliers.
