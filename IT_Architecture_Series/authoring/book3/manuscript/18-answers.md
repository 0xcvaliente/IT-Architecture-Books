# Exercise answers and assessment guidance

Attempt each exercise before reading its answer. Numerical answers follow the assumptions stated in the chapter and show their working. For open questions, the answer gives a model response and the criteria a marker should look for; other answers are equally valid when their authority, reasoning and consequences are explicit.

## Chapter 1

**1.1** Retail deploys a reliable customer database with its own identifiers and no shared correction process. Retail's service works well, but group reporting and cross-division handoffs now need manual reconciliation, because nobody can match Retail's customers to the other units'. The enterprise cost comes from incompatible identifiers and unclear authority rather than from poor local engineering, so a shared identifier contract may fix it without replacing the database. Credit answers that name the mechanism as well as the symptom.

**1.2** Three examples: annual licence and support spend on duplicate applications, measured from invoices after verified retirement (owner: CIO); hours per month spent reconciling duplicate customer records, from time recording (owner: service operations manager); the percentage of cross-unit handoffs that fail because identifiers do not match, from the integration log (owner: group data owner). Each needs a baseline period and population. Application count alone is a weak measure, because consolidation can leave interfaces, support work and contract costs in place.

**1.3** Chain: verified links, then correct routing, then fewer reassignments, then faster resolution. Challenge the first link with audited false matches; the second with routes that ignore the verified identifier; the third with an unchanged reassignment rate; the fourth with data showing that technician availability dominates elapsed time. Credit answers in which each challenge is something that could actually be observed.

**1.4** Overall reassignment is 330 / 5,000 = 6.6%. Identity-related reassignments are about 0.40 × 330 = 132, or 132 / 5,000 = 2.6% of eligible requests, against 3.6% last quarter. The comparison may mislead because each share comes from a review of only 100 cases, so the 40% and 46% are uncertain by several points; and because seasonal demand, case mix, or a change in how reviewers classify causes could move the figures without any real change. Regression to the mean is also likely, because last quarter was the worst of the past eight (Chapter 14).

**1.5** A model brief asks three questions: how the service processes differ and which differences are contractual; who should be authoritative for each shared customer fact; and what consolidation and a staged alternative would cost. Its outputs are a process-variation assessment, a fact-authority proposal and two costed options. The group service director owns it, supported by the architect, with a six-week deadline. Decision rule: consolidate if the process differences are accidental and one product meets all contractual obligations at an acceptable transition cost; otherwise fund shared identity and contracts first and revisit in a year. Credit a rule that the board could apply mechanically to the outputs.

**1.6** The claim jumps from an association (mature EA and profit occur together) to a causal effect of buying a tool. The missing steps are a mechanism (how would the tool change decisions, data quality or operations?), the direction of influence (profitable firms may simply invest more in architecture), the definition of "mature EA" (a practice rather than a tool) and transfer to Helios. A sound response asks which decisions the tool would improve and how that improvement would be observed.

**1.7** Reporting to the CIO keeps the team close to technology decisions, platforms and delivery, but the board may see it as an IT specialism and business units may bypass it. Reporting to the transformation programme director puts it next to investment and sequencing decisions, but it may lose influence over run-the-business technology and be disbanded when the programme ends. One good solution keeps a reporting line to the CIO, gives the head of architecture a standing seat on the transformation board and embeds solution architects in the units. Rhythm: fortnightly design reviews against the review triggers, a monthly portfolio and exception update, a quarterly roadmap review tied to funding gates and an annual review of principles. Measures: the share of significant investment decisions that used current architecture information; median time from design submission to decision; exceptions past their expiry date. Credit measures of effect on decisions rather than counts of documents.

## Chapter 2

**2.1** A taxonomy classifies what could be described: Zachman's grid used to check whether Helios has documented the "why" of its CRM decision. A method orders the work: a tailored ADM cycle for the customer-identity decision. A modelling language expresses the model precisely: ArchiMate elements and relationships for the service view. A practice is what architects actually do: Helios's architects attending investment reviews with a one-page options paper. Reward answers that show the four are complementary.

**2.2** Board three-year goals: why (motivation), scope contexts or business concepts depending on detail. Technician on-call rota: who (responsibility) and when (timing), technology physics or tool components. Retail warehouse network diagram: where (distribution), technology physics. Entitlement rules for maintenance contracts: why (motivation) and how (process), business concepts. Nightly warehouse load schedule: when (timing), technology physics. List of data owners: who (responsibility), business concepts. The most important remaining gap for the customer-identity decision is a business-concepts definition of what is shared (what, inventory) together with the rules that justify sharing it (why, motivation). Accept other gaps with a reason tied to the decision, and accept defensible row placements when the student explains the level of detail.

**2.3** A model answer. Preliminary (light, half a week): confirm that existing principles and decision rights apply to the acquisition. Phase A (full, 1.5 weeks): scope, sponsor, stakeholders from both businesses, and the decisions required. Phases B and C (full, 4 weeks): profile the acquired data, model party, site and contract relationships, assess access rules and affected applications. Phases G and H: set up governance for the chosen option. Give credit where answers concentrate effort on data meaning and access, where the uncertainty is.

**2.4** Example: name "Grant access by verified relationship"; statement "Access to customer data across divisions is granted only through verified relationships, never through a probabilistic match"; rationale "a false match can disclose one customer's data to another"; implications "matching services must record match status; access policies read only verified links; unresolved matches need a steward process". It would rule out a proposal to give service agents access to every record with a match score above 0.85. Look for principles that can be broken and that change a real decision.

**2.5** Business domain: service coordinator (role, active structure; a common-domain element used here for a business role), handle service request (process, behaviour), field maintenance (service, behaviour), customer (business actor, active structure). Application domain: CRM North and customer link hub (application components, active structure), case management and customer lookup (services, behaviour). Technology domain: shared cloud platform (node, active structure), container hosting (service, behaviour). To add CRM Central, add an application component realising a case-management service for Retail's process, served by container hosting, and served by customer lookup if Retail uses the link hub. The view then shows that both CRMs and the link hub depend on one hosting service and that the link hub serves both divisions: two shared dependencies. In ArchiMate 3.2 the behaviour elements would have been a business process, a business service, two application services and a technology service, and the coordinator a business role.

**2.6** The claim treats framework adoption as the goal. Kotusev's case study of a TOGAF-based practice concluded that none of the TOGAF-specific recommendations proved useful there, and his analysis of 27 organisations found 24 artefacts that proved useful in practice, used for specific purposes. Full adoption would consume effort on unused deliverables. A better measure of progress is the share of significant investment, design and governance decisions that used current architecture information, together with whether those decisions changed as a result. Look for answers that acknowledge the studies' limited number of cases.

## Chapter 4

**4.1** Customer identity resolution: units must recognise shared customers for cross-unit service and reporting (high integration), but maintenance, retail and subscription processes can remain different (low standardisation). The answer should say which facts must agree and which process differences remain legitimate.

**4.2** "Closed" could mean that a technician completed the work, that a retail order was delivered or that a customer confirmed the fix. One shared code without a mapping produces false comparisons of closure times. Either agree a common business definition and change processes to match, or keep distinct events and map each to an explicit reporting concept, preserving the distinctions where no valid equivalence exists.

**4.3** Payroll: low integration, high standardisation (replication-like): the same process and rules in every unit, with little need to share payroll data across units. Customer billing across the three units: high integration if group customers are to receive consolidated invoices, with low standardisation where each unit bills different products (coordination); if units bill separately, low integration. Group cash management: high integration, high standardisation (unification), because the treasury needs a single view and common rules. Retail's warehouse picking does not belong in the grid, because only Retail performs it: the grid describes how units relate to each other, so a single-unit process is a local design decision. Credit justified placements that consider the scope of sharing.

**4.4** Central consolidation is hardest to unwind: Retail's data, process and operating responsibility must be extracted from a common application. Federated coordination needs separable identity mappings, permissions, contracts and platform dependencies, which can remain contractual after the sale through a transition services agreement. Local autonomy simplifies separation but leaves group reporting and shared supplier contracts to untangle. Data rights and transition agreements must be explicit; not every shared service needs physical duplication. Credit a consequence-specific boundary rather than a blanket preference.

**4.5** The claim describes only the strategy-execution perspective. The strategic alignment model also includes perspectives in which IT strategy enables new business strategy (competitive potential) or shapes IT services to the organisation (service level). Helios example: a shared customer identity could enable Digital Services to sell combined maintenance and monitoring contracts, a strategy the group cannot pursue today. Alignment also requires strategic fit between external choices and internal infrastructure, which "doing what the strategy says" ignores.

**4.6** ERP Core, the nightly billing run and the party register belong to the operational backbone: they run core transactions and hold authoritative core data, and they change deliberately. The customer lookup service and the stream of verified-link events belong to the digital platform: they are reusable components that teams can combine quickly into offerings. The link hub itself sits between the two, as a backbone data service exposed through platform interfaces. Building the monitoring contract directly on CRM North's database would couple a fast-changing offering to a backbone system's internal schema: a CRM upgrade could break the offering, the offering could write data that bypasses CRM North's rules and the customer-identity controls, and the backbone's change process would slow the offering down. Full marks go to answers that name the published contract as the boundary.

## Chapter 5

**5.1** €420,000 + €360,000 + €900,000 + €600,000 + €300,000 + €240,000 = €2,820,000. These costs are already inside the €12 million baseline, so adding them would count the same money twice. The remaining €9.18 million cannot be attributed to particular applications without further data.

**5.2** It may support a legally required low-volume process; it may feed an undiscovered batch interface or report; and exit may carry contract penalties or data-retention costs. A replacement could also cost more than continued operation. Investigate dependencies, obligations and lifecycle costs before treating low usage as a reason to retire.

**5.3** Keeping both preserves local fit and avoids migration now, but requires governed contracts and continuing dual support. Consolidation may reduce duplication but introduces process change, migration and concentration risk. The information most likely to change the choice: whether the process differences are real or accidental, the true run costs, the interface dependencies, contract terms and the users' actual work. A pilot should target the most consequential of these uncertainties.

**5.4** Business value = (3 + 2 + 2 + 3) / 4 = 2.5; technical fitness = (4 + 2 + 3 + 2) / 4 = 2.75. Both are below the 3.0 threshold (a score of exactly 3.0 would count as high), so the system falls in Eliminate. The quadrant alone should not decide, because the system may be Retail's only route for picking orders, and fitness at 2.75 is close to the boundary: one criterion moving by a point would place it in Tolerate. Facts to check first: which processes and interfaces depend on it (for example, whether order allocation and delivery scheduling read its data), whether a replacement exists, the contract and exit terms, and whether the low value scores reflect a few users rather than low importance. Good answers treat Eliminate as a prompt for investigation and a retirement plan, never as an instruction.

**5.5** The original deadline is 14 months; the extension moves it to 20 months for €150,000. Without the extension the margins are: rehost 10 months, replatform 6, repurchase 2, and refactor −4 (infeasible). With it they are: rehost 16, replatform 12, repurchase 8 and refactor 2. Only refactoring needs the extension; for repurchase it buys comfort, and replatforming does not need it.

Compare refactor and replatform over the same five years from the decision, at 8%, with one-off costs at the start and running costs at year ends, as in Chapter 10's TCO table. Replatform: €0.60m one-off; year 1 €0.55m (8 months of Legacy Orders at €0.6m a year, €0.40m, plus 4 months of the new platform, €0.15m); years 2 to 5 €0.45m each; present value €2.49m. Refactor: €1.80m plus the €0.15m extension, €1.95m one-off; Legacy Orders keeps running for 18 months, €0.60m in year 1 and €0.30m in year 2, which is €0.50m more legacy cost than replatforming; the refactored system runs for only the last 3.5 years at an unknown cost R a year. Its present value is 1.95 + 0.60 / 1.08 + 0.30 / 1.08² + R × (0.5 / 1.08² + 1 / 1.08³ + 1 / 1.08⁴ + 1 / 1.08⁵) = €2.76m + 2.64 × R. Even with R = 0, refactoring costs €0.27m more in present value than replatforming over five years, and with R = €0.45m it costs €3.95m. Refactoring could only pay off over a longer horizon, through benefits other than running cost, or both, and it would still have only two months' margin. Replatform remains the recommendation, with repurchase plus the extension as the alternative if process change is wanted.

## Chapter 6

**6.1** Example rows: service site (authority: party register for site identity, maintained by party stewards; the CRM may hold local site notes); customer contact person (authority: the CRM of the unit that owns the relationship; local service staff may update it); contract entitlement (authority: ERP Core or the contract system; changed only by contract administration). Reward choices that separate facts which different teams legitimately maintain, and that justify one choice by its consequence.

**6.2** A probabilistic match with a known error rate can be acceptable for estimating, for example, the number of shared customers. Used for authorisation, a single false match can disclose one customer's data to another. The consequence of an error, and therefore the acceptable threshold, is different. Use verified, governed relationships for access decisions and send ambiguous matches to review.

**6.3** The sample rate is 3 / 400 = 7.5 per 1,000, above the contract's limit of 5, so the contract is breached. Applied to 20,000 links, about 20,000 × 0.0075 = 150 links could be false. With only 3 errors in the sample, the estimate is very uncertain: an exact 95% interval for the rate runs from about 1.5 to 21.8 per 1,000, or roughly 30 to 440 false links. The audit should be enlarged, or the next month's sample combined, before drawing firm conclusions.

**6.4** Platform operations pause propagation; the evidence is that the identifier has stopped flowing. The data steward lists affected copies and reports from the lineage records. The group data owner decides the split and signs a decision naming the correct source records. The link-hub team publishes the correction event, and each consumer acknowledges it. Division stewards reconcile the CRMs and the analytics owner rebuilds or annotates reports, recording reconciliation counts. The privacy officer assesses any disclosure. The group data owner closes the case once every consumer confirms completion. Strong workflows preserve history and reach every copy.

**6.5** One master record for every fact would force every system to wait for, and agree with, a single model, even where meanings differ legitimately; it would also concentrate risk and slow local change. A better design assigns authority fact by fact, uses a governed identifier with match status to link records, publishes contracts for shared facts and leaves local facts local.

**6.6** The registry style keeps each CRM authoritative and needs little change in the divisions, which suits federated coordination and a short timetable; its weakness is that every correction still has to be made in the source system. A centralised hub would give one place to maintain customer facts and stronger control, but it forces every division, including the acquired business, to adopt common meanings for those facts and to change its processes and integrations to write to the hub. For Helios the change is justified only if the divisions agree common definitions for most customer facts, the acquired business's site-and-contract model can be expressed in the hub without losing distinctions, and the cost and transition risk fit the funding. A coexistence step, with the hub and the sources synchronised, is a defensible intermediate. Strong answers tie the style to authority and process change rather than to technology preference.

## Chapter 7

**7.1** Example: scope: all production applications. Requirement: logs use the group structure with timestamp, service, correlation identifier and severity, and are sent to the central platform within five minutes. Rationale: incident analysis across services currently takes days because formats differ. Owner: observability platform owner. Release evidence: an automated test shows a request traced across two services. Exceptions: time-limited, approved by the owner, with a dated plan. A strong standard states why it exists and how compliance is shown.

**7.2** Code may run elsewhere but still rely on provider-specific database features. Data portability needs an export with types, keys and relationships intact. Operational portability needs deployment, credentials, backup, monitoring and recovery working in the destination. Commercial portability depends on licences, egress charges and exit terms. Demonstrate a business journey after transfer; a built container or a downloaded file proves only a narrower property.

**7.3** A faulty identity configuration change can block sign-in to every application on the platform. Recovery requirement: a tested rollback of identity configuration within a stated time, protected break-glass administrative access, and a documented degraded procedure for critical workflows. The numerical target should come from the business service's needs and measured recovery capability.

**7.4** Across all ten changes the mean wait is 67 / 10 = 6.7 working days; across the eight that needed approval it is 67 / 8 = 8.4 days. Six of the eight approvals, 75%, concerned single-team data. A first action is to delegate approval of single-team schema changes to the owning team under published rules, keeping central review for shared tables. Success after three months: median wait for single-team changes near zero, no increase in incidents caused by schema changes, and central review time focused on genuinely shared data.

**7.5** Conway's law states that organisations design systems whose structure copies their communication structure. The mirroring study found that, across matched software products, loosely coupled organisations produced more modular designs. Together they justify investigating whether Helios's approval structure is shaping its system coupling. Neither proves that reorganising will improve delivery: the evidence concerns software products in a small sample, organisational and technical changes interact, and Helios's own data must show where delay arises. Reward responses that propose a bounded change with measures.

## Chapter 8

**8.1** Architects and security specialists advise on consequences and controls. The authorised business risk owner decides whether the residual risk is accepted, within delegated limits. A review board may hold delegated rights, but only if that is explicit. At Helios, the platform owner advises and the division's risk owner accepts.

**8.2** The record should name the application, the affected identity functions, why integration cannot happen yet, the observed gaps, the controls actually operating (for example, monthly manual account reconciliation), the owner, the expiry date and the integration milestone. Planned controls, such as automated removal, are listed as conditions that must be shown working before approval. Include triggers for revocation or escalation.

**8.3** Either the standard does not fit a legitimate, common need, or a shared implementation or support problem prevents teams from complying with a sound standard. Compare the use cases, consequences, effort and control results across the three requests. Change the standard only once the pattern is understood; otherwise fix the platform or guidance and keep the requirement.

**8.4** IT principles: anarchy (nobody states them). IT architecture: anarchy for customer data, since nobody owns the identifier. IT infrastructure: IT monarchy (the CIO). Business application needs: feudal (each unit buys its own). Investment: feudal or business monarchy, depending on how budgets are set. The most serious gap is the absence of any decision right over shared customer data, which is exactly where the board's objectives need coordination. A federal arrangement for that domain is the natural fix.

**8.5** A model answer escalates the change of meaning to the group data owner with both business roles represented, and keeps delegated approval for compatible additions. Structural: named owner and escalation route. Procedural: example records, consumer tests, migration window, versioned decision. Relational: service staff who resolve disputed cases join the design discussion. The main paradox is collaboration (integration versus autonomy), with distinction (comparability versus differentiation) also present. Credit work that links each mechanism to a specific failure it prevents, and that treat the two-case study as a source of questions rather than proof.

**8.6** Charging per event discourages teams from publishing events that reconciliation depends on, which undermines correctness. Better units are per connected application with a fixed tier, or a central allocation for core events with charges only for unusually high volumes. Whatever the unit, check that it does not penalise the behaviour the architecture requires.

**8.7** Evaluate: the board assesses the options paper and each funding-gate submission, including the business case and its sensitivity analysis. Direct: it sets the architecture principles, approves the envelope and the gate conditions, and names accountable owners. Monitor: it reviews gate results, the benefits register, overdue exceptions and audit findings each quarter. Engage stakeholders: it hears business-unit leaders, and where relevant key customers, before each gate. A suitable design factor is the I&T-related issues factor, because inconsistent customer data across acquired systems is a known current problem; the risk profile (acquired applications and personal data) and compliance requirements are also defensible. The best answers link the factor to a Helios fact.

## Chapter 9

**9.1** Risk: because account removal does not reach acquired applications, a former employee may sign in after leaving, causing unauthorised disclosure of customer data. Control 1: lifecycle events reach every application, with coverage monitoring; evidence: a reconciled list of applications and recorded removal events. Control 2: automated denied-access tests for removed accounts; evidence: test results naming the identity, resource, configuration and date. A policy document alone is not evidence that either control works.

**9.2** They can share an identity provider, a DNS configuration, an administrator account, a software release pipeline or a supplier's control plane. A failure in any of these can affect all three at once, so separate hosting does not guarantee independence.

**9.3** Availability is 0.998⁴ × 0.999 = 0.991, so the service is unavailable about 0.9% of the year, or roughly 79 hours. If failures are correlated, for example if two applications share an administrator account or release, the simple product understates the risk of simultaneous outages and overstates confidence in the figure; the shared elements need their own analysis and recovery tests.

**9.4** Which service and failure scenarios does the objective cover? When does the clock start and stop? What does "recovered" mean, and what data-loss objective applies? What evidence supports the figure? Which steps remain Helios's own: detection, the decision to resume, access restoration, integration catch-up and reconciliation? What configuration duties and exclusions apply to Helios as the customer?

**9.5** A certificate shows that the supplier's management system met a standard within a stated scope at audit time. It does not cover Helios's configuration, identity integration, data flows or application code. Request the certificate's scope, the supplier's relevant control reports, and Helios's own results: configuration review, access tests, a restore test and a review of data flows for the new service.

**9.6** Today each acquired application trusts anyone who holds a valid local account, so a leaver whose local account was not removed can still sign in. In a zero trust architecture the application grants no implicit trust: every access request is evaluated per session against current policy, including the person's status in the workforce identity service and the state of their device. The policy engine decides, consulting the workforce identity service and device information; the policy administrator opens or refuses the session; the policy enforcement point, for example an access gateway or proxy in front of the acquired CRM, carries out the decision. A leaver is refused at the enforcement point even if the CRM's own account list is stale. Look for responses that recognise zero trust as a target direction that needs integration work, with interim controls meanwhile.

## Chapter 10

**10.1** Investment €2.5m at time zero; benefits at the end of years 1 to 3. At 8%: −2,500,000 + 1,200,000 / 1.08 + 1,200,000 / 1.08² + 1,200,000 / 1.08³ = −2,500,000 + 1,111,111 + 1,028,807 + 952,599 = €592,516. With €960,000 a year: −2,500,000 + 888,889 + 823,045 + 762,079 = −€25,987. The investment is unchanged while the discounted benefits fall by 20%, from €3.09m to €2.47m, which is just below the €2.5m outlay, so the sign changes. The break-even benefit is €970,084 a year, 19.2% below the estimate.

**10.2** Record the €200,000 reduction in contractor invoices once, as a cash benefit, if the reduction is achievable. The released hours are the mechanism behind that saving and cannot be counted as a second €200,000. If some hours are redeployed rather than removed from invoices, split the population: the removed hours are cash, and the redeployed hours are capacity, with any further value supported separately.

**10.3** The real rate is 1.08 / 1.03 − 1 = 4.85%. Discounting €1.2m a year in today's prices at 4.85% gives present-value benefits of €3,276,836 and an NPV of about €776,836. The same answer follows from inflating the benefits by 3% a year and discounting at 8%. Discounting the constant-price benefits at 8% would understate the NPV by about €184,000.

**10.4** The gross value of the pilot policy is 0.34. With a cost of 0.16 its net value is 0.18, below the 0.20 of expanding now, so the pilot is not worth running; the break-even cost is 0.34 − 0.20 = 0.14. If the result cannot change the decision because expansion is already committed, the information is worth nothing: the policy is worth 0.20 minus the pilot's cost, unless the pilot creates separately specified learning or implementation value.

**10.5** Fund a migration rehearsal on representative data and the riskiest interface, with a fixed budget and a four-week limit. Collect reconciliation defects, throughput, specialist effort and rollback feasibility, then re-estimate the migration cost. Decision rule: proceed if the re-estimated cost keeps the NPV positive at the agreed discount rate and all continuity conditions are met; narrow the scope if only part of the migration meets the rule; stop if the rehearsal reveals data losses that cannot be fixed within the budget. Credit rules set before the rehearsal.

**10.6** The IRR of 20.71% is well above the 8% hurdle, but the argument ignores how the value depends on assumptions. The break-even benefit is €0.97m a year, 19.2% below the estimate, so a 20% shortfall turns the NPV to −€25,987. IRR also says nothing about scale or about what happens if the budget changes. If €0.6m of integration work is deferred, the NPV falls to €407,886; if it is removed and the service-credit benefit is lost, the NPV falls to €161,678 and the case becomes fragile. Approval should rest on confidence in the size and timing of the benefits, with the integration funding protected or a staged commitment tied to evidence of benefits, rather than on the IRR alone.

**10.7** The listing books each year's €1.2m benefit at the end of the year, so cumulative benefits pass €2.5m only when year 3's cash arrives: payback is 3 years. The analyst assumed benefits accrue evenly within each year and interpolated: 2 + (2.5 − 2.4) / 1.2 = 2.08 years. Both are arithmetically right under their conventions. The board should see 3 years, the figure consistent with the NPV model, or both figures clearly labelled; mixing an interpolated payback with a year-end NPV makes the case look faster than the model behind it.

**10.8** With an investment of €3.0m and the same benefits, the NPV is −3,000,000 + 3,092,516 = €92,516, and the IRR falls to 9.70%. The case then looks marginal. Charging the workforce identity work to it mixes two different mechanisms: the €0.5m workforce package delivers a risk benefit (former staff lose access) that has nothing to do with handoffs. Keeping them apart lets the board judge the handoff case on its own cash flows and the access case on its risk reduction, and stops either being approved or rejected for the other's reasons.

## Chapter 11

**11.1** Forward pass: A finishes at week 4, B at 10, C at 7, D at 14 and E at 16. Backward pass: C may finish as late as week 10, so it has 3 weeks of slack; A, B, D and E have none, giving the critical path A–B–D–E. If one indivisible team must do B and then C, C runs from week 10 to 13, D from 13 to 17 and E from 17 to 19, so completion moves to week 19.

**11.2** In State 2, each division's CRM remains authoritative for its own customer records; the customer link hub is authoritative for verified cross-system links; only party stewards may create or reject links; consumers read verified links only. Retirement evidence for the spreadsheets: every consumer migrated to the link hub, records reconciled, retained history accessible, no remaining dependent reports and a named operational owner for the link service.

**11.3** One alternative keeps legacy modernisation at €2.4m (because several legacy systems carry security risk) and instead removes the €0.9m analytics package, reduces the CRM pilot to €0.6m and reduces contingency to €0.6m, saving 0.9 + 0.6 + 0.3 = €1.8m. **11.5** The line names no outcome, no intermediate states and no conditions. A better set of entries runs as follows. ## Chapter 14

**14.1** Before: A 10 / 100 = 10%, B 45 / 900 = 5%, pooled 55 / 1,000 = 5.5%. After: A 72 / 900 = 8%, B 4 / 100 = 4%, pooled 76 / 1,000 = 7.6%. Both units improve, but the higher-rate unit's share of demand rises from 10% to 90%, so the pooled rate rises. Standardised to the earlier mix, the later rate is 4.4%. Show the board both: 7.6% is what customers experienced, and 4.4% isolates the change in unit performance.

**14.2** Name: unresolved customer-identity links. Definition: proposed cross-system links awaiting a steward's decision, counted daily at 06:00, with their age in working days. Population: links in the customer link hub for participating divisions. Exclusions: links withdrawn by the source system. Owner: group data owner. Decision: if more than 200 links are older than five working days for two consecutive weeks, add stewardship capacity or pause rollout to new divisions; set the threshold from business consequence and observed demand.

**14.3** The improvement is real if the measure was stable, but the architecture's contribution is not isolated. Compare similar workflows with different rollout timing, stratify by staffing level and case mix, examine reassignment causes and review incidents and interviews. State the remaining confounding and avoid crediting the whole improvement to either staffing or architecture.

**14.4** The pilot changes by 8 − 12 = −4 percentage points and the comparison by 8 − 9 = −1, so the difference-in-differences is −3 percentage points. Three threats: the pilot was chosen after an unusually bad quarter at 12%, so some improvement is regression to the mean; staffing or process changed in the pilot division at the same time; the comparison division was partly exposed to the new service. Sample sizes and dependence are also needed before stating any uncertainty.

**14.5** Confidence: a defined before-and-after survey of relevant roles. Adaptability: a rehearsed change, such as adding a service-site relationship, with effort, dependencies and defects recorded. Cash savings: realised incremental cash flows net of replacement and transition costs. Rival explanations: better leadership that improves both; reverse influence from better-performing divisions; shared optimism among respondents. Supportable conclusion: "Respondents report greater confidence in changing systems than before the programme." It cannot establish adaptability or savings.

**14.6** The research does not show that EA improves performance in general. Tamm and colleagues offer a conceptual model; Lange and colleagues and van de Wetering and colleagues report associations in cross-sectional, perception-based surveys; Kotusev's case studies show practitioners relying on artefacts used in planning and decisions. Together they suggest that value depends on embedding architecture in real decisions, which tells Helios what to measure rather than what it will gain. Whether the programme pays for itself depends on Helios's own cash flows, measured as in Chapter 10.

## Chapter 16

**16.1** Tamm et al. built a conceptual model: it supports a proposed mechanism but cannot estimate an effect size. March used a formal model: it shows the consequences of assumptions without showing that they hold. MacCormack et al. used comparative measurement: it supports a relationship across matched products rather than a general law. Lange et al. ran a survey: it supports associations among perceived constructs, not causal direction. Jöhnk et al. studied qualitative cases: they explain how mechanisms work in context, not how common they are.

**16.2** The argument moves from association to causation, does not say which part of maturity matters, assumes a tool changes practice, ignores reverse influence (profitable firms invest more in architecture) and ignores differences between the studied organisations and Helios. The missing chain links the tool to changed decisions, those decisions to operational changes, and those changes to measured financial outcomes.

**16.3** A strong synthesis identifies three different questions (what to share, how organisational and technical boundaries interact, and how standards affect learning), bounds each source accurately, applies them to one coherent Helios option and gives a credible counterargument. It does not treat a stylised model, a small comparative sample and a practitioner framework as equivalent proof. Mark for traceability and handling of assumptions, not citation count.

**16.4** For example, compare the van de Wetering survey (299 CIOs and IT managers reporting on their organisations, perceptions at one time) with the Dang and Pekkola case (one public-sector organisation, process over time). Neither isolates the effect of Helios's intervention. A conditional decision: fund a bounded stewardship pilot while collecting workflow and outcome data. Overturning observation: no change in identity-related reassignment, or an unsustainable exception workload. Good answers distinguish the studies' observations from the learner's proposal.

**16.5** Question: which factors and measures characterise EA management success. Constructs: EA product, infrastructure and service quality, organisational anchoring, satisfaction, intention to use and benefits. Method: a model built from literature and interviews, tested with a cross-sectional survey. Sample: 133 practitioners. Results: four success factors were supported; organisational anchoring mediated the effects of infrastructure and service quality on the success measures; satisfaction was irrelevant. Limits: perceptions, one point in time, practitioner respondents. Transfer: Helios should measure whether architecture is anchored in its decision processes, such as investment reviews and design approvals, and whether people intend to use it, rather than how satisfied they are with it.
