# 9 Enterprise risk, security and assurance

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Map a critical business service to its technical, supplier and human dependencies.
- Write a risk statement with a condition, an event and a consequence, and derive controls from it.
- Explain the principles and logical components of a zero trust architecture and apply them to an access risk.
- Calculate the effect of serial and shared dependencies on service availability.
- Design an assurance plan that links each claim to current evidence.

## Begin with the service the organisation must preserve

Helios must keep urgent maintenance running while it changes the systems underneath. This chapter shows how to find what that service depends on, how to state its risks and how to prove that the controls work.

A critical business service may depend on several applications, identity systems, networks, data stores, suppliers and people. Assess the combined service rather than treating each application as a separate entry in a risk register. Helios's urgent maintenance service, for example, cannot schedule work if entitlement checking, technician sign-in through the workforce identity service or access to ERP Core fails, even when the customer portal is running normally. {FIG:risk} shows the dependencies.

Map each dependency and its consequence. Ask three questions:

- Which functions can continue in a degraded mode, and for how long?
- Which information must remain trustworthy during the disruption?
- Who can authorise a switch to manual operation?

A fallback spreadsheet may carry a short interruption, but it creates reconciliation, access and recovery work of its own. Plan that work in advance.

## Frameworks organise the work

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 groups cybersecurity outcomes under six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover {REF:csf}. The Govern function, new in version 2.0, connects cybersecurity strategy, roles, policy and oversight to the organisation's enterprise risk management. The framework describes outcomes; each organisation chooses the controls and evidence that achieve them in its context. More generally, ISO 31000 defines **risk** as the effect of uncertainty on objectives, which keeps attention on what the organisation is trying to achieve rather than on a list of threats {REF:iso31000}.

## Write risk statements, then derive controls

A useful **risk statement** names a condition, an event and a consequence. For example: because account removal does not reach the acquired applications (the condition), a former employee may sign in after leaving (the event), causing unauthorised disclosure of customer service data (the consequence).

The controls follow from the mechanism:

- an authoritative lifecycle event that reaches every application;
- monitoring of which applications receive those events;
- an automated test that a removed account is refused;
- an exception process for applications that cannot yet integrate.

"Use encryption" would leave this risk untouched, because an authorised application account can still decrypt the data. Control selection needs a threat and an access path.

### Zero trust as a design response

The same risk shows why many organisations are moving towards **zero trust**. NIST describes zero trust as a set of principles that move defences from static, network-based perimeters to users, assets and resources {REF:zerotrust}. A zero trust architecture grants no implicit trust because of where a device sits on the network or who owns it. Its tenets include treating every data source and service as a resource, securing all communication wherever it travels, granting access to each resource per session with the least privilege needed, and deciding access by dynamic policy that considers the identity of the user or service, the state of the requesting device and other attributes. NIST separates the decision from its enforcement. A **policy engine** decides whether a subject may use a resource, a **policy administrator** opens or closes the session accordingly, and a **policy enforcement point** in front of the resource carries out the decision.

The link to Helios's risk is direct. The acquired applications keep local accounts, so each one trusts whoever holds a valid local password, whether or not that person still works for Helios. Under zero trust, every sign-in to those applications would pass through an enforcement point that consults the policy engine, and the engine would check the workforce identity service, where the leaver's account is already disabled. Access would fail even if the application's own account list were out of date. Zero trust is a direction for the target architecture rather than a product to buy. Helios can move towards it application by application, starting with those that hold customer service data, while the lifecycle events and denial tests above close the gap in the meantime.

Be careful with numerical risk scores. A score of "likelihood 3 × impact 4 = 12" looks precise but is the product of two judgements. Where likelihood is uncertain, show the supporting data, a range or a qualitative judgement. A severe but poorly quantified risk may still deserve action.

## Shared dependencies and availability

Availability figures combine in ways that surprise people. Suppose the urgent maintenance service needs three applications, each available 99.9% of the time, and that their failures are independent. The service is available only when all three are, so its availability is 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 = 0.997, about 26 hours of downtime a year.

Now add the workforce identity platform, which all three applications need for staff sign-in and which is available 99.5% of the time. The service's availability falls to 0.997 × 0.995 = 0.992, about 70 hours a year. The workforce identity platform alone contributes about 44 hours. A single shared component can matter more than all the applications together.

The calculation also rests on an assumption that is often false: independence. Applications hosted in different environments can still share an identity provider, a DNS configuration, an administrator account, a software release or a supplier's control plane. When failures are correlated, multiplying separate availability estimates understates the risk. Identify those shared elements and test their recovery directly.

## Assurance follows claims through evidence

**Assurance** is the justified confidence that controls work as intended. For each important claim, identify the requirement, the control, who implements it and the evidence that it works.

| Claim | Control | Evidence | Scope and date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Removed staff cannot sign in | Lifecycle events to all applications | Automated denial test results | Production, each release |
| Privileged access is reviewed | Quarterly access review | Signed review records and removals made | All admin accounts, last quarter |
| Customer data can be recovered | Daily backups with restore tests | Restore test report with record counts | Production database, last month |

Evidence that privileged access is reviewed is a signed review record and a list of removals; a policy stating that reviews happen is only an intention. A test run in a different environment supports a claim about that environment. Good evidence states its scope, date, configuration and exceptions.

Different kinds of evidence answer different questions: design reviews, configuration inspections, automated tests, restore exercises, supplier reports and operating observations. A certification or audit report is useful within its scope, but it rarely covers the customer's own configuration or every service used.

## Supplier dependencies

Supplier assessment should cover the service contract, data use, subcontractors, incident communication, recovery evidence, export and exit. A supplier's promised recovery time usually refers to its infrastructure rather than to Helios's complete business service.

For example, a supplier advertises a 15-minute recovery time for its hosting platform. The end-to-end recovery of Helios's service also includes its own steps:

| Step | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Helios detects the outage and declares an incident | 20 |
| Supplier restores the infrastructure | 15 |
| Helios decides to resume and restores user access | 45 |
| Integrations catch up on queued messages | 60 |
| Staff reconcile records changed during the outage | 120 |
| **Total** | **260** |

The business service takes about 4 hours 20 minutes to recover, and the supplier's 15 minutes is the smallest step. Most of the time belongs to Helios. Design the response to supplier failure before signing a long-term dependency: tested export, alternative operating procedures, recoverable copies held under policy, and a realistic transition plan. A second supplier helps only if Helios can actually switch the business responsibility safely.

## A first-year assurance plan

Helios's first-year plan focuses on four areas: workforce identity lifecycle coverage, recovery of critical service data, reliable handoffs and incident ownership. It begins with a baseline of known gaps and links each remediation to a roadmap milestone. Working controls with current evidence make the programme compliant; adopting a framework organises the work.

For each release, keep evidence that critical access and recovery controls work in the deployed configuration. Reassess when an acquisition, a new data use or a shared-platform change alters the risk.

> **In practice.** Ask for the most recent restore test rather than the backup policy. A dated report with record counts answers the question the board actually cares about: could we get the data back?

9.2 Explain why three applications hosted in different environments can still share a common failure mode. Give two examples.

9.3 A service needs four applications, each 99.8% available, and a shared network component that is 99.9% available. Assuming independent failures, calculate the service's availability and annual downtime in hours. Then explain how correlated failures would change your answer.

## Key points

- Analyse risk at the level of the business service, following its dependencies across applications, shared platforms, people and suppliers.
- A risk statement names a condition, an event and a consequence; controls follow from the mechanism. Zero trust removes implicit trust and checks every access against current identity and policy.
- Serial dependencies multiply, and shared components can dominate a service's downtime. Correlated failures make simple multiplication optimistic.
- Assurance links each claim to a control and to current evidence with a stated scope and date.
- Supplier recovery times cover the supplier's part. Plan and measure the organisation's own recovery steps as well.
