# 12 Organisational change, adoption and architecture leadership

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain how responsibilities and incentives determine whether an architecture is adopted.
- Compare the Lewin, Kotter and ADKAR change models and critique the evidence behind them.
- Design an adoption plan with observable stages and measures.
- Facilitate a decision among stakeholders whose objectives differ for legitimate reasons.

## Deployment is only one transition

A shared customer identifier is useful only if people and applications use it correctly. Local staff must know how to create, correct and dispute links. Support teams must know who resolves an ambiguous match. Reports must say which definition they use. Each of these changes in daily work needs an owner, practice and time.

Map the affected work before planning any training. For each role, record the current task, the proposed task, the decision authority, the information needed and the route for exceptions. If a new process adds three approval steps to a service agent's day, reluctance may reveal a poor process rather than a lack of commitment. Treat what people actually do as information about the design.

Helios's units also have different customer relationships. Industrial Maintenance works with asset owners and service sites, Retail Distribution with buyers and delivery addresses, and Digital Services with subscription administrators and end users. A single field labelled "customer" can hide several roles, and adoption planning must keep the distinctions that make local work valid.

## Three models of change

Architects borrow three models of organisational change more than any others. Each is useful for a different purpose, and each has a weaker evidence base than its popularity suggests.

### Lewin: unfreeze, change, refreeze

Kurt Lewin described planned change in groups as moving from one stable level of behaviour to another: unfreezing the present level, moving to a new level, and freezing group life at the new level {REF:lewin}. The idea is that behaviour is held in place by social forces, so change needs those forces to be loosened first and re-established afterwards.

Cummings, Bridgman and Brown show that the familiar "three-step model" was assembled after Lewin's death by later writers and textbooks; comparing those accounts with what Lewin actually wrote, they conclude that he never developed such a model {REF:lewinrethink}. The model's lasting lesson is about the old way of working. If the shared spreadsheets that staff used for cross-division matching remain available, work drifts back to them. Retiring the old route is part of the change.

### Kotter's eight steps

John Kotter's 1995 article analysed why transformation efforts fail and turned the common errors into eight steps {REF:kotter}:

1. Establish a sense of urgency.
2. Form a powerful guiding coalition.
3. Create a vision.
4. Communicate the vision.
5. Empower others to act on the vision.
6. Plan for and create short-term wins.
7. Consolidate improvements and produce still more change.
8. Institutionalise new approaches.

A review by Appelbaum and colleagues found research support for most individual steps but no formal study of the model as a whole, and concluded that its popularity comes more from its direct, usable format than from scientific consensus {REF:kotterreview}. The model is also linear and top-down, whereas architecture change often proceeds in parallel streams and is reshaped by what local teams discover.

### ADKAR

Jeffrey Hiatt's ADKAR model looks at change from the individual's point of view {REF:adkar}. It holds that a person adopts a change by achieving five outcomes in order: **Awareness** of why the change is needed, **Desire** to take part, **Knowledge** of how to change, **Ability** to do it in practice, and **Reinforcement** that sustains it. The table applies the elements to a Helios service agent who is asked to use shared customer identifiers.

| Element | What it means for the agent | Signal that it is missing | Typical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Awareness | Knows why shared identifiers are being introduced | "Nobody told us why we have to do this" | Show the misrouting problem with the agent's own cases |
| Desire | Wants to take part | Keeps the old spreadsheet despite knowing the reason | Address what the agent loses, such as time or control |
| Knowledge | Knows how to search, verify and dispute a link | Errors in training exercises | Training, guides and worked examples |
| Ability | Can do it in real work, at normal speed, with the real screens | Correct in training, wrong under time pressure | Coaching, practice and design changes |
| Reinforcement | Keeps doing it after the project team leaves | Drift back to the old route | Measures, recognition and retiring the old route |

The model is a diagnostic. Hiatt suggests assessing each element and working on the first one that is weak, which he calls the barrier point; effort spent on later elements is wasted while an earlier one is missing. The distinction architects most often need is between Knowledge and Ability. People can know exactly what to do and still be unable to do it, because the screen, the data or the time available does not allow it. The model comes from a change-management consultancy and rests mainly on practitioner experience, so use it to structure questions rather than as a tested theory.

### How architects use the models

| Model and level | Architectural use | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Lewin: the group | Plan the removal of old routes so that work does not drift back | Implies a stable end state; the "model" is a later simplification |
| Kotter: the organisation | Sponsorship, a cross-unit coalition, pilot milestones as short-term wins | Linear and top-down; limited evidence for the whole sequence |
| ADKAR: the individual | Diagnose why a particular role has not adopted the change | Can treat structural problems as personal ones |

For example, a Helios service agent may know the new matching procedure (Knowledge) but be unable to choose the right service site because the screen does not show sites at all (Ability). ADKAR locates the gap; the fix is a design change rather than more training. That is the general lesson: use the models to ask better questions, and expect many answers to be about the architecture.

## Incentives can oppose the enterprise objective

Suppose the central platform team is measured on the number of applications migrated, while business units are measured on uninterrupted service and local delivery speed. The platform team pushes early migration; local managers delay it. Both responses are rational under their incentives. An enterprise decision needs a shared outcome and an agreed way to bear the cost of transition.

Make participation concrete. Allocate time for local specialists, include them in acceptance criteria, and fund the support responsibility after go-live. A programme that depends on unpaid local effort has hidden costs and an unreliable plan. Record disagreements about priorities as disagreements, rather than disguising them as technical incompatibility.

## Plan adoption as an observable progression

Plan adoption in four stages, each with its own evidence:

1. **Readiness.** Support staff are trained and pilot data is reconciled.
2. **Supervised use.** Real work runs on the new route with the project team close by, revealing confusing tasks and exceptions.
3. **Independent use.** Normal work continues without the project team.
4. **Sustained operation.** Performance holds over an agreed period and recurring problems have owners.

Choose measures that follow the mechanism. Training attendance is an **input**. Correct use of the identifier in eligible workflows is a **behaviour**. Fewer misrouted service requests is an **outcome**. Staff can log in often and still keep a shadow spreadsheet, so observe the work and find out why parallel processes persist.

Compliance and understanding are different things. A mandatory field can force users to enter data while lowering its quality. Make the correct action easy and the exception visible. When staff cannot resolve a customer match, a governed "pending" state is more accurate than a forced false match.

## Facilitate a consequential disagreement

Prepare a decision brief with the problem, mandatory conditions, options, supporting data and unresolved questions. During the meeting, separate facts from preferences, and authority from expertise. An experienced engineer may understand a failure mode; a business owner may be accountable for accepting its consequences. Both contributions matter, but they are different roles.

In a disagreement over group identifiers, for example, ask each stakeholder which consequence they are trying to prevent. Maintenance may fear incorrect entitlement, analytics may need consistent grouping, and the platform team may need a stable contract. A design that separates legal party, service site and reporting group may resolve all three concerns better than a debate about which team owns the word "customer".

Record the decision, any material dissent, the conditions, the owner and the review date. A clear record lets someone who missed the meeting understand what is authorised, and it stops the same question being reopened without new information.

## Research: adoption unfolds across levels

Dang and Pekkola studied enterprise architecture adoption in a public-sector organisation, using a qualitative case study and a process view of organisational change {REF:change}. They found that the change was both sociotechnical and punctuated: it moved in bursts rather than smoothly, and it oscillated between different organisational levels as direction from the top met implementation below. A single case explains a process in its setting, which is useful for asking questions and weak for predicting a rollout sequence or EA's benefit. For Helios it suggests a question: when local operating experience contradicts the authorised target, who can revise the target, and on what basis?

### Worked adoption episode

The board approves a shared identifier, but the first Maintenance pilot reveals that staff choose the legal customer when the task needs a service site. The acceptance test checked that an identifier was present but never checked that the selected entity could legitimately receive the service. Staff start a spreadsheet to keep track of sites. The spreadsheet is a signal to investigate rather than proof of resistance.

{FIG:adoption_feedback} shows two routes for the response. Local support can clarify labels and improve training within the current contract. A missing service-site concept, however, changes shared meaning and must go to the data owner and the consumer teams. The revised contract then returns through a controlled release and another operating trial. Training cannot repair a contract that cannot express the required fact, and a contract change cannot ensure that staff know which fact to enter.

FIGURE adoption_feedback | Operating experience can revise a local workflow, the enterprise contract, or both, before rollout expands.

| Response | Rationale to test | What must be true before expansion |
|---|---|---|
| Enforce the current form | The contract already expresses sites; staff need better guidance | Staff resolve representative ambiguous cases without project help |
| Revise the shared contract | Site and party distinctions are missing or ambiguous | Agreed meanings, reconciled example records, consumer tests, authorised migration |
| Narrow the rollout | The current model fits Retail but not Maintenance | A clear eligibility boundary, a funded interim workflow, a dated decision for Maintenance |

Helios chooses to revise the contract and narrow the rollout. Retail continues within its demonstrated scope, and Maintenance is not reported as migrated. A business data owner resolves the meanings, a product team implements the change, and an operating steward handles ambiguous records; the sponsor funds their time and accepts the revised schedule.

Collect observations that could overturn the choice. If representative records show that the contract already expresses site relationships correctly, the problem lies in the form or the training. If correct site selection still produces invalid entitlement, examine the entitlement service. If only unusually skilled pilot users succeed, repeat the trial with the intended operating team and normal support.

## Leadership includes changing a recommendation

An architect should say so when new information weakens an earlier position. If the pilot shows that consolidated workflows slow urgent service work, revise the scope or the design, keep what was learned, and identify the next question that would distinguish between the options. Do not expand a programme simply because a pilot was popular; check whether its users, data, dependencies and support conditions represent the next rollout group.

## Key points

- Architecture changes people's work. Adoption needs usable processes, aligned incentives, funded support and evidence that the new way works without the project team.
- Lewin, Kotter and ADKAR operate at group, organisation and individual levels. They are useful prompts with limited empirical support, and many of the gaps they reveal are design problems.
- Plan adoption in observable stages, and measure inputs, behaviours and outcomes separately.
- Facilitation separates facts from preferences and authority from expertise, and records decisions and dissent.
- Good leadership revises a recommendation when the operating experience contradicts it.
