# 12 Modernisation studio brief and discovery

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Design a discovery plan that finds every writer to a business object, with a confidence level for each.
- Compare direct replacement, phased migration and temporary rehosting against a fixed deadline.
- Explain how the strangler fig approach applies when some writers bypass the routing seam.
- Evaluate a partner interface change as a contract negotiation with technical, commercial and timing terms.

## Orion has a deadline and an incomplete inventory

> **Building on Book 1.** Book 1 introduced Orion's migration as a sequence of operable transition states, with a strangler fig routing seam, a write fence and a latest safe decision point. This studio works from measurements instead: a writer inventory built from audit logs, replication lag per table, dual-run results, three timed rehearsals and a partner delay, and it enforces the write fence inside the new database.

Orion sells industrial supplies to business customers. Its order management runs on an ageing platform whose hosting agreement ends in nine months. The studio's task is to move order intake and order management to a new order service, with its own PostgreSQL order store, before that date. Warehouse execution stays with Orion's warehouse partner. The brief gives these facts:

- the legacy database holds 12 million historical orders;
- about 45,000 new orders arrive each day, and the order tables see about 1.5 million row changes a day;
- order intake may pause for at most 45 minutes, in a Sunday window from 02:00 to 02:45, and customers must still be able to look up order status during the pause;
- the new service supports two order states that the legacy platform cannot represent: "partially dispatched" and "awaiting customs";
- the warehouse partner writes status updates directly into the legacy order tables through its own database account.

The migration must keep every accepted order and never dispatch an order twice. The business may postpone the two new states if the architect shows why that reduces risk.

## Find every writer

A migration fails most dangerously through a writer nobody knew about. Inventory every path that can create or change an order: application interfaces, batch jobs and file imports, database accounts used by partners and other teams, and support scripts. Combine owner interviews, scheduler entries, database account lists and observed activity. Orion enabled database audit logging on the order tables for four weeks, deliberately including a month end; for example, a one-week trace would have missed the finance job, which runs only at month end.

The audit produced this inventory, with a confidence level for each entry:

| Writer | Evidence | Confidence | Planned action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legacy application | Code, configuration and audit log | Confirmed | Replaced by the new order service |
| Marketplace import job | Scheduler entry and audit log | Confirmed | Rebuilt as a client of the new order API |
| Partner account `wh_partner` | Audit log shows updates between 06:00 and 22:00 | Confirmed | Moved to a new status API by contract change |
| Finance month-end adjustment | Owner interview and one observed run | Observed once | Observed again at the next month end, then rebuilt |
| Account `ops_batch` | Audit log shows 212 status updates at 00:30 | Observed, owner unknown | Investigated |

The last row is what discovery exists for. Investigation traced `ops_batch` to a script, written years earlier by the support team, that closes orders with no activity for 90 days. Nobody had listed it, because nobody thought of it as part of order management. It must become a function of the new service or be retired by decision; either way, it cannot keep writing to the legacy tables after the move.

## Compare the three strategies

**Direct replacement** copies the data and switches all traffic at one moment. It keeps coexistence short but concentrates every kind of change into one event, and the 45-minute window rules out a bulk copy of 12 million orders inside it.

**Phased migration** keeps the legacy platform authoritative while change data capture fills the new store, runs the two systems side by side, and then transfers write authority in a short, rehearsed cutover. Each step is smaller, but the intermediate states must be operated.

**Temporary rehosting** moves the legacy platform unchanged to new hosting for, say, twelve months. It removes the deadline risk but keeps the technical debt and adds a second migration later.

Martin Fowler coined the strangler fig image in 2004: gradually create a new system around the edges of the old one until the old one can be removed {REF:fowlerstrangler}, and his 2024 description adds the activities around it: understanding the desired outcomes, breaking the problem into parts, delivering those parts, and changing the organisation, with transitional architecture that lets old and new coexist {REF:fowlerstrangler2024}. Microsoft's pattern guidance describes the same idea with a routing façade that sends each request to the old or the new implementation {REF:strangler}. In Orion, the natural seam is the order API used by the web shop and the call centre. The seam controls only the traffic that passes through it, however, and the partner account, the finance job and the support script all write to the tables directly. They must be brought behind the seam, or fenced off, before the new service can own the orders.

A sound first recommendation is phased migration, with temporary rehosting as a fallback whose decision date is the end of month six, after which rehosting would no longer fit before the deadline.

## The partner contract is part of the architecture

The partner's direct database writes cannot continue after the move, so the interface must change, and that is a negotiation as much as a design task. Orion's proposal covers six terms:

- **Interface:** the partner sends each status change to a new HTTPS status API, with a unique event ID that makes a resent event harmless.
- **Service level:** status events within 15 minutes of the warehouse scan, and resends with the same event ID for up to 24 hours.
- **Authority:** every dispatch command from Orion carries an authority epoch, and the partner rejects commands with an older epoch than the latest it has seen.
- **Schedule:** ten weeks of partner development and two weeks of joint testing, including one joint cutover rehearsal.
- **Cost:** a one-off charge of €18,000 quoted by the partner.
- **Fallback:** if the API is unavailable, the partner delivers a status file every 15 minutes, which Orion converts into API calls.

The authority term protects the partner's side, where Orion's own controls cannot reach: after cutover, the legacy platform must never send a dispatch command that the partner accepts. Chapter 13 develops the same mechanism inside Orion's new store. The fallback term decouples Orion's timeline from the partner's. Orion estimates 15 engineer-days to build the file adapter, an option that Chapter 14 will need.

## Your first submission

Produce:

1. a dependency map of the current estate, with a confidence level for each writer and reader;
2. a comparison of the three strategies against the nine-month deadline;
3. a write-authority table showing who may write orders in each phase of your plan;
4. the earliest step that can be reversed easily, and the first step after which recovery must preserve new business data;
5. your negotiating position for the partner contract, with the terms you would give up first.

Treat the deadline as a constraint to analyse, not as proof that the plan fits. If the evidence does not support replacement in time, propose the fallback, its cost and the date by which it must be chosen.

## Exercises

12.2 Compare temporary rehosting with phased migration under the nine-month deadline. State what evidence would favour each.

12.3 Design a rule that keeps each in-flight order under one write authority during a phased migration in which orders move in cohorts.

12.4 The audit finds 212 unexplained updates from account `ops_batch`. Write the investigation steps and the two possible outcomes, with the design consequence of each.

## Key points

- Discovery must find every writer, including partner accounts, scheduled jobs and support scripts, and record how strong the evidence is for each.
- Observation windows must cover infrequent work such as month-end jobs.
- Phased migration with change data capture keeps one authority while reducing the size of each step.
- A strangler seam controls only the traffic that passes through it, so direct writers must be moved behind it or fenced.
- A partner interface change is a negotiated contract with technical, timing, cost and fallback terms.
