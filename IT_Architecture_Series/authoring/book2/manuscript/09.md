# 9 Data studio brief and semantic contracts

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Define a metric contract with its population, event boundaries, time zone, exclusions and version.
- Calculate combined measures correctly from groups of different sizes.
- Design a grain-and-key table that prevents double counting when facts are joined.
- Explain event time, processing time and publication time, and how windows, watermarks and triggers handle late data.

## Beacon must explain its numbers

> **Building on Book 1.** Book 1 built Beacon's first platform: metric definitions, a declared grain, event time, windows, watermarks and triggers, and a daily report that is revised when late events arrive. This studio scales Beacon to six sources and three reporting products, asks how a reader can trust a number that has been restated, and tests the design against a regulator's deadline for corrections.

Beacon Analytics combines records from six operational systems and publishes a daily service dashboard at 07:00 in the business time zone. About ten million new or changed records arrive each day. Three sources provide ordered change streams with positions, two provide daily extracts, and one sends events that may arrive up to seven days late. Older corrections are allowed through an approved exception process.

Three groups use the data differently. Managers compare service completion time by division and customer segment. Finance wants a monthly snapshot that it can reproduce exactly later. Operations wants to see changes during the day. These are three reporting products, even if they share ingestion infrastructure, and a single table marked "latest" cannot serve them all.

The team proposes a streaming lakehouse and claims that real-time ingestion will make the figures trustworthy. Your task is to evaluate that claim, design two feasible alternatives and state what a reader may conclude from each published version.

## Define the question before the pipeline

A **metric contract** fixes what a measure means, so that a change in meaning can be told apart from a change in the business:

| Element | Definition for "time to first closure", version 1 |
|---|---|
| Population | Requests first closed during the reporting day |
| Start | The time the source accepted the request |
| End | The first verified closure |
| Time zone | The business reporting time zone, applied to both timestamps |
| Exclusions | Test records; negative intervals and missing timestamps go to quarantine |
| Reopened requests | Measured at first closure; a separate measure tracks reopen rates |
| Published alongside | The number and age of open requests |
| Owner | Head of service quality |

The population of closed requests leaves out open work, so a division that leaves difficult requests open will look faster than one that closes them, even as its customers wait longer. Publishing the open backlog beside the closure time prevents that misreading. When the definition changes, for example from first closure to final closure, the version number changes too.

A median for the whole organisation cannot be rebuilt from division medians, and a mean must be weighted by group size. For example, division N closes 40 requests with a mean of 3 hours, and division S closes 160 with a mean of 8 hours. The combined mean is (40 × 3 + 160 × 8) / 200 = 7.0 hours, while averaging the two division means gives 5.5 hours, because it treats the small division as if it were as large as the other.

## Grain and identity

The **grain** of a fact table states exactly what one row represents. Kimball distinguishes several kinds of fact table, each suited to a different analytical purpose {REF:kimball}. Beacon declares three grains:

| Fact | One row per | Key |
|---|---|---|
| Service event | Source event and version | Source, event ID and version |
| Daily request snapshot | Request, reporting date and publication | Request key, date and publication ID |
| Completion | Qualifying first closure | Request key |

FIGURE datagrain | Facts at different grains share governed dimensions and are aggregated to a common grain before their measures are combined.

{FIG:datagrain} shows the three facts sharing governed dimensions. Joining facts of different grain multiplies rows: a completion of 6 hours joined to its request's five status events becomes five rows, and summing the duration gives 30 hours for one request. Aggregate each fact to the comparison grain before combining measures.

Identifiers must carry their source. Customer 17 in CRM A and customer 17 in CRM B may be different organisations, so an enterprise customer mapping needs an owner, a rule for ambiguous matches and visible handling of unresolved cases.

## Three kinds of time

Beacon must keep three timestamps apart:

- **Event time** is when the source says the business event happened.
- **Processing time** is when Beacon received and processed the record.
- **Publication time** is when a particular result became visible to users.

The Dataflow Model of Akidau and colleagues names the parts {REF:dataflow}: a **window** groups events by event time, a **watermark** estimates how complete the input is, and a **trigger** decides when to emit a result. A **refinement mode**, which the paper calls an accumulation mode, says how a later result relates to an earlier one: discarding (only the new data), accumulating (the full updated value) or accumulating and retracting (the updated value plus an explicit retraction of the old one).

Beacon's daily dashboard maps onto these ideas. The window is the business day by event time. Each night a run starts at 01:00 with every record received by then and publishes at 07:00. The first trigger for a day fires at 07:00 the next morning, and the result is marked provisional if the late-arriving source has not caught up. Later triggers fire at each following nightly run for seven days: any day whose input has changed is restated, and each later result replaces the earlier one and records which publication it supersedes, which corresponds to the accumulating and retracting refinement mode. For example, a request closes at 23:50 on 3 March, but its record reaches Beacon at 08:15 on 4 March, after the run that published 3 March. The run that starts at 01:00 on 5 March includes it, so the 3 March figure is restated at 07:00 on 5 March, and the dashboard shows that it changed and why.

Beacon must answer two questions about history: what is the current, corrected result for a past period, and what did the report show at a particular past publication? A design can store source versions and materialised publication versions, or rebuild old results from controlled history. Reproducibility does not mean keeping every personal field forever; Chapter 11 shows how deletion interacts with this history.

## Key points

- A metric contract fixes meaning, population, time zone and version, so that definition changes are visible.
- Means combine only with weights, and medians cannot be rebuilt from group medians.
- Each fact has a declared grain, and facts are aggregated to a common grain before their measures are combined.
- Event time, processing time and publication time differ, and windows, watermarks and triggers make the handling of late data explicit.
- Beacon must reproduce both the current corrected result and what was shown at each past publication.
