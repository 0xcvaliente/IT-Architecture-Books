# 2 Research experiments and architectural evidence

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Compare kinds of evidence and match each to the claim it can support.
- Design an experiment with variables, controls, business-level observations and a decision rule fixed in advance.
- Calculate drain times with a constant-rate queue model, and an upper failure bound after failure-free trials.
- Evaluate whether a load test's workload model, open or closed, fits the question being asked.
- Critique a research source by separating what it shows from how you apply it.

## Match the evidence to the claim

Different kinds of evidence answer different questions:

| Kind of evidence | What it can establish | What it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| Proof | A result that holds within a stated model | Whether the deployed system matches the model |
| Model or calculation | The consequences of stated assumptions | Whether real traffic follows those assumptions |
| Load test | How one build behaves under one workload | How another workload or configuration behaves |
| Fault-injection test | What happens at the injected failure point | What happens at failure points never injected |
| Product documentation | The vendor's stated contract | Whether your configuration relies on it correctly |
| Case study | What happened in one organisation | Whether the same causes apply in yours |

Architecture evaluation methods apply this reasoning before full implementation. The Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) examines architectural decisions against quality scenarios and identifies sensitivity points, tradeoff points and risks {REF:atam}. The studios use a lighter review with the same principle: spend investigation effort where a result could change the design.

A useful experiment asks a question that could have a negative answer. "Demonstrate our scalable platform" invites confirmation. "Does the worker pool clear 9,000 pending handoffs within thirty minutes while five new handoffs arrive each second?" can fail, so its answer informs a decision. Write down before the run what you will change if it fails.

## Variables, controls and observations

An experiment separates three kinds of quantity:

- **Independent variables** are the conditions you change on purpose, such as the number of workers or the offered arrival rate.
- **Dependent variables** are the outcomes you measure, such as completed handoffs per second or the age of the oldest pending handoff.
- **Controlled conditions** are held fixed: the build, the schema, the hardware class, the test data and any competing traffic.

Times such as response time and replication lag are usually summarised by **percentiles**. The p-th percentile is the value that p percent of the observations do not exceed: **p50** is the median, **p95** is the time within which 95 percent of requests finish, and **p99** covers all but the slowest 1 percent. For example, if 1,000 receipts are sorted by response time and the 950th took 0.8 seconds, the p95 is 0.8 seconds, however slow the last 50 were. A percentile says nothing about the observations above it, so a test report also gives the maximum, and Chapter 8 shows why percentiles of separate stages cannot simply be added.

If two things change at once, the result cannot be attributed. For example, a team doubles its workers from four to eight and also moves to a larger database instance. Throughput rises by 60 percent, but nobody knows which change caused it.

Measure business outcomes as well as technical counts, and keep the business identifier, such as the submission ID, separate from the attempt identifier. Suppose 1,000 submissions produce 1,150 successful HTTP responses because 150 calls were retries, and 20 of those retries created a second case. A dashboard of successful responses shows a healthy 1,150. The business result is 1,020 cases for 1,000 requests, which is a defect.

## Open and closed workloads

Load generators follow one of two models. In a **closed** model, a fixed population of virtual users each sends a request, waits for the response, pauses for a think time and then sends the next. In an **open** model, requests arrive on a schedule whether or not earlier requests have finished. Schroeder, Wierman and Harchol-Balter showed that the two models can behave very differently at the same load, so that the benefit of a design change, such as a new scheduling policy, can look large under one model and small under the other {REF:openclosed}.

A closed test can hide overload. With 40 virtual users and a one-second think time, a 0.1-second response time gives an offered rate of 40 / 1.1 ≈ 36.4 requests per second. If responses slow to 1.0 second, the rate falls to 40 / 2.0 = 20 per second. The slower the system, the less load the test offers, so the queue never grows. Customers reacting to a product recall behave like an open system: they keep arriving while the service slows.

## Model before you measure

In a **constant-rate queue model**, the backlog changes at the arrival rate minus the successful completion rate. With a backlog B, an arrival rate a and a successful completion rate s, the backlog clears in B / (s − a) seconds when s exceeds a, and never otherwise.

Suppose B is 9,000, a is 5 per second and s is 10 per second. The drain time is 9,000 / 5 = 1,800 seconds: exactly thirty minutes, with no allowance for failed attempts or slow calls. A thirty-minute objective therefore needs more successful capacity, less admitted demand or a revised promise, and the model found this before any load test. The listing implements the model and the failure bound discussed below.

~~~
import math

def drain_seconds(backlog: int, arrivals: float, successes: float) -> float:
    """Time to clear a backlog when work arrives and completes at constant rates."""
    if successes <= arrivals:
        return math.inf                  # the backlog never shrinks
    return backlog / (successes - arrivals)

def zero_failure_bound(trials: int, confidence: float = 0.95) -> float:
    """Upper bound on the failure probability after `trials` runs with no failure."""
    return 1 - (1 - confidence) ** (1 / trials)

def trials_needed(target: float, confidence: float = 0.95) -> int:
    """Smallest number of failure-free trials whose bound falls below `target`."""
    return math.ceil(math.log(1 - confidence) / math.log(1 - target))

for backlog, arrivals, successes in [(9000, 5, 10), (9000, 5, 15), (9000, 5, 5)]:
    seconds = drain_seconds(backlog, arrivals, successes)
    shown = 'never' if math.isinf(seconds) else f'{seconds:,.0f} s'
    print(f'backlog {backlog:,}, arrivals {arrivals}/s, '
          f'successes {successes}/s: {shown}')
print(f'bound after 400 clean crash trials: {zero_failure_bound(400):.2%}')
print(f'bound if they behave as 8 independent batches: {zero_failure_bound(8):.1%}')
print(f'clean trials needed for a bound below 0.5%: {trials_needed(0.005):,}')
~~~

Running it prints:

~~~
backlog 9,000, arrivals 5/s, successes 10/s: 1,800 s
backlog 9,000, arrivals 5/s, successes 15/s: 900 s
backlog 9,000, arrivals 5/s, successes 5/s: never
bound after 400 clean crash trials: 0.75%
bound if they behave as 8 independent batches: 31.2%
clean trials needed for a bound below 0.5%: 598
~~~

The constant rates are an assumption that real traffic never meets exactly. The model's value is that it makes its assumptions explicit and gives the experiment a number to beat.

**Little's law** is a further consistency check. For a stable system over the long run, the average number of items in the system equals the arrival rate multiplied by the average time each item spends there: L = λW {REF:little}. For example, if workers complete 15 CRM calls per second and each call takes 0.4 seconds on average, then on average 15 × 0.4 = 6 calls are in progress, so a pool limited to four concurrent calls cannot sustain that rate. The law describes averages in a stable system; it says nothing about peaks or about a backlog that is still growing.

## Interpret a run with zero failures

Chapter 1's test X-04 stopped the portal 200 times just before the commit and 200 times just after it, and found no lost or orphaned record. What does a clean run of 400 trials allow you to claim?

Suppose the trials are independent and each has the same unknown failure probability p. The probability of seeing no failures in n trials is (1 − p) to the power n. Setting that probability to 0.05 gives a one-sided 95 percent upper bound after zero failures: p_upper = 1 − 0.05^(1/n). For X-04 the bound is 0.75 percent, so the clean run is consistent with a defect that strikes up to about one crash in 133. Pushing the bound below 0.5 percent needs 598 clean trials.

Independence matters more than the count. Suppose the 400 trials ran in 8 batches, each on a freshly deployed build, and a defect would appear in every trial of a batch or in none, for example a misconfigured connection pool. The evidence is then closer to 8 trials than to 400, and the bound on a batch failing is 31.2 percent. Spread trials across the conditions that could hide a defect, such as builds, hosts and tenants, before multiplying them within one condition.

Statistical confidence and scenario coverage are different things. A thousand requests cannot exercise a failure that the test never introduces, while one targeted crash at a transaction boundary can expose a defect that a week of clean traffic would miss.

> **In practice.** Write the decision rule before the run, for example "both policies must lose no accepted request; among those, prefer the one that clears the backlog sooner". Choosing the rule afterwards invites choosing the one that favours the result you hoped for.

## Read a paper for transfer

Each studio asks for a short research critique. Identify the source's question, method, assumptions, supported conclusion and limits. Then state which part of your design corresponds to the source's model, and which does not.

For example, Parnas's information-hiding argument helps you judge boundaries around design decisions that are likely to change {REF:parnas}. It does not say that every hidden decision should become a network service. If Meridian expects one business unit to replace its CRM within eighteen months, an adapter interface that hides all CRM-specific behaviour confines that change to one module. A separate deployable service would add network and operational coupling that the information-hiding argument does not assess.

## Exercises

2.1 Recalculate the recovery time for 9,000 pending handoffs with 5 arrivals per second and 9 successful completions per second. Does it meet a thirty-minute objective?

2.2 A test report states: "Zero failures in 2,000 trials proves 99.99 percent reliability." Calculate the 95 percent upper bound that the run supports, and explain two distinct defects in the conclusion.

2.3 Design an experiment that compares two worker retry policies. Specify the independent variables, controlled conditions, business observations, injected failures and a decision rule, all before generating any results.

2.4 A closed load test uses 60 virtual users with a two-second think time. Calculate the offered request rate when the response time is 0.5 seconds and when it is 3 seconds. Explain what the difference means for the test's conclusions.

2.5 A data team publishes corrections once a night. About 3,000 corrected records arrive each day, and a monitoring query shows that on average 1,875 of them are waiting to be published. Use Little's law to find the average time a correction waits. A new rule requires every correction to be published within 24 hours: does the average show that the rule is met? State what Little's law assumes here and what it cannot tell you.

## Key points

- Each kind of evidence supports a particular kind of claim, so choose evidence that fits the question.
- A good experiment asks a question that can fail and fixes its decision rule in advance.
- Count business outcomes by business identifier, separately from attempts and retries.
- Closed load generators reduce their offered load as the system slows, which can hide overload.
- Simple models such as B / (s − a) and Little's law expose risks before testing, within their assumptions.
- Zero observed failures still leaves a quantifiable upper bound, and no clean run covers a failure that was never injected.
