# 15 Architecture review, communication and judgement

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Design a review around one bounded decision, with the right participants and a short pre-read.
- Classify findings by severity and separate them from preferences.
- Communicate one decision to executives and to engineers without changing its substance.
- Evaluate whether a finding is closed, accepted as residual risk or deferred for investigation.

## Review a bounded decision

A useful review names the decision, the architecture version and the evidence available. "Review the platform" is too broad for one meeting. "Decide whether Meridian's first release can rely on manual review of CRM B handoffs during recalls" gives the participants a concrete responsibility. Invite the people who own each part of it: the sponsor, who owns the business promise; the technical lead; the support lead, who owns the manual review queue; and a security or risk owner where needed.

Send a short decision brief beforehand, such as the one at the end of Chapter 5, with detailed diagrams and experiment records as supporting material. The meeting's time is for challenging assumptions, not for a long presentation.

A review ends in one of four outcomes: proceed, proceed with conditions, investigate further, or reject. Each condition needs an owner and checkable closure evidence. "Security to review" names an activity; "Cross-tenant download, search and export tests pass for release 1.4, and the policy owner approves any exception" can be checked.

## Separate findings from preferences

A **finding** identifies the requirement affected, the defect or uncertainty, its consequence and the evidence for it. Severity follows the consequence:

| Severity | Typical consequence | Effect on release |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | Accepted transactions lost, stock oversold or data disclosed to the wrong tenant | Blocks release |
| Major | An important claim, such as a recovery objective, is unsupported | Blocks release unless the accountable owner accepts it |
| Minor | A diagram label or document is ambiguous | Fixed in the normal course of work |
| Enhancement | An optional improvement | Recorded for later |

A register makes the status of each finding visible. Part of the register from Meridian's quota review reads:

| ID | Finding | Severity | Owner | Closure evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F-11 | Each worker replica runs its own rate limiter, so three replicas could send 24 calls per second against an 8-call quota | Major | Technical lead | Shared limiter; load test with three replicas stays at or below 8 calls per second | Closed |
| F-12 | Manual review of CRM B handoffs during a recall needs about 2.4 agent hours per surge | Major | Business unit B manager | Staffing plan approved for declared recalls | Accepted residual risk |
| F-13 | The vendor has not confirmed that a temporary uplift can be switched on within an hour | Major | Sponsor | Written confirmation from the vendor | Deferred investigation, due 30 April |
| F-14 | The container view calls the worker a "sync service" | Minor | Architect | Diagram updated | Closed |

Preferences need different handling. If a reviewer prefers another database, ask which requirement the change would improve and what evidence supports that. Equally, never downgrade a broken invariant because the team likes its current stack.

Treat risk matrices with care. Multiplying a likelihood score by an impact score can structure a discussion, but the product is not a measured probability. For example, risk X scores 3 × 4 = 12 and risk Y scores 2 × 5 = 10, so X ranks first; if a reasonable reviewer rates Y's likelihood as 3, Y scores 15 and the order flips. Record the reasoning behind each score and check whether plausible changes alter the priority.

## Communicate the same decision at two levels

Executives need the business consequence, the options, the cost range, the uncertainty and the decision required of them. Engineers need ownership, contracts, failure states, configuration and test results. Both should receive two views of the same argument: an unconditional promise in the executive summary, with its conditions hidden in an appendix, is a misrepresentation.

For example, Meridian's executive message could read: "CRM B's new limit cannot meet the thirty-minute recall promise. We can buy capacity during recalls for about €1,200 a year, buy it permanently for €14,400, or accept 75 minutes. Customer requests are safe in every option." The technical appendix shows the backlog equation, the quota's scope and the shared rate limiter, and nothing in it contradicts the summary.

Diagrams should support what is said. Start with the business journey, then show the boundary that delivers the key guarantee; when discussing resilience, show the failure sequence.

## Disagree without inventing certainty

When challenged, restate the concern and decide which of three kinds it is:

- **A new fact**, such as a vendor's new rate limit, may change what is feasible.
- **A different preference**, such as a stronger wish for low cost, may change the weights in a comparison.
- **A different interpretation** of a requirement may show that the design solves the wrong problem, as when Aster's team first read "confirmed" as "checkout received".

If you do not know the answer, say so and propose the smallest investigation that could settle it. Do not fill the gap with a confident claim about a product. Equally, avoid deferring every ordinary choice: a reversible, low-consequence choice can proceed under a recorded assumption, while a high-impact uncertainty blocks release until it is resolved.

> **In practice.** "I don't know yet; here is the test that would tell us, and it takes two days" is a strong answer in a review. It turns an argument into a plan.

## Record learning after the review

Keep the finding register current. When a condition closes, record what was tested and which design version the test applies to, because a configuration change can invalidate earlier results. If a finding is accepted as residual risk, name the accountable owner and a review date rather than marking it "fixed". After each studio, compare your revised design with your first version: which assumptions changed, which decisions held, and was each investigation worth its cost?

## Key points

- A review should address one bounded decision, with the owners of each part present and a short pre-read.
- Findings state the requirement, defect, consequence and evidence, and their severity follows the consequence.
- Conditions need owners and checkable closure evidence; residual risk needs a named owner and a review date.
- Executive and engineering messages are two views of the same argument and must never contradict each other.
- Saying what is unknown, with a plan to find out, is stronger than an invented certainty.
