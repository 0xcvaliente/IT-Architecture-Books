# 1 Architecture as a defensible argument

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain how a requirement, a claim, a mechanism and a test combine into an architecture argument.
- Classify statements as constraints, preferences or assumptions, and apply constraints before any weighted scoring.
- Design a small set of views that share one vocabulary and agree about failure behaviour.
- Write an architecture decision record with alternatives, consequences and a measurable review trigger.

## Start from a decision

An assignment becomes manageable when it names a decision. "Design an integration platform" invites unlimited scope. "Choose how service requests reach three CRMs while each business unit keeps its own case records" names the actors, the boundary and the consequences, so it can be answered, challenged and revised.

An architecture is therefore an argument: it claims that a particular arrangement of components, data and responsibilities meets particular needs. A strong argument shows how each promise is kept and what would show that it is broken.

## The argument chain

This book uses a six-link chain to make the argument explicit:

- A **requirement** is an observable promise. For example: a valid service request receives a receipt within one second.
- A **claim** states what the design will achieve: the portal keeps accepting requests while a CRM is down for thirty minutes.
- A **mechanism** explains how: the API stores the request and a handoff record in one database transaction, and a worker delivers the handoff later.
- **Evidence** challenges the claim: a test, a calculation, a measurement or a documented contract.
- A **finding** states what the evidence supports and what remains open.
- A **decision** chooses an option, within a stated scope and under stated conditions.

FIGURE argument | An architecture argument links a requirement to a decision through a claim, a mechanism, evidence and a finding.

{FIG:argument} draws the chain as a cycle, because new findings often send you back to the requirement. Take the claim that the portal tolerates a thirty-minute CRM outage. It needs three observations: requests are accepted during the outage, accepted requests survive a restart of the portal, and the backlog clears within the recovery objective once the CRM returns. A screenshot of a full queue shows none of these.

One counterexample breaks a claim, however polished the rest of the argument. A passing test supports the claim only for the conditions it exercised. That is why later chapters design tests that try to break the design.

## Keep a small set of connected views

A **view** describes the system from one perspective. The C4 model offers a widely used hierarchy for software structure: system context, containers and components {REF:c4}. Behaviour, placement and data need further views, so a typical dossier has five:

- a **context view** of scope and of which external systems own which facts;
- a **container view** of the running applications and data stores and their responsibilities;
- a **sequence or state view** of important behaviour, including failure;
- a **deployment view** of where containers run and which failures they share;
- a **data view** of identity, ownership and lifecycle.

The views must agree. For example, if the container view shows a worker calling a CRM, the sequence view must say what happens when the CRM commits a case and the reply is lost, and the data view must hold the identifier needed to recognise that case later.

### Use one vocabulary

Ambiguous nouns hide disagreements about responsibility. Keep a short register of terms and use them consistently in schemas, diagrams and user messages. The Meridian studio uses this register:

| Term | Meaning | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Submission | A customer's request as stored by the portal | Portal |
| Receipt | The portal's confirmation that it has durably stored a submission | Portal |
| Handoff | The delivery of a submission to the business unit's CRM | Portal |
| Service case | The CRM's business record for the work | CRM |
| Work order | The ERP record that schedules a technician or parts | ERP |

Calling all of these "ticket" would hide the question of who owns what.

## Constraints, preferences and assumptions

Statements in a brief fall into three categories:

- A **constraint** decides eligibility. An option that breaks it is out, whatever else it offers.
- A **preference** ranks the eligible options.
- An **assumption** is a fact you believe but have not yet verified.

A weighted scoring matrix suits preferences and misleads about constraints. Suppose three integration options are scored from 1 to 5:

| Option | Cost (0.4) | Delivery speed (0.3) | Operability (0.3) | Weighted score | Keeps requests during a 30-minute outage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3.3 | Yes |
| B | 5 | 4 | 3 | 4.1 | No |
| C | 2 | 2 | 5 | 2.9 | Yes |

Option B scores highest, 0.4 × 5 + 0.3 × 4 + 0.3 × 3 = 4.1, but it loses accepted requests when the CRM is down. It is ineligible, and the real choice is between A and C. If the business wants B, it must change the promise, and the dossier should say who has the authority to do that.

Assumptions need an owner and a deadline. For example, if a CRM vendor's sales team says that repeated create requests are safe, record that as assumption A-03, with the person who will verify it, the date and the consequence if it is false.

## Record decisions and their history

An **architecture decision record (ADR)** captures one significant decision. Nygard's format records its context, the decision, its status and its consequences {REF:adr}. This book adds the alternatives considered, the identifiers of supporting evidence and a review trigger.

A **review trigger** is an observable condition that should reopen the decision:

- Weak: "Move to Kafka later."
- Strong: "Revisit if a second consumer needs to replay handoff events, or if outbox polling uses more than 10 percent of database CPU at peak load."

The weak version names a preferred technology; the strong version names a condition that can be checked. When a decision is replaced, keep the old record and write a new one that supersedes it and says which assumption changed.

## A worked dossier fragment

The following fragment links five items by identifier:

- Requirement R-01: a valid request receives a durable receipt while a CRM is unavailable.
- Decision ADR-M01: store the submission and its handoff record in one local transaction.
- Invariant V-01: every receipt has exactly one handoff record.
- Test X-04: stop the portal process just before and just after the commit, 200 times each, and check both tables after restart.
- Finding F-04: no receipt existed without its handoff record, or the reverse. Local atomicity holds for these crash points; duplicate CRM cases remain an open risk, addressed by ADR-M03.

The finding is deliberately narrower than the design. It does not show that the CRM received the request or that the caller was authorised correctly; each of those is a separate claim with its own test.

> **Common mistake.** Treating one passing test as proof of the whole design. State which claim the test supports and list the claims that still need their own evidence.

## Exercises

1.1 Turn "the platform must be reliable" into two measurable quality scenarios that observe different things. For each, state the stimulus, the environment, the expected response and the measure.

1.2 A team scores three options with weights of 0.5 for cost, 0.2 for delivery speed and 0.3 for security. Option P scores 4, 3 and 3; option Q scores 5, 4 and 2; option R scores 2, 3 and 5. Q cannot export data in an open format, which the business has made mandatory. Calculate the weighted scores and write a short decision paragraph that applies the constraint before the scores.

1.3 A payment request times out, so its outcome is unknown. Trace this situation through the context, container, sequence, data and deployment views. Name one contradiction between views that a review could detect.

1.4 Critique the review trigger "We will move to microservices when we grow." Rewrite it as a measurable trigger for a system of your choice.

1.5 Classify each statement as a constraint, a preference or an assumption: (a) customer data must stay in the EU; (b) the team would like to use a managed database; (c) the CRM vendor says its API is idempotent; (d) the first release should cost as little as possible to run.

## Key points

- An architecture is an argument that links requirements to decisions through claims, mechanisms and evidence.
- One counterexample can break a claim, while a passing test supports only the conditions it exercised.
- Views must share one vocabulary and agree about failure, not just about the normal path.
- Constraints decide eligibility before preferences are scored, and assumptions need an owner and a deadline.
- A decision record with a measurable review trigger keeps the reasoning available when the facts change.
