# 5 Integration evaluation and a changed quota

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Calculate the backlog, drain time and required capacity for a CRM outage under a call quota.
- Design observations that reveal starvation of one business unit and duplicate effects.
- Evaluate responses to a reduced shared quota in terms of cost, customer promise and support effort.
- Assemble a complete dossier: problem brief, quality scenarios, fact ownership, decision record and decision brief.

## Establish the recovery scenario

During the recall surge from Chapter 3, 5 new requests per second arrive for CRM B for two hours. The worst case for recovery is an outage at the start of the surge: CRM B fails for its first thirty minutes, and the surge continues for ninety minutes after CRM B returns. The portal keeps accepting requests, so the backlog grows to 5 × 1,800 = 9,000 pending handoffs. Decision record ADR-M04 proposed that this backlog must clear within thirty minutes of CRM B's return, while requests keep arriving at 5 per second.

CRM B's contract allows 15 create calls per second. With the drain model from Chapter 2, the net drain is 15 − 5 = 10 per second, and recovery takes 9,000 / 10 = 900 seconds, or fifteen minutes.

A call quota limits attempts, not successes. If 10 percent of attempts fail without creating anything, successful creates run at 13.5 per second, the net drain falls to 8.5 per second, and recovery takes about 1,059 seconds, or seventeen and a half minutes. The objective still holds, with less margin than the simple calculation suggested.

## Observe the right quantities

A recovery test should record, for each business unit:

- accepted receipts and distinct confirmed handoffs, counted by submission ID;
- create attempts, searches, throttled calls and unknown outcomes;
- the age of the oldest pending handoff and the size of the manual review queue;
- database wait time and connection use.

An aggregate graph can look healthy while one business unit starves. For example, 100 completed attempts per second across the portal says nothing about whether CRM B's backlog is shrinking. The test should stop CRM B at the peak, restart workers during recovery and inject duplicate deliveries. It passes only if every accepted receipt ends in `confirmed` or in a visible `manual_review` item.

## The change card

After the first design review, Meridian receives this change card.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Card | CC-M1, CRM B quota change |
| New fact | From next month, CRM B allows 8 calls per second per customer account, shared by creates, searches and status reads |
| Source | Vendor notice, confirmed by the vendor's account manager |
| Assumptions affected | A-05: "CRM B allows 15 creates per second, with searches counted separately" |
| Decisions affected | ADR-M04, the thirty-minute recovery objective |
| Question | How should Meridian respond, and what does each response change for customers? |

The portal polls CRM B for status changes so that customers see updates within a minute, which uses about 2 calls per second and leaves 6 for creates. Even if every create succeeds, the net drain is now 6 − 5 = 1 per second. By the end of the surge, at minute 120, 5,400 handoffs have cleared and 3,600 remain; with no new arrivals, 6 creates per second clear them in ten more minutes, so recovery takes 100 minutes. With 10 percent failed attempts, the net drain is 0.4 per second, 6,840 handoffs remain when the surge ends, and recovery takes about 111 minutes, until minute 141. Only the end of the surge stops the backlog from lasting far longer. Adding workers cannot help, because the quota limits the whole account; more workers would only waste quota on throttled calls.

To meet thirty minutes, successful creates must reach 5 + 9,000 / 1,800 = 10 per second. With 10 percent failures that needs about 11.1 attempts per second, and with status polling about 13.1 calls per second, so the account needs a quota of at least 14. The calculation turns a compute question into a contract question.

## Revise the recommendation

Every response changes a business or technical contract. Silently dropping accepted requests is never one of them, because it breaks the promise made by the receipt. The credible options, all assuming 10 percent failed attempts, are:

| Option | Calls per second | Recovery after CRM B returns | Annual cost | Effect on customers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Buy a permanent quota of 16 | 16 | About 20 minutes with polling on; about 16 with polling paused | €14,400 | Thirty-minute objective kept |
| 2. Keep 8, pause status polling during recovery | 8 | About 68 minutes with polling paused | None | Objective becomes 75 minutes; B's statuses stay "Received, awaiting service team" for longer |
| 3. Temporary uplift to 16 during declared recalls | 16 while a recall is declared | About 20 minutes with polling on; about 16 with polling paused | About €1,200 at €100 a day, if switch-on within an hour is confirmed | Objective kept during recalls |

Option 1 leaves 16 − 2 = 14 calls for creates, of which 12.6 succeed per second, so the backlog clears in about 9,000 / (12.6 − 5) ≈ 1,184 seconds. Option 2 has 7.2 successful creates per second and needs about 9,000 / (7.2 − 5) ≈ 4,091 seconds. Options 1 and 3 improve further if status polling pauses while more than 1,000 handoffs are pending. All 16 calls then go to creates and 14.4 succeed per second. The backlog clears in about 8,000 / 9.4 + 1,000 / 7.6 ≈ 983 seconds, or about 16 minutes.

Two controls apply to every option. The rate limiter must match the scope of the quota: a limiter inside each worker replica multiplies the permitted rate by the number of replicas, so Meridian needs one limiter shared across the account. And the quota must be shared fairly: a strict priority for recall requests could starve ordinary CRM B requests indefinitely, while a weighted share, for example 90 percent for recall work with unused capacity lent back, keeps both moving.

## Count the costs on the same basis

The vendor's price list offers two ways to buy capacity. A permanent upgrade to 16 calls per second costs €1,200 a month, or €14,400 a year. A temporary uplift costs €100 a day while a recall is declared. The risk register expects up to two declared recalls a year, each lasting about six days, so the temporary uplift would cost about 2 × 6 × €100 = €1,200 a year. The account manager has not yet confirmed that the uplift can be switched on within an hour of a declaration, which is what the recovery promise needs.

The support team estimates that a recall coinciding with a CRM B outage produces about 400 extra "where is my request" calls. At six minutes each, that is 40 agent hours, or €1,800 at an internal rate of €45 per hour, and the risk register expects one such event a year. Support savings of €1,800 a year do not justify €14,400 a year, so the case for buying capacity rests on the safety promise: customers with potentially unsafe equipment expect the service team to see their request quickly. That judgement belongs to the sponsor; the architect's job is to show the trade clearly, including the cheaper option 3 and the assumption it depends on.

## The worked Meridian dossier

The following dossier is one sound answer to the studio. Compare it with your own first submission.

### Problem brief

Meridian's three business units each run their own CRM, with three different contracts for creating cases. Customers need one portal that accepts service requests reliably, including during a product recall, and shows each request's true status. The first release must deliver every accepted request to the right CRM, with no losses and no unmanaged duplicates.

- **Sponsor:** head of service operations.
- **Stakeholders:** the three business unit managers, the portal team, the three CRM vendors and the service agents.
- **In scope:** submission, receipt, handoff to CRMs A, B and C, and the customer-visible status.
- **Out of scope:** replacing any CRM, and changes to how the work-order integration creates ERP work orders.
- **Open questions:** CRM B's index lag, the price of a temporary quota uplift, and agent time for manual review during recalls.

### Quality scenarios

| ID | Stimulus and environment | Required response | Measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 20 valid submissions per second for ten minutes | Receipts returned | 95 percent within one second, excluding upload |
| Q2 | Normal operation over a month | Eligible submissions receive receipts | 99.9 percent success |
| Q3 | CRM B unavailable for thirty minutes while 5 requests per second arrive | Submissions still accepted | No accepted request lost; every receipt has a handoff record |
| Q4 | CRM B returns after Q3 | Backlog cleared | Within thirty minutes of CRM B's return (ADR-M05) |
| Q5 | A CRM changes a case status | Portal shows the new status | Within one minute for 99 percent of changes, outside recovery periods |
| Q6 | A staff member without tenant T042 in scope requests a T042 submission | Request refused | No disclosure in the release access test matrix |
| Q7 | A client repeats a submission with the same key | Same receipt returned | No second submission; no second case in CRM A or CRM C |
| Q8 | CRM B takes 20 seconds per call | CRM A and C handoffs unaffected | Oldest pending A and C handoff under one minute |

### Fact ownership

| Fact | Owner | Portal's treatment | Conflict rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Submission and receipt | Portal | Durable record with the idempotency result | Same key and body return the same receipt |
| Handoff state | Portal | One five-state record per submission | Changed only by the lease owner or a guarded review |
| Business unit for a tenant | ERP service contract | Copied at acceptance | Later changes affect only new submissions |
| Service case and status | The business unit's CRM, after confirmed handoff | Projection with the CRM's version | Older versions ignored |
| Customer-visible status | Portal, derived | "Received" until confirmed, then the CRM's status | Recomputed from handoff state and projection |
| Assets, contracts and entitlements | ERP | Cached read model labelled with source version and freshness | ERP wins; stale entries flagged |
| Work orders | ERP | Read-only reference | Created only by the work-order integration after a `ServiceCaseApproved` event |
| Staff scope | Identity administration | Role, business unit and set of tenants | Checked on every request |

### Decision record

**ADR-M05: Respond to CRM B's shared quota.** Status: accepted. Supersedes the capacity assumption in ADR-M04.

- **Context.** CRM B now allows 8 calls per second per account for all call types (change card CC-M1), and status polling uses about 2 of them. With 10 percent failed attempts, recovery from a thirty-minute outage during a recall would take about 68 minutes with status polling paused and about 111 minutes with polling on, because the two-hour surge keeps adding requests until minute 120. The objective is thirty minutes (Q4).
- **Decision.** Buy the temporary uplift to 16 calls per second for declared recalls, at the listed €100 a day, provided the vendor confirms within four weeks that it can be switched on within an hour of a declaration. If the vendor cannot confirm, buy the permanent upgrade to 16 calls per second at €1,200 a month, and cancel it if the temporary uplift is confirmed later. In all cases, run one account-wide rate limiter, and pause CRM B status polling while more than 1,000 CRM B handoffs are pending.
- **Alternatives.** The permanent upgrade alone (objective kept, €14,400 a year); no upgrade with a 75-minute objective (no cost, slower status); the vendor's nightly bulk import (rejected as far too slow).
- **Consequences.** With 16 calls per second and polling paused, the backlog clears about 16 minutes after CRM B returns. During that time, CRM B customers with unconfirmed requests see "Received, awaiting service team", and confirmed CRM B cases show their last known status. Recall declarations become a vendor dependency. No accepted request is lost under any option.
- **Evidence.** The drain calculations in this chapter; recovery test X-07 at 8 and 16 calls per second; vendor notice CC-M1.
- **Review triggers.** The vendor changes the quota again; a forecast recall exceeds 5 requests per second; status polling exceeds 2 calls per second; or two years pass without a CRM B outage during a recall, which would reopen the cost question.

### Decision brief

**Decision needed by 30 April from the head of service operations:** how to keep the recall recovery promise after CRM B's quota cut.

**What changed.** CRM B cut its call allowance from 15 to 8 per second. If CRM B fails for thirty minutes at the start of a recall, requests could now wait almost two hours to reach the service team, instead of under thirty minutes.

**What stays the same.** In every option, the portal accepts every request, loses none, and issues a receipt.

**Options.**

1. Buy permanent extra capacity: €14,400 a year, recovery in about 16 minutes with status polling paused.
2. Buy nothing and change the promise to 75 minutes: no cost, but status updates for business unit B pause during recovery.
3. Buy extra capacity only during declared recalls: €100 a day, about €1,200 a year, recovery in about 16 minutes with status polling paused, if the vendor confirms that it can be switched on within an hour.

**Recommendation.** Option 3, with option 1 as the fallback if the vendor cannot confirm the one-hour switch-on within four weeks. Support savings alone, about €1,800 a year, do not pay for option 1; the case rests on how quickly recall requests must reach the service team.

**Risks and conditions.** The one-hour switch-on for option 3 is unconfirmed, and a recall larger than 5 requests per second, or longer than two hours, or an outage longer than thirty minutes, would need a new calculation.

> **In practice.** Write the decision brief last, for someone who reads nothing else. The sponsor should be able to decide from this page alone, and engineers should find every number on it traced in the dossier.

## Key points

- Recovery depends on net successful capacity: successful completions minus new arrivals, not worker count.
- A quota counts attempts and every call type, so failures and status polling consume recovery capacity.
- A changed external limit can require a changed business promise, and the dossier must show that dependency.
- Economic comparisons need explicit units, a stated basis for every price and a plain statement of what the money buys.
- A complete dossier links scenarios, ownership, decisions and evidence, and ends with a one-page brief for the decision maker.
