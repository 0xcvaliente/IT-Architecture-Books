# 14 Cutover rehearsal and modernisation review

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Analyse rehearsal timings to find the latest safe go/no-go decision point.
- Design preconditions that remove the causes of slow cutover steps.
- Evaluate a change card that delays a partner dependency, and choose a response with explicit conditions.
- Define the evidence needed to retire the replaced responsibility and to claim the promised benefits.

## The cutover sequence

Orion's cutover runs inside the 45-minute Sunday window in seven steps:

1. Fence the legacy writers: revoke rights, end sessions and confirm that probe writes fail.
2. Let CDC apply everything up to the final log position.
3. Run the final reconciliation.
4. Raise the target's authority epoch to 8 and open the internal route.
5. Run smoke tests through the internal route, with test-tenant orders and a test call to the partner.
6. Make the go/no-go decision.
7. On "go", switch public routing to the new service and verify it.

Customers can look up order status throughout, because public routing stays on the legacy platform until step 7.

## Three rehearsals

Orion rehearsed the cutover three times on a full-size copy of production. The durations, in minutes, were:

| Step | Rehearsal 1 | Rehearsal 2 | Rehearsal 3 | Worst observed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Fence legacy writers | 6 | 4 | 4 | 6 |
| 2. Drain to final position | 3 | 9 | 2 | 9 |
| 3. Final reconciliation | 11 | 10 | 9 | 11 |
| 4. Epoch and internal route | 2 | 2 | 1 | 2 |
| 5. Internal smoke tests | 9 | 8 | 8 | 9 |
| 6. Go/no-go decision | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| 7. Switch public routing | 4 | 3 | 3 | 4 |
| Total | 38 | 39 | 30 | 44 |

Every rehearsal fitted the window, but the real question is whether a "no" can still be carried out in time. The latest safe decision point is the window minus the time needed to reverse: the rehearsed reverse path from Chapter 13 takes six minutes, so the decision must be made by minute 45 − 6 = 39. Three rehearsals then raise a question that a single timing cannot answer: how much do the steps vary, and why? A "go" needs at most four minutes to switch routing, which would allow a decision as late as minute 41, so the reverse path is the binding constraint.

If every step took its worst observed time, the decision would finish at minute 6 + 9 + 11 + 2 + 9 + 3 = 40, one minute too late. The cause is visible in the table: rehearsal 2 started a few hours after a month-end job, so step 2 had to apply its 600,000-row transaction, about 500 seconds at the applier's large-transaction rate, plus the changes queued behind it: nine minutes in all. The lag precondition from Chapter 13, never within 24 hours of the month-end job, removes that case, and with a drain of at most three minutes the decision finishes at minute 34.

One more change buys margin. Running the full key-set comparison in the hour before the window, and reconciling only the changes since then during step 3, should cut that step to about six minutes; rehearsal 4 must confirm it. The revised plan reaches the decision at minute 29, ten minutes before the latest safe point, and completes the switch at minute 33. The decision rule is written down in advance: if smoke tests have not passed by minute 36, the answer is no-go, because the decision itself takes three minutes.

> **In practice.** Summing the worst observed time for every step is conservative when steps vary independently. But three rehearsals are a small sample, and steps that share a cause, such as month-end load, can be slow together. Remove shared causes with preconditions rather than hoping for slack.

## Readiness beyond the timeline

A cutover can run perfectly to time and still fail. The release review also checks:

- the new service's capacity at the weekday peak, not just at 02:00 on a Sunday;
- production service identities and permissions, since a load that works under an administrator account says nothing about the production account;
- operator access, monitoring and alerting for the new service;
- routing behaviour, for example lowering the DNS time-to-live a day in advance;
- support runbooks and the customer message for the 45-minute pause;
- the partner's readiness, including the joint rehearsal.

Test data must look like production. For example, twelve million near-identical test rows can hide old character encodings, very long text fields and a few very large customer accounts. Rehearse failures at the points where the response differs: CDC stalls after the fence, reconciliation finds a mismatch, a smoke test fails on the partner call, or routing has switched but the new service rejects production identities. Before the switch, each leads to the six-minute reverse path; after it, to the reverse-CDC procedure or a fix forward.

## A change card: the partner slips

Orion's plan counts weeks back from the hosting deadline, so week −10 means ten weeks before the hosting agreement ends. Under the partner contract from Chapter 12, the partner delivers its status API in week −14, after ten weeks of development. Two weeks of joint testing follow, ending with rehearsal 4, the joint cutover rehearsal, in week −12. The cutover is planned for the Sunday of week −10, which leaves nine more Sundays for a second attempt after a no-go. The current host will extend the agreement by three months for €120,000, but only with notice given by week −8.

In week −16, six weeks before the planned cutover, Orion receives this change card.

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Card | CC-O1, partner API delay |
| New fact | The partner's status API will arrive six weeks late, in week −8 |
| Source | Partner project manager, confirmed in writing |
| Assumptions affected | The partner status API is available for joint testing and rehearsal 4 |
| Decisions affected | The cutover date; the partner integration in the plan for each phase |
| Question | Does Orion delay, cut over without the API, or extend hosting? |

Three responses are credible:

| Milestone | 1. Delay the cutover | 2. File fallback | 3. Extend hosting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Partner status API | Week −8 | Week −8, adopted after cutover | Week −8 |
| Rehearsal 4 | Week −6, with the API | Week −12, with a status file | Week −6, with the API |
| Cutover | Week −5 | Week −10, as planned | Week −5 or later |
| Sundays left after a no-go | 4 | 9 | 4, plus three months |
| Extension decision | Week −8, before any joint test | Week −8, after the cutover | Week −8, €120,000 |

1. **Delay the cutover until the API is ready.** Joint testing moves to weeks −8 to −6, and the cutover to week −5, after a week to fix what rehearsal 4 finds. Four Sundays remain for a second attempt, which is tight but workable. The weakness is the notice date: Orion must decide on the €120,000 extension in week −8, the week the API is due, before it has tested the API at all. A further slip of even two weeks would leave no room to recover.
2. **Cut over on time using the file fallback.** The partner sends a status file every 15 minutes, and Orion's adapter, estimated at 15 engineer-days, turns each row into a status API call using the row's event ID. Rehearsal 4 stays in week −12 and uses a full day's status file. Status updates can take up to about 20 minutes instead of 15. The partner's database account is revoked at the fence in any case. Because the cutover in week −10 comes before the notice date, a no-go still leaves time to buy the extension.
3. **Extend the hosting agreement.** €120,000 buys three months on the current host but no progress, and the migration still depends on the delayed API. This is a different option from the temporary rehosting of Chapter 12, which would move the legacy platform to new hosting and remains a fallback until its decision date at the end of month six, in week −13, three weeks after this card; the extension leaves the platform where it is.

Decision record ADR-O07 chooses option 2 under four conditions:

- rehearsal 4 processes a full day's status file through the adapter;
- the business owner agrees in writing to a temporary 20-minute status objective;
- Orion moves the partner to the API within four weeks of its delivery, by week −4;
- if the week −10 cutover is a no-go whose cause cannot be fixed within a week, Orion gives the extension notice by week −8.

The authority epoch on dispatch commands is unaffected, because dispatch flows from Orion to the partner while the fallback carries status the other way. The fallback term negotiated in Chapter 12 turns the partner's delay into a bounded change with a known cost, about 15 engineer-days and five minutes of status delay.

## Retire the old responsibility

The migration is complete only when the old responsibility is gone. Orion's retirement checklist includes:

- no reads or writes on the legacy order tables for 30 days, confirmed by the audit log;
- all legacy accounts disabled and scheduled jobs removed through controlled changes;
- the support team's auto-close script rebuilt as a scheduled function of the new service, with the support manager's approval of its 90-day rule;
- historical records archived according to the retention policy;
- hosting and licences cancelled only after all of the above.

Measure the benefits that justified the programme. If hosting cost falls but two people now reconcile data by hand each day, the cost has moved rather than disappeared. The technical cutover is a milestone; the business improvement is a separate outcome with its own measurements.

## Compare the alternatives again

A good review may change the original recommendation, and it should record why. For Orion, direct replacement is now clearly ruled out, by the window and by the writers that only a long observation revealed. Temporary rehosting remained a live fallback until the end of month six, when the clean dual run removed the need for it, and phased migration with CDC is confirmed. Keep the earlier decision records and supersede them explicitly, so that a later reader can see which facts changed the ranking.

## Studio assessment

Submit a migration dossier containing the writer inventory with confidence levels, the strategy comparison, the authority table for each phase, the CDC design with its lag preconditions, the mapping rules and dual-run results, the fence design, the rehearsal analysis with its latest decision point, the response to change card CC-O1, and the recovery and retirement plans. A strong dossier states what must be true before each step and how Orion stays operable if that condition fails.

## Exercises

14.1 The rehearsed reverse path takes eight minutes instead of six. Calculate the latest safe decision point. If smoke tests then take 14 minutes under the revised plan, does the plan still fit?

14.2 Suppose each of steps 1 to 6 varies independently and is equally likely to take any of its three rehearsal times. Calculate the probability that all six take their worst observed times in the same cutover. What does the result say about the plan that sums the worst times, and in which two situations would that reasoning fail?

14.3 Draft a short decision notice for change card CC-O1, stating the option chosen, its conditions, the owner and the date of the next review.

14.4 Design a go/no-go gate of five checks for step 6. For each check, state the evidence and who confirms it.

14.5 A manager proposes skipping the internal smoke tests to save nine minutes and switching public routing straight after reconciliation. Evaluate the proposal.

## Key points

- The latest safe decision point comes from the time needed to reverse, not from the time needed to finish.
- Rehearsal variance has causes, and preconditions that remove them are worth more than added slack.
- Keeping public routing on the old system until go/no-go makes a "no" cheap and fast.
- A fallback negotiated early can turn a partner delay into a bounded change.
- A migration ends with evidence-based retirement of the old responsibility and measured business benefits.
