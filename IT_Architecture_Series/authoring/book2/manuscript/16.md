# 16 Portfolio assessment and professional transfer

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Assemble a portfolio that shows reasoning, evidence and revision across the four studios.
- Apply a common rubric consistently to your own dossiers and to others'.
- Compare how one principle, such as durable intent, changes from one context to another.
- Plan supervised experience that closes a specific gap in your practice.

## The final portfolio

Choose your two strongest studio dossiers for detailed presentation, and write shorter comparative reports for the other two. Keep the briefs, assumptions, first designs, decision records, experiment records, findings and revisions, because the portfolio should show how you reasoned and improved the design. Each detailed dossier contains:

- a one-page decision brief and a register of requirements and assumptions;
- connected context, container, data, sequence and deployment views;
- at least three significant decision records;
- a failure and recovery design, with security and data lifecycle reasoning;
- a cost model with every input stated, and an experiment report;
- a statement of what remains unverified.

The comparative reports explain which principles transfer and which constraints change the answer. Durable intent is a good example. Meridian records it as an outbox row committed with the submission, Aster as an order state with a payment operation ID, and Orion as the authority epoch in `write_authority`, together with the cutover gate record, which state durably who may write and when that changed. Beacon's publication identifier serves a different purpose, explaining a past result rather than driving future work. The comparison should therefore name what each studio's durable record protects, and who acts on it next, instead of reusing one diagram for all four.

## A common assessment rubric

Use the same rubric for every dossier. The points organise feedback and comparison; they do not certify professional competence. Award full credit to a well-argued alternative even when it differs from the worked design.

| Dimension | Points | What the dossier must show |
|---|---|---|
| Problem and requirements | 15 | Clear outcomes, constraints, measurable scenarios and owners |
| Boundaries and invariants | 20 | Coherent authority, transactions, states and data contracts |
| Alternatives and judgement | 15 | At least two viable options and explicit tradeoffs |
| Failure, security and recovery | 20 | Threats, unknown outcomes, compensation and tested recovery claims |
| Evidence and research use | 15 | Reproducible methods, stated limits and accurate use of sources |
| Operation, economics and transition | 10 | Feasible support, explicit cost inputs and operable intermediate states |
| Communication and revision | 5 | A clear dossier and a traceable response to challenge |

A strong submission scores at least 75 points and contains no unresolved critical flaw. A design that could knowingly oversell, lose accepted work or show one tenant's data to another must fix that defect, however well it is presented. When a constraint truly cannot be met, a clearly argued conclusion that it is infeasible can show strong judgement.

## The oral defence

Prepare a ten-minute explanation followed by twenty minutes of questions. Open with the business problem and the promise, explain the two decisions that most shape the result, walk through one difficult failure sequence, and finish with the evidence and the conditions for proceeding.

During the questions, the examiner presents a change card, such as:

- a vendor halves its call quota;
- the payment provider shortens its idempotency window from 24 hours to one hour;
- a source starts correcting events that are 30 days old;
- a legacy writer reappears after cutover.

You have five minutes to name the assumptions affected and propose a response. For example, a one-hour payment window means Aster must resolve every unknown payment outcome within the hour or route it to people before the window closes, so the manual queue grows and the review triggers in the payment decision record fire. You need not redesign every component on the spot, but you must show which guarantees are now at risk. A careful answer that names an unresolved contract is stronger than a quick one that invents a contract. If no possible evidence could change your recommendation, it is probably a preference rather than an evaluated conclusion.

## A research synthesis

Write a synthesis of about 1,500 words using at least four primary sources cited in this book. Compare the kind of knowledge each provides: a formal result, a design argument, a research method, a standard or operational guidance. For each source, explain one studio decision it informed and one claim it does not establish.

For example, Ongaro and Ousterhout's Raft algorithm keeps a replicated log consistent across servers {REF:raft}. Once a majority of servers has stored an entry, the entry is committed and survives the failure of the leader, and all servers apply the same commands in the same order. Transferring that result to Aster needs care, because Aster's PostgreSQL database does not run Raft. PostgreSQL's streaming replication is asynchronous by default, so a failover can lose transactions that the primary had already confirmed to clients, in proportion to the replication delay {REF:pgsync}. A reservation confirmed to a customer could then vanish. Aster gets a guarantee comparable to Raft's only if each commit waits for a standby. Three settings work together: `synchronous_standby_names` lists the standbys, for example `ANY 1 (s1, s2)` for a quorum of one; `synchronous_commit` keeps its default value `on`; and the failover procedure promotes a standby that has confirmed the latest commits. The guarantee has a price on the hot row. In a test on PostgreSQL 18, a committing transaction kept its row lock while it waited for the standby, so the standby round trip adds to the lock-hold time h. Raft also says nothing about payment compensation, which lies outside any replicated log. Avoid long quotations, and do not let a bibliography stand in for your own transfer reasoning.

## From course work to professional practice

Present the portfolio as course work on fictional cases, and explain the experiments you ran and the limits of their models. A reviewer trusts a portfolio more when it states plainly which results came from your own experiments and which came from worked examples.

Real architecture responsibility also means working with stakeholders under real operating constraints, which a book can prepare you for but not replace. Choose a next experience that closes a specific gap: take part in a design review, help maintain a set of decision records, trace a real incident across several dependencies, assist with a migration rehearsal, or review an integration contract with an experienced colleague. Record what you learned and how it changed one of your earlier assumptions.

The next book in this series moves from one solution to an organisation's portfolio of systems, its capabilities, decision rights and transformation programme. Enterprise scope changes the scale and the stakeholders, while the need to make assumptions and evidence visible stays the same.

## Exercises

16.1 Compare the role of durable state in two studios. Explain one shared principle and one important difference in who holds authority.

16.2 Apply the rubric to your first and your revised dossier for one studio. Identify the evidence behind the largest improvement, rather than only reporting a higher score.

16.3 Write a ninety-day development plan with one technical, one stakeholder and one operational objective. Give each an observable sign of progress.

16.4 Using Ongaro and Ousterhout's description of Raft, explain what a Raft-replicated log guarantees about a committed entry after a leader failover. Then compare Aster's PostgreSQL primary with asynchronous replication and with `synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (s1, s2)'`. For each, state what happens to a reservation confirmed just before the failover, what it costs on the hot row, and what is left to the application, such as a client whose reservation request timed out.

16.5 During your defence, the examiner states that, two days after Orion's public switch, the audit log on the new order store shows 40 status updates from an unknown database account at 00:30. Within five minutes, list the assumptions and decisions in the Orion dossier that this puts at risk, and propose immediate and follow-up actions.

## Key points

- A portfolio shows decisions, evidence and revision, not just finished diagrams.
- One rubric, applied consistently, lets different but well-argued designs earn equal credit.
- Principles such as durable intent transfer between contexts, while authority and external effects change.
- A good defence identifies which guarantees a new fact puts at risk, without inventing contracts.
- Course work prepares you to contribute to architecture practice; supervised experience and feedback build professional judgement.
