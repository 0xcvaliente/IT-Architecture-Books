# How to use the design studio

This book teaches architecture by doing it. You work through four design studios. Each studio gives you a brief, asks for a first design, then adds measurements and new facts that make you revise it. The aim is a professional habit: state what the design promises, show the mechanism that keeps the promise, and test the promise before anyone relies on it.

The book builds on the foundations of the first book in this series, such as quality scenarios, transactions, messaging and data modelling. You do not need that book open: whenever a chapter relies on an earlier idea, it recaps it first.

## The four studios

| Studio | Organisation | Central problem | Chapters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Integration | Meridian Service Portal | Hand service requests to three different CRMs without losing or duplicating them | 3 to 5 |
| Transactions | Aster Retail | Sell 2,000 units of one product to 500 checkout attempts per second | 6 to 8 |
| Data | Beacon Analytics | Publish a daily dashboard that stays explainable when data arrives late or changes | 9 to 11 |
| Modernisation | Orion | Move order management off a legacy platform with one write authority at every moment | 12 to 14 |

Chapters 1 and 2 teach the method: how to argue for a design and how to gather evidence for it. Chapter 15 covers design reviews, and Chapter 16 brings your work together in a portfolio.

Meridian, Aster, Beacon and Orion are fictional organisations. Their workloads, budgets, provider contracts, datasets and experiment results are teaching examples, not industry benchmarks. Each studio states its own assumptions, which apply only within that studio.

## How a studio works

Every studio follows the same cycle:

1. Read the brief: the business problem, the workload, the constraints and the open questions.
2. Produce a first design before reading the worked analysis, and keep it, so that you can see later what you learned.
3. Study the worked analysis, which compares at least two credible alternatives.
4. Run the experiment: a calculation, a small Python model or a SQL listing.
5. Respond to the change card. A **change card** introduces one new fact part-way through the studio, for example a vendor cutting its rate limit, and asks you to revise the design and record why.
6. Assess your dossier against the rubric in Chapter 16.

The output of a studio is an **architecture dossier**: the connected set of documents that explains a design and the reasons for it. {FIG:dossier_map} shows how its parts link. Every item carries a stable identifier, such as Q3 for a quality scenario or ADR-M05 for a decision record, so that a reviewer can follow a requirement to the decision that addresses it and the test that supports it.

The worked designs show one sound answer. An alternative earns equal credit when it protects the same business rules and explains its cost, operation and remaining uncertainty.

## What you will produce

- Four studio dossiers.
- Four experiment records, each with a question, a decision rule and a result.
- Four research critiques of 400 to 600 words, each applying one primary source to a studio.
- A final portfolio, prepared for an oral defence.

## An eight-week study route

Plan five study days a week of four to five hours each, about 160 to 200 hours in total. Spend roughly a quarter of each day reading, half on modelling and experiments, and the rest on critique and revision.

| Week | Reading and focus | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chapters 1 and 2 | Argument method and a first experiment |
| 2 | Chapters 3 to 5 | Integration dossier and recovery analysis |
| 3 | Chapters 6 to 8 | Transaction dossier and failure tests |
| 4 | Chapters 9 to 11 | Data dossier and publication design |
| 5 | Chapters 12 to 14 | Migration dossier and cutover plan |
| 6 | Revisit all four studios | Alternative designs and research critiques |
| 7 | Chapter 15 | Design review and revised decisions |
| 8 | Chapter 16 | Final portfolio and oral defence |

## Listings, templates and sources

Listings use Python 3.10 or newer with the standard library, and SQL. The PostgreSQL listings were run on PostgreSQL 18, and the features they rely on, such as `FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` (available since PostgreSQL 9.5) and `EXECUTE FUNCTION` in triggers (since PostgreSQL 11), work in every currently supported release. Wherever the text reports how a statement behaved, for example under concurrency, that behaviour was observed by running it. The Python listings, including those that use SQLite, run anywhere Python runs. Each is printed with its exact output, and none needs a cloud account or network access. The final chapter, "Experiments, templates and research tasks", contains the dossier templates and extensions for each experiment.

References are numbered in IEEE style in order of first citation. Research papers support general ideas; standards and official documentation support specific protocol and product behaviour. Product behaviour changes over time, so check the documentation for the exact service, version and configuration before relying on it.
