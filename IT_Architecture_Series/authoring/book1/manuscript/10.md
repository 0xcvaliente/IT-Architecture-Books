# 10 Messaging, events and reliable handoffs

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Distinguish commands, events and queries, and choose between synchronous calls, work queues and event streams.
- Explain at-most-once and at-least-once delivery, and why exactly-once processing holds only within a stated boundary.
- Design a transactional outbox and an inbox for a reliable handoff.
- Calculate a retry schedule with backoff, a cap and a limit, and design the path to manual review.
- Model business state and delivery state as separate state machines, and design tests for their transitions.

## Commands, events and queries

Messages carry three kinds of intent:

- A **command** asks a receiver to do something: “create an ERP work order”.
- An **event** reports something that has happened: “service case approved”.
- A **query** asks for information: “what is the status of submission 7f3a9c2e?”

Name messages after their intent, because consumers make different assumptions about each. A command has one intended handler and can be refused. An event can have many consumers, and none of them can refuse that it happened.

A **synchronous call** suits a caller that needs an immediate result and can tolerate the provider's response time and availability. **Asynchronous messaging** separates acceptance from later processing. It absorbs bursts and short outages, but it brings delayed state, queues to operate and more complex messages to users. Introduce messaging only when the business can tolerate the delay and someone owns the work that fails.

## Queues and streams

Two broker designs dominate:

- A **work queue** hands each task to one of several consumers. Once a consumer acknowledges the task, it is gone from the queue.
- An **event stream** keeps an ordered, retained log of records, divided into partitions. Each consumer tracks its own position and can replay from an earlier one.

Many products support both behaviours, so read the actual semantics. RabbitMQ, for example, distinguishes two acknowledgements {REF:rabbit}. A **publisher confirm** tells the producer that the broker has taken responsibility for a message. A **consumer acknowledgement** tells the broker that a consumer has finished with it. Neither tells anyone whether an external side effect, such as an email or a CRM call, happened exactly once.

Kafka stores records in partitioned logs and offers transactions within stated boundaries {REF:kafka}. Ordering holds within a partition, not across partitions. Its documentation explains that its exactly-once guarantees cover reading from and writing to Kafka itself; effects on external systems need their own design.

## Delivery guarantees and side effects

Delivery guarantees describe what happens when something fails between sending and acknowledging:

- **At-most-once** delivery never redelivers, so work can be lost.
- **At-least-once** delivery redelivers unacknowledged work, so work can be repeated.
- **Exactly-once processing** is a property of a specific system and transaction boundary, not of a network.

For example, a worker sends an email and crashes before acknowledging the queue message. The broker redelivers the message and the customer receives a second email. The broker cannot know whether the email went out. Protect the side effect instead, with a provider-supported idempotency key, a durable record of the operation, or an agreed tolerance for rare duplicates plus reconciliation.

A deduplication table needs the right transaction boundary. Recording “processed” before the business update loses work if the worker crashes between the two. Recording it afterwards repeats work after a crash, unless both writes commit in one transaction.

> **Common mistake.** Acknowledging a message as soon as it arrives, before the work it describes has committed. A crash in between loses the work silently, because the broker will never redeliver it.

## The transactional outbox

The **dual-write problem** arises when a service must change its database and also send a message. If it commits and then crashes before sending, the message is lost. If it sends and then fails to commit, the message describes something that never happened.

The **transactional outbox** solves this. The service writes the business change and an outbox record describing the message in the same local database transaction. A separate worker reads committed outbox records and delivers them {REF:richardson,outbox}. Because both rows commit together, every stored request has a durable handoff record.

{FIG:outbox} shows Meridian's version. The outbox record holds an event identifier, the request identifier, the event type, a schema version, the creation time and just enough data to perform the handoff. Avoid copying the whole request into every message when a smaller payload or an authorised lookup will do.

The worker can still deliver a handoff twice: it may send, crash and resend. The receiver must therefore handle duplicates. Meridian's worker sends the submission ID as the idempotency key, so a repeated handoff returns the existing CRM case instead of creating a new one.

An **inbox**, or processed-event table, gives the same protection to a consumer. The consumer stores each event's unique identifier in the same transaction as the state change the event causes. A redelivered event then finds its identifier already present and is skipped. If the consumer goes on to call another external system, the external side-effect problem appears again and needs its own design.

## Retry policy

Retry only failures that may be temporary, such as timeouts, `503` responses and rate limiting. A rejected schema or a permission failure will not succeed after waiting. Good retry policies have four parts:

- **exponential backoff**: each wait is longer than the last, so a struggling dependency gets room to recover;
- **jitter**: each wait is randomised, so that many workers do not retry in synchrony;
- a **cap** on the longest single wait;
- a **limit** on the total time or attempts, after which a person takes over.

Meridian's worker waits 2 seconds after the first failure and doubles each wait, capped at 15 minutes, before applying jitter. Ignoring jitter and request time:

| Attempt | Wait before it | Time since first attempt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | – | 0 s |
| 2 | 2 s | 2 s |
| 3 | 4 s | 6 s |
| 10 | 512 s | 1,022 s (about 17 minutes) |
| 11 | 900 s (capped) | 1,922 s (about 32 minutes) |
| 32 | 900 s | 20,822 s (about 5.8 hours) |

Attempt 11 arrives after the thirty-minute outage that Meridian must survive. Attempt 32, at about 5.8 hours, is the last automatic one, because attempt 33 would fall after the six-hour limit; if attempt 32 fails, the handoff moves to `manual_review`. Every automatic retry therefore falls inside the CRM's idempotency window described in Chapter 8.

Move work that keeps failing into a visible state or a **dead-letter queue**. Keep the event identifier, error category, retry history and safe diagnostic context. Define who reviews it, when it may be replayed, and how a replay avoids duplicate side effects.

Monitor age as well as count. Ten critical handoffs that are three hours old matter more than a thousand that arrived a minute ago during a burst.

## Ordering and duplicate events

Messages can arrive out of order. Suppose the CRM sends “approved” and then “resolved”, and the portal receives them in reverse. A naive consumer overwrites “resolved” with “approved”. Three defences help:

- a **domain version** or sequence number on each event, so the consumer ignores anything older than what it holds;
- a **partition key** that keeps related events in order, such as the service case ID;
- **transition rules** that reject an impossible move, such as from resolved back to approved.

Event time alone is insufficient, because clocks differ and events are delayed. Choose the smallest ordering boundary the business needs. Ordering per service case is cheap. Ordering per tenant would funnel a large tenant's events through one partition, creating a hot spot and limiting parallelism.

## Separate business state from delivery state

Two different things progress after a submission, and each needs its own state machine:

- **Business state**: where the service case stands. The portal shows “Received” until the handoff is confirmed. After that, the CRM owns the case and the portal projects its status, such as “Under review”, “Approved” or “Resolved”.
- **Delivery state**: where the handoff to the CRM stands. It takes exactly five values: `pending`, `in_flight`, `confirmed`, `retry_due` and `manual_review`.

FIGURE states | Business progress and handoff delivery are two separate state machines with different owners.

Store the two separately, as in {FIG:states}, so that one cannot overwrite the other. For example, a case can be approved in the CRM while ERP has no work order yet, and a handoff can fail once while the customer's request still stands. The customer's view follows the delivery state:

- While the handoff is `pending`, `in_flight` or `retry_due`, the customer sees “Received”.
- If it stays undelivered beyond the agreed delay, or reaches `manual_review`, the customer sees “Received, awaiting service team” rather than an error.
- Once it is `confirmed`, the customer sees the CRM's case status.

Nothing is silently dropped: every handoff ends in `confirmed` or waits in `manual_review` for a person.

Each transition has a source state, a trigger, a guard, a durable effect and an authorised actor:

| From | To | Trigger and guard | Durable effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `pending` | `in_flight` | Worker claims the record with a conditional update | Lease holder and expiry recorded |
| `in_flight` | `confirmed` | CRM returns a case, or a duplicate-key reply naming one | CRM case reference stored |
| `in_flight` | `retry_due` | Uncertain or temporary failure, an expired credential, or the lease expires | Attempt count and next attempt time stored |
| `retry_due` | `in_flight` | Next attempt time reached and claim succeeds | New lease recorded |
| `in_flight` | `manual_review` | CRM rejects the handoff, or six hours have passed | Reason and history kept for review |
| `manual_review` | `retry_due` | An operator approves a replay after checking the CRM | Operator identity recorded |

A stale lease holder must not overwrite a newer attempt's result, so every update includes the expected state and lease in its `WHERE` clause, as in Chapter 6. A timeout leads to `retry_due`; it never erases the durable record.

For each transition, test three awkward cases: the same event delivered twice, an event arriving in an unexpected state, and a restart between the external action and the local update. This small exercise in state-space reasoning finds more defects than testing each endpoint once.

## Sagas and compensation

A **saga** coordinates a long-running business process as a sequence of local transactions {REF:saga}. If a later step fails, **compensation** performs a new business action that counteracts an earlier step. Compensation is not a rollback: a sent email cannot be unsent, and a charged fee may need a refund with its own rules.

For example, after approval the work-order integration creates the ERP work order, and ERP then reserves a technician slot and the parts. If the parts cannot be reserved, the process may wait for a person to choose an alternative rather than deleting the earlier records. Model each state and name who handles each exception. Chapter 22 develops sagas fully for an order process.

## Key points

- Commands, events and queries carry different intent; use messaging only when the business accepts delay and someone owns failed work.
- Broker guarantees stop at the broker; protect external side effects with idempotency keys, operation records or reconciliation.
- A transactional outbox stores the business change and the message intent in one local transaction; an inbox deduplicates on the consumer side.
- Retries need backoff, jitter, a cap and a limit that fits inside the provider's idempotency window, followed by visible manual review.
- Keep business state and delivery state in separate state machines, with explicit transitions, guards and tests.
