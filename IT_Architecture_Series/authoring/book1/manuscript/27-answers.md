# Exercise answers and assessment guidance

Attempt each exercise before reading its answer. Numerical answers use the assumptions stated in the question and show the working. Design answers give one defensible approach and the criteria for judging alternatives. A different design earns full credit when it meets the requirements and explains its costs and failure behaviour.

## Chapter 1

**1.1** Choosing a database is architectural when it sets important data guarantees, platform dependencies, team skills, operating costs or migration constraints. For example, choosing PostgreSQL as the system of record for a new platform fixes the transaction model, the operations skills needed and the cost of moving later. Adding a small component to an approved PostgreSQL platform, using the standard managed instance, is a routine implementation choice. The distinction rests on consequence and reversibility, not on the product name.

**1.2** A suitable brief: “Meridian wants to cut duplicate records and manual effort between service request submission and ERP work order creation. Agents currently copy approved cases into ERP by hand and cannot trace failed handoffs. The existing CRM must remain for two years. Six engineers will build and run the first release. It is not yet confirmed how the CRM behaves when it receives the same external reference twice.” The brief states an outcome, an obstacle, two constraints and an unknown, and names no solution.

**1.3** Ask which outcomes “modern” should deliver: independent releases, higher scale, easier integration or better maintainability. Turn the chosen outcomes into measurable requirements. Compare a modular monolith with a small set of services against them, the team's capability and the measured workload, and propose a prototype or review for the biggest uncertainty. Marking: credit an answer that takes the concern seriously, converts it into criteria and sets up a comparison. Deduct for dismissing the stakeholder or for treating the requested pattern as a requirement.

**1.4** (a) Performance efficiency (time behaviour). (b) Reliability (fault tolerance and recoverability). (c) Security (confidentiality). (d) Maintainability (modifiability). One conflicting pair is (a) and (c): checking the agent's business unit and customer scopes on every request adds lookups to the receipt path, and caching the entitlements to save time delays the effect of a revocation. Another is (a) and (b): replicating each commit synchronously to protect accepted requests adds latency. Credit any pair whose conflict is explained by a mechanism.

**1.5** The statement names no problem or requirement, and “future-proof” cannot be tested. It gives no alternatives, no consequences and no review trigger. It ignores scale: 100,000 requests a year is about 0.0032 per second, and a streaming platform would add substantial operational work for six engineers. A reviewable version: “Deliver CRM handoffs from a transactional outbox with a database-backed worker, because a thirty-minute CRM outage must lose no accepted request and the peak is 20 submissions per second for ten minutes. Consequences: the team runs a worker, a retry policy and reconciliation, and no streaming platform. Revisit if three or more consumers need the same events, or if sustained handoff volume exceeds what one worker can deliver within the one-minute target.” Marking: one third for the critique, one third for a reason tied to requirements, one third for consequences and a measurable trigger.

## Chapter 2

**2.1** One acceptable requirement: “When an authenticated service agent requests a service request whose business unit is not assigned to them, or whose customer organisation is outside their authorised set, the service denies access and records an audit event. Automated tests cover viewing, search, attachment download and export.” Name the policy owner and the expected response. “Uses encryption” describes one possible control, not an access requirement.

**2.2** (a) 43,200 × (1 − 0.9995) = 43,200 × 0.0005 = 21.6 minutes.

(b) The peak contains 20 × 600 = 12,000 submissions, and 2 percent of them, 240, fail. The month has 8,000 + 12,000 = 20,000 eligible requests, of which 19,760 succeed: 19,760 ÷ 20,000 = 98.8 percent. A 99.95 percent objective allows 0.0005 × 20,000 = 10 failures, so with 240 failures the month misses it by a wide margin.

(c) At the same 99.95 percent threshold, the time-based objective is met. The service answered 98 percent of requests during the peak, and even if all ten minutes counted as downtime, 10 minutes is within the 21.6-minute allowance. Requests concentrate in the incident, so the request-based measure reflects what customers experienced. The two measures need agreed rules for what counts as “down” and for the denominator.

**2.3** Ask what counts as accepted data, which failures the target covers (a server, a zone, a region, an operator error), and whether zero loss includes a regional disaster. Ask what starts and stops the recovery clock, which functions must be back within five minutes, whether manual steps and dependencies count, and what budget and staff are available. Separate the RPO (zero) from the RTO (five minutes) and plan recovery tests for each. A synchronous local write alone does not give zero loss in every disaster.

**2.4** Source: the CRM vendor's service or the network path to it. Stimulus: the CRM becomes unavailable for thirty minutes. Environment: normal operation, possibly during an incident peak. Artefact: the outbox, the handoff worker and the receipt API. Response: the portal keeps issuing receipts, holds handoffs in `pending` or `retry_due`, shows “Received, awaiting service team” once a handoff passes the agreed delay, and delivers every handoff after recovery using the submission ID as idempotency key. Response measure: no accepted request lost, no duplicate CRM cases, and every queued handoff `confirmed` within a stated time after recovery, such as 60 minutes. Test: in a test environment, block the CRM endpoint for thirty minutes while submitting a known load, for example 1,000 requests, then restore it. Compare portal receipts with CRM cases by submission ID: the key sets must match exactly, with no duplicates, and the drain time must meet the measure.

**2.5** R03 is mandatory, so option A is excluded whatever its score. A weighted total hides a mandatory failure by averaging it with strengths elsewhere. Run the comparison in two stages: first remove every option that fails a mandatory requirement, then score the remaining options, recording the basis of each score and testing how the ranking changes when weights change. If option A's supplier offers a credible change that meets R03, the requirement owner may approve re-entry after that change is verified.

## Chapter 4

**4.1** More instances open more database connections and increase contention for a resource that is already the bottleneck. They can also queue more work and use more memory. Measure connection-pool wait time, active queries, locks, query latency and request concurrency. Fix the limiting query or capacity, bound concurrency, or apply backpressure before adding API replicas.

**4.2** Use 10.20.10.0/24 for ingress components (addresses 10.20.10.0 to 10.20.10.255), 10.20.20.0/24 for the API and worker runtime, and 10.20.30.0/24 for private data endpoints. All three lie within 10.20.0.0/16 and differ in the third octet, so they do not overlap. A subnet assignment neither grants nor denies access: routes decide where packets can go, and firewall rules, service policies and identity controls decide what is allowed. Account for the platform's reserved addresses in each subnet.

**4.3** A resolver or client may still hold the old answer until its TTL expires, or an existing connection may remain open to the old endpoint. Some clients also cache answers longer than the TTL. Keep both endpoints working during a planned overlap, lower the TTL well before the change, monitor traffic on the old endpoint, and retire it only when that traffic has stopped. Lowering the TTL after caches are filled does not change answers already cached.

**4.4** (a) A /24 fixes 8 more bits than a /16, so the /16 holds 2 to the power 8 = 256 subnets of size /24. (b) A /26 leaves 32 − 26 = 6 bits, so it contains 2 to the power 6 = 64 addresses. (c) 64 − 5 = 59 usable addresses.

**4.5** HTTPS protects data in transit on the segments where it is used. The load balancer usually terminates TLS, so the internal segments need their own protection. HTTPS does not authenticate the end user's permissions, decide which tenant's requests an agent may read, protect data at rest, secure the administrative path or control egress. By the end-to-end argument, guarantees such as “only authorised agents read this request” can be enforced only by the application at the endpoint. A better statement lists the control at each boundary: TLS at the edge and internally, authorisation in the API, encryption at rest, a separate management path and egress rules.

## Chapter 5

**5.1** Length, required-field and formatting checks can be pure functions, as in `validate_submission`. Permission checks need trusted identity and policy. Asset eligibility may need current contract data. Persistence, outbox writes and vendor calls are effects. A good design passes trusted data into pure policy functions, but must still ensure the data is current enough and that the final write preserves the rule.

**5.2** Test that a new key creates one request; that an identical retry by the same submitter returns the same receipt and submission ID; that the same key with different content is rejected; that the same key from another submitter creates a separate request; and that two concurrent attempts with the same key create one request. For a lost response, commit the first attempt, discard its response, retry, and check that one request exists. Two sequential lookups in a dictionary do not prove concurrency safety; state the database constraint the guarantee depends on.

**5.3** Shared code can remove duplication for stable, genuinely common rules. A broad common module also couples unrelated domains, forces coordinated releases and blurs ownership. Prefer small shared technical utilities and carefully governed domain concepts. If two contexts use the same word with different meanings, keep separate models and translate at the boundary.

**5.4** (a) Only the adapter function and its tests change; the rest of the portal never sees `caseRef`. (b) Only the eligibility rule and its tests change; the adapter is untouched. (c) The adapter cannot absorb this change. An `UNCERTAIN` outcome is no longer safe to retry, so the worker's retry policy, the reconciliation process (a lookup by external reference before each retry), the runbook, assumption A01 and the related ADR all need revisiting. Information hiding hides representations and formats; it cannot hide a change in the guarantees a component offers.

**5.5** (a) Invalid input: reject with a field-level error and do not retry. (b) Not authorised: deny, perhaps with “not found” so the request's existence is not revealed, and record an audit event. (c) Conflicting state: reject with a conflict response that includes the current state. (d) Temporary dependency failure: no customer-facing error; the worker retries later with the same idempotency key under the backoff policy. (e) Internal defect: return a generic error with a correlation identifier, alert the team and fix the code; automatic retries rarely help.

## Chapter 6

**6.1** A foreign key on `asset_id` alone proves that some asset `a-9` exists, but not that it belongs to the request's tenant. The composite key requires the pair (`t-017`, `a-9`) to exist in `asset`, so a request in `t-017` cannot reference `t-023`'s asset. The constraint does not authenticate users or enforce other access rules.

**6.2** Under PostgreSQL Read Committed, editor A's update changes version 7 to 8 and commits. Editor B's `UPDATE ... WHERE version = 7`, if it was waiting for A's row lock, re-checks the condition against the new row and updates zero rows. B's application reports a conflict, rereads the note and lets the user decide again. Under Repeatable Read or Serializable, B's transaction, whose snapshot saw version 7, fails with a serialisation failure (SQLSTATE `40001`) and must be retried from the start {REF:isolation}. Simply resubmitting with version 8 would overwrite A's change without anyone seeing it.

**6.3** First, tune the queries and indexes: cheap, but it reduces rather than removes the contention. Second, run the dashboard on a read replica: it isolates the load, at the cost of replication lag and another database to run. Third, build a separate analytical store fed by a pipeline: the cleanest separation, at the cost of transformation, freshness and reconciliation work. Choose by scale, required freshness and ownership; a warehouse is not always necessary.

**6.4** The rule “at least one doctor on call” concerns the pair of rows, while each check constraint sees only its own row, and “off call” is a valid value for either row on its own. Locking a common shift row, or using `FOR UPDATE` on the rows read, makes the second transaction wait until the first commits, so its decision sees the change; every writer must follow the protocol. Serializable isolation lets both transactions run and aborts one with SQLSTATE `40001`; the application must retry the whole read-decide-write unit and report when retries run out. Locking reduces concurrency predictably; Serializable keeps more concurrency but needs retry handling everywhere. Neither protects an external effect such as a notification. Credit a counter with a check constraint if every writer maintains it.

**6.5** Silo (a database per tenant) gives the strongest isolation and per-tenant backup and restore, but 600 databases multiply cost and operations. Bridge (separate tables per tenant in one database) gives some separation and allows schema variation, but 600 sets of tables multiply migrations. Pool (shared tables, tenant identifier and RLS) is cheapest and simplest to operate, with risks of noisy neighbours and cross-tenant leaks that RLS and tenant-qualified keys reduce. Recommendation: keep the pool for 599 tenants and give the large customer a silo, meaning its own database with the same schema and code, chosen through a tenant-to-database directory. Consequences: a routing layer, migrations run on two databases, and cross-tenant reports must combine both. Confirm the contract's exact demand, such as separate encryption keys or region, before building.

**6.6** The most likely cause is that the application connects as the tables' owner, or as a superuser or role with `BYPASSRLS`, all of which skip the policies. Create a dedicated application role that owns no tables, lacks `BYPASSRLS`, and holds only the privileges it needs; optionally add `FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY` so that even the owner is filtered. Run the integration tests as that role. RLS enforces only the tenant boundary; the application must still enforce that a customer reads only requests they submitted, unless delegated, and that an agent's business unit matches the request.

**6.7** Queries that carry the tenant identifier, such as a customer's request list and the receipt transaction, stay on one shard. An agent's work queue covering several tenants becomes a scatter-gather query across up to eight shards, with merged sorting and harder pagination. Hash placement cannot move one large tenant on its own, and that tenant may make its shard a hot spot. Moving from eight to nine servers with `hash mod n` relocates most tenants. A better design uses a directory, or many fixed logical partitions mapped to servers, so a large tenant can be moved or given its own database. Agent work queues can come from a separate read model maintained by events.

## Chapter 7

**7.1** Use one row per request per status interval, or one row per status change with a defined method for deriving intervals. Include the request identifier, status, event time or effective period, and a sequence number to handle duplicates and late events. A customer has many requests, and each request passes through many statuses, so one row per customer loses the detail needed.

**7.2** Check required keys, allowed statuses and transitions, plausible timestamps, and reconciliation of source and target key sets. A uniqueness check on event identifiers, or a count grouped by the declared grain, reveals duplicates even when the total row count matches. Compare batch identifiers and missing-key sets as well as totals, quarantine invalid records and show the effect on reports.

**7.3** Ask what quality purpose needs the prompts, what personal data they contain, who can access them, whether a vendor processes them, and how correction and deletion work. A bounded alternative keeps a small, minimised evaluation dataset under an approved retention period, with access control and de-identification where possible. Keep operational diagnostics separate from model evaluation, and have the retention policy approved for the actual processing.

**7.4** The join produces five rows for r-1 and three for r-2, so the report shows 120 × 5 + 80 × 3 = 600 + 240 = 840. The correct total is 120 + 80 = 200. Keep the fee in a fact table whose grain is one row per request, and keep status measures in a separate status-event fact table. Combine them only at a common grain, such as per request or per day.

**7.5** For names as they were at the time, use a type 2 dimension: one row for “Field Services North” valid until 30 June, and a new row for “Northern Service” valid from 1 July, both with the same durable unit identifier but different surrogate keys. Each fact points to the row valid when it happened. For current names, add a type 1 attribute, such as `current_name`, set to “Northern Service” on both rows, or join through the durable identifier to the current row.

**7.6** The output side is unchanged: 30 million tokens × EUR 8 per million = EUR 240. Input becomes 100,000 × 3,000 = 300 million tokens for the basic prompt plus 40,000 × 12,000 = 480 million tokens of attachment text, 780 million in total, which costs 780 × EUR 2 = EUR 1,560. The year therefore costs 1,560 + 240 = EUR 1,800, more than double the EUR 840 of the worked example. Reasons to retrieve selected passages instead: (1) latency grows with prompt length, and a 15,000-token prompt takes noticeably longer to process; (2) every extra page is untrusted text, so the surface for indirect prompt injection grows; (3) more personal data is sent to the model provider and may appear in logs; (4) relevant facts are diluted among irrelevant ones, which tends to worsen summaries, and long attachments may exceed the model's context limit. Credit any three that are tied to a mechanism.

**7.7** Retrieval runs under the agent's business unit and customer scopes, so the prompt contains only this tenant's request, its attachments and its asset history; other customers' requests are never retrieved. The prompt marks the attachment as content to summarise, which may or may not stop the model following the instruction. If the model obeys, it has no other customers' data to add and no tools with which to fetch any, so the worst outcome is a misleading summary of this one request. The portal escapes the output, the agent reviews the draft against its cited passages before use, and the adversarial test set should include this attachment. The control that must hold is authorisation at retrieval: a model cannot disclose content it never received. Detection, such as flagging attachments that contain instruction-like text, is a useful extra layer but cannot replace it.

## Chapter 8

**8.1** A response can stay valid JSON with the same field type while its meaning changes. If `status: "received"` once meant “stored by the portal” and later meant “confirmed by the CRM”, every client still parses it, but clients that act on it now behave differently. Document the meaning of every state and treat a change of meaning as a contract change.

**8.2** Scope the key to the tenant and the authenticated submitter, and store it with the submitter, a hash of the relevant payload and the portal-assigned submission ID, in the same transaction as the request. A unique constraint on (`tenant_id`, `submitted_by`, `submission_key`) decides concurrent attempts. An identical retry by the same submitter returns the original receipt and submission ID; the same key with different content is rejected with `422`, and a repeat that arrives while the first attempt is still in progress receives `409` and retries later; the same key from a different submitter is a separate request. Take the submitter from the verified identity, never from the request body. State the retention period and what happens after expiry, protect the stored result with the same authorisation as the request, and provide reconciliation for very late retries. The key is never an access credential.

**8.3** An unbounded response can exhaust database, memory, network and client resources, and it can expose far more personal data than any user needs. Require authorisation, cursor pagination with a stable order, limited fields and rate limits. For large exports, use an asynchronous job that produces a controlled, expiring download with an audit trail, and state the snapshot semantics.

**8.4** (a) Messaging: duplicates or out-of-order events, handled with the service case ID as idempotency key and ordering per case. (b) Remote procedure invocation: the portal depends on ERP's availability and latency, reduced by a cached read model labelled with its freshness. (c) File transfer: stale data, file integrity and the exposure of sensitive data in transit and at rest. (d) Shared database: couples analytics to the portal's schema, adds load to the transactional database and bypasses the portal's access rules; an extract into an analytical store is better.

**8.5** No. Backward compatibility requires consumers using version 3 to read records written with version 2. Those records have no `priority`, and version 3 declares it required with no default, so the registry rejects the change. If it were accepted, consumers upgraded to version 3 would fail on older events, for example during a replay or while some producers still emit version 2. A compatible change adds `priority` as an optional field with a default, such as `normal`. A truly required new field needs a new event version with a planned migration.

## Chapter 9

**9.1** A valid signature establishes only that a trusted issuer created the token and that it is unaltered. An ID token's audience is the client application, and its purpose is to report the sign-in. An API must accept only access tokens issued for it, and must validate issuer, audience, lifetime and scopes under its access-token contract.

**9.2** Permit download only when the caller is authenticated; the attachment belongs to a request that the caller may read under the policy (the submitter, a delegated colleague, or an agent with the right business unit and customer scope); the caller's role permits download; and the attachment has passed scanning and is released. Read the request's tenant, business unit and submitter from the database rather than from the client. If the download uses a temporary URL, issue it only after this check and keep its scope and lifetime short.

**9.3** Access tokens already issued may stay valid until they expire, the API may cache permissions, or the directory change may not yet have reached the identity provider. Logging out of the browser does not revoke tokens. Choose token lifetimes, introspection or per-request entitlement checks, and cache expiry to meet a stated revocation target. Faster revocation increases the load on, and dependence upon, the policy service.

**9.4** The missing predicate: the caller is the submitter of the request, or holds a valid organisation-wide delegation for T1; the attachment belongs to that request and is released. Test 1: P, with no delegation, requests Q's attachment and must be denied. Test 2: after P is granted the delegation, the same call must succeed. Add a control: a T2 user with a T2 delegation must be denied. A tenant-only policy fails test 1; a policy that denies everything fails test 2.

**9.5** (a) If access depends on claims inside 60-minute tokens, a removed agent keeps access for up to an hour, which misses the target. (b) With 5-minute tokens, the identity provider re-evaluates assignments at each refresh, giving a worst case of about five minutes plus the time for the directory change to reach it; this just meets the target if refresh checks current assignments, at the cost of more token traffic. (c) A per-request entitlement lookup with a 60-second cache gives a worst case of about one minute, independent of token lifetime, at the cost of a dependency on the policy store for every request, which needs high availability and a fail-closed rule. Recommended: (c) for business-unit and customer scopes, with moderate token lifetimes for authentication.

## Chapter 10

**10.1** The broker did not receive an acknowledgement, so it redelivers the message and the update runs twice. Record the event identifier in an inbox table in the same transaction as the consumer's state change; the redelivered message then finds its identifier and is skipped. An external side effect outside that transaction still needs its own idempotency key or reconciliation.

**10.2** Retry timeouts, rate limiting and `5xx` replies with exponential backoff from 2 seconds, doubling, capped at 15 minutes, with jitter, always sending the submission ID as idempotency key. Move a handoff straight to `manual_review` when the CRM rejects it as invalid, and after six hours of failed retries, which keeps every automatic retry inside the CRM's 24-hour key window. Alert when any handoff stays unconfirmed for more than ten minutes in normal operation, since the status target is one minute. Before a manual replay, search the CRM by external reference; record who approved the replay and why.

**10.3** Find the exact transaction boundary of the guarantee, the failure assumptions, the producer and consumer settings, the retention period and the behaviour during replay. The guarantee usually covers reading and writing within the broker, not the ERP system's write. Check whether ERP enforces the service case ID as an idempotency key under concurrency, how long it remembers keys, and how an uncertain response is reconciled. Never turn a broker-local guarantee into a promise about external business effects.

**10.4** (a) The waits before attempts 2 to 11 are 1, 2, 4, …, 512 seconds, which sum to 1,023 seconds, so attempt 11 happens at about 17 minutes. (b) The next wait would be 1,024 seconds, capped at 600, so later attempts come at 1,023 + 600k seconds. Two hours is 7,200 seconds, and (7,200 − 1,023) ÷ 600 ≈ 10.3, so k runs from 1 to 10, giving attempts 12 to 21. Attempt 21 happens at 7,023 seconds and attempt 22 would come at 7,623 seconds, after the limit. There are 21 attempts in total.

**10.5** Trigger: the CRM returns a case reference, or a duplicate-key reply naming an existing case. Guard: the row is `in_flight` and the reply belongs to the attempt that currently holds the lease, enforced by a conditional update on state and lease token. Durable effect: in one local transaction, set the state to `confirmed`, store the CRM case reference, append to the attempt history and clear the lease. Actor: the worker holding the lease, under its workload identity. Tests: (1) process the same confirmation twice; the second update changes no rows and raises no error. (2) Let a lease expire, start a newer attempt, then deliver the old attempt's late success; the conditional update fails, the late reply is logged, the newer attempt receives a duplicate-key reply naming the same case, and the row ends `confirmed` with exactly one CRM case. (3) Crash after the CRM succeeds but before the local update; after lease expiry the retry receives the duplicate-key reply and confirms, again with one CRM case.

## Chapter 11

**11.1** A modular monolith can enforce clear interfaces and data ownership while using local calls and one controlled deployment. Poorly designed microservices can share mutable data, depend on synchronous call chains and need coordinated releases. Deployment separation alone does not reduce semantic or operational coupling; compare how one change spreads through each design.

**11.2** A defensible start is a modular application with a separate worker process and managed data services. It suits a small team, local submission transactions and moderate demand. Reject a large microservice estate because independent ownership and scaling needs are not yet demonstrated, and reject synchronous CRM creation because a CRM outage must not stop submission. Review trigger: revisit the worker boundary if a representative peak repeatedly breaks the one-minute status target after query tuning and at the permitted worker capacity. First establish whether the limit is local processing or the vendor's API; extracting a service does not lift a vendor rate limit.

**11.3** One service per table usually splits a business rule across remote calls and turns ordinary transactions into distributed ones. It increases network traffic, the number of contracts and the need for coordinated change. Examine which capability owns the data, which rules must commit together and which parts truly change independently. Tables are storage structures, not service boundaries.

**11.4** The customer-portal team owns the web interface, submission API, receipts and status projection. The integrations team owns the CRM handoff worker and adapter, the CRM status consumer and the work-order integration. Strengthen the module boundary between the two areas first, with separate ownership and tests; a separate deployable for integrations becomes reasonable once release cadences differ. Avoid both teams owning the outbox table: either the portal team keeps it and publishes handoffs through a relay to a queue that the integrations team consumes, or the outbox schema becomes a formal contract. Two contracts the teams must agree: the handoff message, with its schema, idempotency key and delivery states; and the status update interface by which CRM status reaches the portal. Credit operational contracts too, such as who handles `manual_review`.

**11.5** The mirroring hypothesis describes an observed correspondence between organisational and product structure; it does not prove that reorganising alone produces a good architecture. Changing teams first can help when the target boundaries are well understood, since teams will then tend to build along them. The risks are boundaries drawn without understanding the domain, teams owning slices of tightly coupled code, and code that does not restructure itself. A better plan identifies bounded contexts and the target architecture, then changes teams and code together in steps, watching measures such as cross-team change frequency. Credit balanced answers that recognise both directions of influence.

**11.6** Event sourcing would meet the audit need, but at a high price: every event schema becomes a permanent contract, replays must suppress the CRM handoff, snapshots and rebuild tooling are needed, and personal data in an immutable log conflicts with deletion duties. The portal is also not the owner of most of the history, because the CRM owns the case after handoff. A smaller design adds a `status_history` table with one row per status the portal stored or projected: submission ID, status, source (portal or CRM), source version, the time the change happened and the time the portal recorded it. Each row is inserted in the same transaction as the change to the current status, and the table is append-only for the application role. This gives the auditors the sequence and both timestamps, while the current-state tables stay simple. Marking: credit answers that name at least two concrete costs of event sourcing, keep the CRM as owner of the case history and record both event time and recording time.

**11.7** Fields: tenant, business unit, submission ID, asset ID and name, received time, handoff state, CRM case status and its source version, entitlement status and its freshness, and the time the row was last updated. Updates: the row is inserted in the transaction that stores the request; it is updated in the transaction that confirms the handoff, and in the transaction that applies a CRM status event or a read-model refresh from ERP, each guarded by source version so that an older event cannot overwrite a newer one; it is deleted or marked closed when the case closes. Scope: every query filters on the agent's business unit and on tenant in the agent's scope set, with row-level security on the tenant as a second guard. Lag: because each update happens in the same transaction as the change it reflects, the queue can lag only for facts that arrive from other systems; the queue shows each row's last-updated time, and opening a request reads the authoritative tables. A rebuild job can regenerate the whole read model from the source tables. Accept designs that update asynchronously from an outbox if they state the lag and the rebuild path.

The order system then needs the service's reservation ID, its atomicity guarantee, an expiry rule, an idempotency key for retries and a confirmation or release operation, plus reconciliation for timeouts.

**22.3** If expiry committed first, the late authorisation moves the order from `payment_pending` to a resolution state, never straight to `confirmed`. Try a fresh reservation if policy allows; if it succeeds, confirm under the new reservation ID. Otherwise request a void, move to `compensation_pending`, and on success close the order as `cancelled`, a terminal state that ignores later duplicate callbacks. If the void fails, keep the order in `compensation_pending` with an owner, retries and an alert when the deadline passes. Tell the customer that payment is being confirmed, or that the attempt is being reversed, according to the verified outcome. Reward designs that keep both stock and money accountable when compensation fails.

**22.4** Reservations arrive at 50 per second, so 3,000 units are all reserved after 3,000 / 50 = 60 seconds, and the item appears sold out within the first minute. The average reservation is held for 0.4 × 120 + 0.6 × 600 = 408 seconds, so Little's law gives about 50 × 408 = 20,400 units held at steady state, almost seven times the stock, while only 40 percent of those customers will pay. Possible changes: shorten the expiry for unpaid reservations (at 3 minutes the average hold falls to 156 seconds and the steady-state holding to 7,800), reserve stock only at the payment step, admit customers to checkout through a waiting room, release reservations on explicit abandonment, and limit units per customer.

**22.5** At 2,000 orders a day, roughly 0.02 per second on average and 50 attempts per second at the promotion peak, a well-indexed relational database handles the load; hot-row contention on popular items is the real limit, and a separate service does not remove it. Splitting would replace a local atomic reservation with a remote protocol needing expiry, idempotency, reconciliation and a second operational boundary, and checkout would depend on two services being available. Recommend keeping the modular design with clean internal interfaces, measuring contention, and recording extraction triggers such as a measured contention limit, a change in warehouse ownership or a team needing an independent release cadence.

**22.6** A model tree for Aster might include five leaves. Under integrity: "two customers race for the last unit, and at most one reservation commits" (H, M), and "a payment authorisation times out, and a retry never causes a second charge" (H, H). Under performance: "50 checkout attempts per second for five minutes, with 95 percent answered within two seconds" (H, M). Under availability: "the payment provider is down for twenty minutes, browsing continues and checkout shows pending" (M, M). Under modifiability: "a third warehouse is added within one quarter" (M, L). Analyse the duplicate-charge and last-unit scenarios first, because they combine high importance with real difficulty. Mark answers on concrete stimuli and measures, and on a justified ranking.

## Chapter 23

**23.1** If an order has three lines and two payment events, joining by order creates six rows, and every line amount and every payment amount appears more than once. Summing both columns therefore inflates both totals. Aggregate each fact on its own to the order or reporting grain first, then join the aggregates; and keep authorisation, capture and refund events distinct, since they mean different things. Check currency and business scope before comparing the totals. A one-row-per-order bridge can relate the aggregates, but it cannot repair an undefined measure.

**23.2** (a) Day 2 publication (rows received by day 1): day 1 = 40. Day 3 publication (by day 2): day 1 = 40, day 2 = 60. Day 4 publication (by day 3): the duplicate of e5 adds nothing, and e6 arrives one day late (3 − 2 = 1), so day 2 = 85; day 1 stays 40 and is now final. Day 5 publication (by day 4): e7 arrives three days after its event day (4 − 1 = 3), beyond the allowed lateness, so the automatic run still shows day 1 = 40 and day 2 = 85. (b) The naive sum of arriving rows is 40 + 60 + 60 + 25 + 30 = 215; the correct total over unique events is 40 + 60 + 25 + 30 = 155. (c) e7 needs the exception process because it is later than the rule allows and day 1 is already final. If approved, a versioned republication sets day 1 to 70, records a new revision number, the reason (late event e7 from its source) and the time of the change, so that a manager who saw 40 can see why it moved. Assigning e7 to day 4 because it arrived then would be wrong.

**23.3** A defensible policy publishes a timestamped snapshot at 07:00 that states known source completeness; keeps the last valid snapshot, with a freshness warning, when critical checks fail; and marks the most recent days as provisional. Late data within an agreed window, such as two business days, updates those days automatically; older corrections go through an exception route and a versioned republication with a revision number and reason. Access rules apply to exports, extracts and derived stores. Users are told when a correction changes a measure they rely on. The window length is a business choice based on observed lateness and reporting needs.

**23.4** A watermark that is too fast declares an event-time period complete while events for it are still in transit. For example, a CRM export is delayed overnight, the watermark advances past day 1, and the delayed day-1 events arrive behind it. Beacon's two-day allowed lateness absorbs this: day 1 is recomputed and republished as a revision. A watermark that is too slow is held back by one late source. For example, one of three sources stops sending, so the pipeline never declares day 1 complete. Beacon's processing-time trigger publishes at 07:00 regardless, labelled provisional and with the missing source named, rather than waiting indefinitely.

**23.5** First, check whether the metric definition, population or exclusions changed that month, for example a new rule that excludes reopened cases. Second, check whether unresolved cases are excluded, which would hide a growing backlog. Third, check whether a source was late or missing, so that slow cases have not arrived yet. Fourth, check whether the case mix changed, such as a rise in simple requests, and whether duplicates or a join error changed the counts. Compare the numerator and denominator separately with the previous month, and confirm the result in a second view, such as the backlog trend.
