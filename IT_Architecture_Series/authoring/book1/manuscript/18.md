# 18 Economics, procurement and build-or-buy decisions

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Construct a transparent cost model that separates one-time, recurring, fixed and variable costs.
- Calculate cloud running costs under on-demand and committed pricing, including storage tiers and data transfer.
- Calculate the unit cost of a business transaction and explain the difference between average and marginal cost.
- Compare build, buy and hybrid options using sensitivity analysis.
- Evaluate a vendor as an operational dependency, including its exit path.

## Cost follows design and use

A system's cost covers its whole life: implementation and migration at the start; licences, infrastructure, support, security and operations while it runs; training for its users; and the eventual exit. Some costs stay fixed over a range of demand. Others vary with requests, storage, data transfer, users or operating effort.

The cheapest infrastructure line can produce the most expensive service if it needs a lot of manual operation. A managed service can also waste money when chosen without understanding its charging units. Keep three distinctions visible in every model:

- **One-time and recurring costs**, such as a migration project versus a monthly subscription.
- **Cash and internal effort**. An engineer's time creates no extra invoice, but it uses capacity that could do other work.
- **Fixed and variable costs**, such as a database instance versus per-gigabyte data transfer.

## How cloud pricing works

Cloud bills are, at heart, usage multiplied by rate. **FinOps** is the practice of managing technology spending for business value. The FinOps Foundation's framework treats usage and rate as separate levers, and describes managing the rate as a capability of its own {REF:finopsrate}.

### Pricing models

**On-demand** pricing charges for what you use, by the second or hour, with no commitment. It suits variable and short-lived workloads.

**Commitment discounts** trade flexibility for a lower rate. You commit to a level of usage or spend for one or three years. AWS calls its spend-based commitment Savings Plans, and Azure calls its resource commitments Reservations; both apply the lower rate to matching usage during the term {REF:savingsplans,azurereserve}. The FinOps Foundation notes that a commitment is not a resource: it behaves like a coupon, and you pay for it whether or not matching usage exists {REF:finopsrate}.

That gives a simple test. A commitment pays off when the committed capacity is used for more than (1 − discount) of the term. With a 30 percent discount, the break-even point is 70 percent use. Commit only to the steady baseline you are confident of, and leave peaks on demand.

### Storage tiers and data transfer

Object storage is priced per gigabyte-month, plus charges for requests and retrieval. Cooler tiers cost less to store and more to read, and they carry minimum storage durations; early deletion is charged as if the object had stayed the full minimum {REF:s3classes,blobtiers}. Archive tiers go further: Azure's archive tier can take up to fifteen hours to bring a blob back online {REF:blobtiers}.

**Data transfer** out of a cloud region to the internet, often called **egress**, is charged per gigabyte. Transfers between regions, for example replication to a recovery region, are usually charged too. A design that moves large files between regions or to many external users can find egress becoming a major line.

### Worked example: Meridian's monthly cloud bill

The prices below are invented for teaching. Real prices vary by provider, region and date. Assume:

- 100,000 submissions a year, about 8,333 a month; 40 percent carry attachments averaging 2 MB.
- Attachments are kept for 24 months and staff open each one 1.5 times on average.
- Five always-on instances, the three API replicas and two workers of the Chapter 21 deployment, at EUR 0.10 per instance-hour on demand.

| Item | Calculation | Monthly cost (EUR) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compute, on demand | 5 × 730 h × 0.10 | 365.00 |
| Managed database, zone-redundant | Fixed size | 400.00 |
| Attachment storage, all hot | 160 GB × 0.020 | 3.20 |
| Egress for staff downloads | 10 GB × 0.08 | 0.80 |
| Telemetry ingestion | 20 GB × 2.50 | 50.00 |
| **Total** | | **819.00** |

The storage figure comes from 8,333 × 0.4 × 2 MB ≈ 6.7 GB of new attachments a month, or 160 GB after 24 months. Two choices then look very different:

- Moving attachments older than 90 days to a cool tier at EUR 0.010 per GB-month cuts storage from EUR 3.20 to 20 × 0.020 + 140 × 0.010 = EUR 1.80. It saves EUR 1.40 a month and adds retrieval charges and a minimum storage period.
- A one-year commitment at 30 percent off the five steady instances cuts compute from EUR 365.00 to 5 × 730 × 0.07 = EUR 255.50. It saves EUR 109.50 a month, or EUR 1,314 a year.

For Meridian, compute and the database dominate the bill, so the commitment matters and storage tiering barely does. A workload that serves large video manuals to customers would look different: 5 TB of egress at EUR 0.08 per GB adds EUR 400 a month, more than all the compute. Look at the shape of your own bill before optimising.

## Unit economics

A **unit cost** connects spending to an outcome the business recognises, such as cost per completed service request. FinOps practice uses **unit economics** to relate technology spending to the value it creates {REF:finops}.

For example, the bill above is EUR 819 for 8,333 submissions, or about EUR 0.10 of production infrastructure per submission. That number excludes the recovery region, test environments, staff time and the initial build. If annual operating cost, including support effort, is EUR 84,000 for 100,000 submissions, the full operating cost is EUR 0.84 per submission. Label the numerator and denominator every time, so that nobody mistakes an infrastructure unit cost for the full cost.

**Average cost** and **marginal cost** differ. The next thousand requests may cost almost nothing while capacity is idle, yet a small increase at a threshold can require a larger database or another licence tier. Model the shape of the cost curve, not one point on it.

Track the unit that matters to the decision. Cost per API call can fall while cost per completed work order rises, for instance when a change creates more retries or manual reconciliation.

## A three-year build-or-buy comparison

The figures below are illustrative euros for Meridian, with no discounting, tax, inflation or vendor quotation implied. The custom option's EUR 24,000 annual platform line covers production at on-demand prices (12 × 819, about EUR 9,800), the pilot-light recovery region from Chapter 13 (EUR 10,800) and test environments (about EUR 3,400).

| Cost component | Custom portal | Purchased portal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial build or configuration | 120,000 | 45,000 |
| Initial migration and integration | 35,000 | 55,000 |
| Annual platform or subscription | 24,000 | 54,000 |
| Annual operations and support effort | 60,000 | 30,000 |
| Estimated exit work at year three | 20,000 | 35,000 |

The simple three-year totals are:

- Custom: 120,000 + 35,000 + 3 × (24,000 + 60,000) + 20,000 = 427,000.
- Purchased: 45,000 + 55,000 + 3 × (54,000 + 30,000) + 35,000 = 387,000.

The purchased option is 40,000 cheaper under these assumptions. That alone does not settle the choice. The purchased option may fail a mandatory requirement or have a weaker exit path; the custom option may carry more uncertain maintenance effort. Test how sensitive the result is:

- If the subscription is 20 percent higher, it becomes 64,800 a year and the purchased total becomes 419,400.
- If custom operations effort is 25 percent higher, it becomes 75,000 a year and the custom total becomes 472,000.

A 20 percent price rise nearly closes the gap, so the subscription price and its renewal terms deserve the most investigation.

## Benefits and uncertainty

A benefit model connects an intervention to an outcome. For example, if integration saves ten minutes on each of 5,000 work orders a year, the gross saving is 50,000 minutes, about 833 hours. It becomes a realised benefit only when the work actually disappears and the freed time is used well.

Include adoption, process change and exception handling. An automation that saves time on normal cases but creates awkward exceptions may deliver less than expected.

Use ranges or scenarios for uncertain inputs. Keep an estimate separate from a commitment, and a forecast separate from an observed result. A precise spreadsheet can still rest on imprecise assumptions.

## Build, buy or both

Buy when the capability is standard, a product meets the important requirements, and the operating and commercial terms are acceptable. Build when the capability needs distinctive behaviour, integration, control or economics that products cannot offer at acceptable cost.

A hybrid is often the strongest option: buy the CRM, build a narrow portal and integration layer, and run it on managed infrastructure. The key question is which responsibilities the organisation is prepared to own for years.

Check configuration limits and upgrade behaviour. A heavily customised product can become hard to upgrade, while a lightly customised one may not support the required process. Ask the vendor how customisations survive their releases.

## Vendor due diligence

Assess a vendor in four areas:

- **Fit**: functional fit, interface quality and identity integration.
- **Operation**: resilience, security assurance, support and incident handling.
- **Commercial terms**: pricing units and contract terms.
- **Exit**: data export and the effort of leaving.

Test the essential capabilities yourself instead of relying on demonstrations.

Ask for a representative export and check that another system can interpret it, including identifiers, attachments, historical states and audit records. A CSV download that omits relationships or files is a weak exit mechanism.

For an API dependency, investigate quotas, throttling, versioning, idempotency, sandbox behaviour, incident communication and support escalation. A capable API can still be hard to integrate if the contract and operating process are weak.

Public-sector and regulated buyers may face extra requirements for accessibility, records, transparency, security and procurement procedure. Obtain the organisation's current requirements and advice for each purchase.

## A decision matrix with limits

Define mandatory requirements first and exclude any option that fails one. Then compare the remaining options with a small set of weighted criteria, such as functionality, integration, operability, cost and exit. Record the evidence and confidence behind each score.

Test sensitivity by changing plausible weights. If a small change reverses the winner, the decision is fragile and needs discussion. If one option wins across a broad range, confidence rises, subject to the quality of the inputs.

A matrix supports judgement; accountability stays with people. Record the decision maker and the risks they accepted.

> **In practice.** Commit to discounts only after a few months of measured usage, and only for the steady baseline. A commitment bought for a forecast that never arrives is pure cost.

## Key points

- Separate one-time from recurring, cash from effort, and fixed from variable costs, and state what each model excludes.
- Cloud cost is usage times rate: commit discounts to the steady baseline, check storage-tier minimums and retrieval charges, and watch egress.
- Unit costs need a labelled numerator and denominator; average and marginal cost answer different questions.
- Sensitivity analysis shows which estimates deserve investigation before a build-or-buy decision.
- Procurement is an architectural decision: contracts, interfaces, operating support and exit shape the system for years.
