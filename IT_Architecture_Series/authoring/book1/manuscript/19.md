# 19 Enterprise architecture, governance and migration

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain how enterprise architecture connects business capabilities, applications, data and technology.
- Build a capability-to-application map and identify duplication that needs investigation.
- Design a transition roadmap of operable intermediate states with measurable gates.
- Compare big-bang, phased and strangler-fig migration approaches.
- Evaluate a governance process for both control and speed of delivery.

## The enterprise view

A solution can work well on its own while making the organisation harder to run. For example, a new customer database may duplicate an existing source of truth, a new sign-in system may fragment access management, and a quick point-to-point integration may make every later change more expensive.

**Enterprise architecture** looks across business capabilities, processes, information, applications and technology. Its aim is to coordinate change so that it supports the organisation's goals. That needs an honest picture of the current estate and of the organisation's ability to move away from it.

## Capability maps

A **capability** is something the organisation must be able to do, such as "manage service requests", independent of the team or product that does it today. A **capability map** lists these abilities. Overlaying applications, owners, costs, risks and planned investment then reveals duplication, gaps and dependencies.

| Capability | Applications today | Owner | Observation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Service request intake | Email inbox, web form, CRM | Service operations | Three intake paths and duplicate entry |
| Case management | CRM | Service operations | One system of record after handoff |
| Asset and contract records | ERP | Finance and logistics | Authoritative; read by the portal |
| Field work scheduling | ERP, spreadsheet | Field service | One business unit uses a spreadsheet |
| Service reporting | Three report packs | Operations management | Conflicting definitions of lead time |

The map shows Meridian where the portal helps: it removes two of the three intake paths. It also flags work outside the portal's scope, such as the spreadsheet scheduler, that the roadmap must at least recognise.

## Application portfolio decisions

For each significant application, record:

- its purpose, owner and users;
- its lifecycle stage, support status and cost;
- its integrations and the data it holds;
- its criticality and any planned change.

Keep the inventory useful for decisions; completeness for its own sake rarely pays.

Each application then gets an action: retain, invest, replace, consolidate or retire. Retirement needs proof that no essential process, integration, record obligation or user group still depends on it.

Suppose Meridian runs three service management tools after past acquisitions. Consolidation could cut cost, yet each tool may serve a different legal entity or a specialised process. Investigate the differences before treating duplication as waste.

## Frameworks, notations and judgement

The TOGAF Standard provides an enterprise architecture method and supporting guidance. Its Architecture Development Method (ADM) starts with a Preliminary phase, which sets up the architecture capability, framework and principles. It then cycles through eight phases, A to H, with requirements management at the centre of the cycle {REF:togaf}:

- **A** Architecture vision.
- **B** Business architecture.
- **C** Information systems architectures.
- **D** Technology architecture.
- **E** Opportunities and solutions.
- **F** Migration planning.
- **G** Implementation governance.
- **H** Architecture change management.

Organisations adapt the cycle to their needs rather than following it as paperwork.

ArchiMate is a modelling language for enterprise relationships, while UML and C4 answer software and system questions. Choose notation for the audience and purpose. A framework's name on a diagram does not make it understandable.

Four kinds of guidance are often mixed up:

- A **principle** is a broad direction, such as "every business fact has one named owner".
- A **standard** is a required or preferred rule, such as "services authenticate with the corporate identity provider".
- A **pattern** is a reusable approach, such as the transactional outbox.
- A **decision** applies these to one context and is recorded in an ADR.

## Governance that helps delivery

Good governance makes decision rights clear. Teams decide what affects only their own system; wider review is reserved for choices that cross boundaries. For example, a delivery team chooses its own libraries, while a proposal to store contract entitlements in the portal goes to review with the data owner.

| Decision | Who decides | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal design within a service | Delivery team | Choice of library or module structure |
| Shared data or a new system of record | Architecture review with data owner | Portal storing contract entitlements |
| Security boundary or new external exposure | Architecture and security review | Public download links for attachments |
| Significant spend or a new vendor | Sponsor with architecture advice | Purchased portal subscription |
| Exception to a standard | Standard's owner, with expiry date | Temporary shared database read for a migration |

Provide an exception process with an owner, a rationale, an expiry or review date and compensating measures. Without one, teams hide deviations or delay essential work. An approved exception remains a risk to track.

A design review should spend its time on consequential assumptions. Use checklists to catch omissions, then discuss the parts that could change the decision. Record unresolved issues and the conditions for release.

## Current, target and transition architectures

A **target architecture** describes a desired future state. A **transition architecture** describes an intermediate state that can be delivered and operated on its own. Large changes need several transitions, because data, people, contracts and dependencies cannot all move at once.

For Meridian, the first transition makes CRM handoffs duplicate-safe behind the existing intake channels. The second launches the portal for one business unit. The third consolidates reporting definitions. Only after evidence of successful operation does Meridian retire the old forms. {FIG:roadmap} shows the sequence.

## Migration patterns

A **big-bang migration** switches everything at one moment. It shortens coexistence but concentrates risk in a single cutover. A **phased migration** moves users, capabilities or data in steps. It allows learning between steps, at the cost of running old and new side by side and reconciling them.

The **strangler fig** pattern is a disciplined form of phased migration. Martin Fowler described it in 2004 and named it after the strangler fig {REF:fowlerstrangler}. The fig germinates in a nook of a host tree, sends roots down to the ground and gradually grows around its host until it stands on its own, often after the host has died. A routing layer sends selected requests to the new implementation while the old system serves the rest; over time, the old responsibilities shrink and are removed. Microsoft's pattern guidance describes the same facade-based approach {REF:strangler}. The routing rule and the coexistence design are the substance of the pattern.

An **anti-corruption layer** translates a legacy system's model into the new system's own terms, so that the new model is not bent to fit the old one. Evans describes it as an isolating layer that a downstream system builds to use an upstream system's functions in its own domain language {REF:ddd}. Give the layer an owner and an end date, or it can grow into an undocumented permanent integration hub.

## Data migration

Profile source data before mapping it. Look for missing values, duplicates, incompatible identifiers, invalid states and historical exceptions. Agree transformation rules with the business owners, and keep an audit trail for consequential changes.

Rehearse the migration with representative data. Measure its duration and check counts, key coverage, relationships and important totals. Record every exception rather than discarding it silently.

Decide how writes are handled during the move:

- A **frozen source** keeps consistency simple but causes downtime.
- **Change capture** copies ongoing changes from the source, reducing downtime at the cost of complexity.
- **Dual writing** from the application to both systems creates partial-failure risks and needs explicit reconciliation.

Rolling back after new transactions exist is a business and data problem, more than a routing switch. Decide before cutover how records created in the new system will be kept or reconciled. Chapter 24 works through this in detail.

## Adoption and retirement

Train users and support staff for the new workflow, and update procedures, permissions and communications. A successful deployment that nobody adopts fails the business objective.

Retirement means disabling old access, stopping integrations and scheduled jobs, resolving retained records, cancelling licences and updating the inventory. Confirm afterwards that the cost and the risk have actually gone.

> **Common mistake.** Drawing the target architecture and skipping the transitions. Most migration risk lives in the intermediate states, where two systems run together and someone must know which one owns each write.

## Key points

- Enterprise architecture connects local decisions to shared capabilities, data ownership and a feasible sequence of change.
- Capability maps reveal duplication and gaps; investigate the reasons for duplication before consolidating.
- Governance works best with clear decision rights, reserved review for boundary-crossing choices and a time-limited exception process.
- Transition architectures must each be operable, with an owner, success criteria and a recovery path.
- The strangler fig, named by Fowler, replaces a system piece by piece behind a routing layer; an anti-corruption layer keeps the new model clean.
