# 23 Worked architecture: a governed analytical platform

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Design an analytical platform from decision needs, metric definitions and a declared grain.
- Explain event time, processing time and watermarks, and design a policy for late and corrected data.
- Calculate deduplicated and corrected totals and explain why naive sums differ.
- Design quality gates, reconciliation and lineage for published metrics.
- Evaluate access controls for derived data such as extracts, caches and indexes.

## Begin with the decision the data must support

Beacon Analytics is an enterprise data programme that combines service, sales and fulfilment information. Its first users are operations managers, who need a daily view of service lead time and order completion, and analysts, who investigate exceptions. Nobody needs a sub-second dashboard yet. The first release processes up to one million new or changed source records a day from three source systems, and must publish by 07:00 local business time.

Define each metric before designing a pipeline. "Average service time" could mean receipt to first response, approval to scheduling, or receipt to closure, and each measures a different process. A full definition states:

- The population and any excluded cases.
- The start and end events.
- The time zone policy.
- The treatment of reopened work.
- Whether unresolved requests are included.

For example, a dashboard that leaves out all unresolved cases can show completion time improving while a large backlog grows unseen.

Treat freshness and correctness as separate qualities. A report can be fresh but wrong because a join multiplied rows. It can be correct for yesterday yet misleading if its timestamp is hidden. The service contract therefore states both the publication deadline and the completeness and correction policy.

ETL and ELT trade off differently. **ETL** transforms data before loading it into a broadly accessible store, which limits how far sensitive source fields spread. **ELT** loads raw data first and transforms it inside the target, which preserves flexibility and uses the target's compute, but creates a raw-data access and retention duty. ## Grain and measures

The **grain** of a fact table is the business meaning of one row. Kimball calls declaring the grain the first and most important design step {REF:kimball}. In Kimball and Ross's four-step design process, it follows the choice of business process and comes before the dimensions and facts are identified {REF:kimballtoolkit}. {FIG:star} shows three facts at different grains:

- **FactServiceEvent**: one row per accepted source event and version, for one service request. It records the request key, event type, event time, ingestion time and dimension keys. This is a transaction fact.
- **FactRequestDaily**: one row per request per business day, holding the agreed end-of-day state. This is a periodic snapshot.
- **FactOrderLine**: one row per order line, with quantity and money in a stated currency. This is another transaction fact.

FIGURE star | Analytical facts at different grains share conformed dimensions without being joined blindly.

**Conformed dimensions**, such as date, customer organisation, product and business unit, use definitions the business has agreed across facts. Sharing dimensions does not make every fact joinable row by row. For example, joining five service events to three order lines for the same customer produces 5 × 3 = 15 rows, and summing the order amounts from that result counts each one five times. Aggregate each fact to the intended common grain first, then compare.

Measures behave differently under aggregation:

- An **additive** measure, such as order-line quantity in one unit, can be summed across dimensions.
- A **semi-additive** measure, such as a backlog count or balance, is meaningful at a point in time. Summing daily snapshots across days double-counts it.
- A **ratio**, such as on-time percentage, is recomputed from its numerator and denominator at the requested level, rather than averaging precomputed percentages.

State units and currencies with every measure.

## Historical context and corrections

Suppose customer C17 moves from business unit North to Central on 1 June. A report asking who owned the customer when a request was handled needs the historical assignment; a list of current responsibilities needs the latest one. Both are valid questions with different queries. A **history-preserving dimension** gives each period its own surrogate key and keeps the business identifier, with non-overlapping validity intervals per customer. A fact then joins to the dimension row valid at its event time.

Late data complicates this. An event from 30 May that arrives on 3 June must join to the May assignment, even though June's is current. If the source later corrects the effective date of the move, the platform needs a correction mechanism and an audit trail of what it republished. An immutable record of what was ingested can coexist with corrected curated tables.

Tell an update from a duplicate. A repeat delivery of the same event ID and version adds nothing. A higher source version may supersede an earlier value under a defined rule. A deletion or withdrawal needs its own meaning: it may remove a personal-data copy, reverse a business measure or keep a non-identifying audit fact, as the approved lifecycle policy decides.

## Event time, processing time and watermarks

Akidau and colleagues, describing the Dataflow Model, distinguish two time domains {REF:dataflow}.

- **Event time** is when the event actually happened, according to the system that recorded it.
- **Processing time** is when the pipeline observes the event at a given stage.

Event time for an event never changes, while processing time keeps moving forward. The gap between the two, the **skew**, varies with network delays, batch exports and source outages. Beacon also records **ingestion time**, when the landing zone received the record. Store all three; a single column named `date` cannot distinguish them.

A **watermark** is the pipeline's estimate of progress in event time: "we believe we have seen all events up to time T". The Dataflow paper notes that watermarks can be too fast, so late data arrives behind them, or too slow, held back by a single delayed record. For many sources a perfect watermark is impossible. The paper's response is to accept that a pipeline rarely knows when it has seen all its data. **Triggers** then decide when to emit results for a window of event time, and **refinement modes** decide how each later result relates to the ones already emitted {REF:dataflow}:

- **Discarding**: each result covers only the data that arrived since the previous one, which suits consumers that add the pieces up themselves.
- **Accumulating**: each result covers all the data so far and replaces the previous one.
- **Accumulating and retracting**: each result replaces the previous one, and the pipeline also emits a retraction of the old value so that downstream consumers can remove it.

Beacon's first release applies these ideas to a daily batch:

- **Trigger.** Publish at 07:00 each day, a processing-time trigger.
- **Allowed lateness.** Apply an event or correction automatically if it arrives at most two days after its event day, that is, if ingest day − event day ≤ 2. The next publication recomputes the affected day. A later arrival goes through an exception process that can trigger a versioned republication.
- **Refinement.** Each publication replaces earlier results for the same days, which is accumulating mode, and carries a revision number and the reason for any change.

An event day is therefore provisional until the rows received on its second following day have been published, and final after that: day 1 is final from the publication on the morning of day 4. If the business needs to correct a final day, the revision identifier explains why a published total changed.

## A worked example: duplicates and lateness

Three events report completed work, with values in whole euros:

| Event | Event day | Value | Delivery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| e1 | Day 1 | 100 | Arrives on day 1, then again as a duplicate |
| e2 | Day 1 | 50 | Arrives late, on day 3 |
| e3 | Day 2 | 75 | Arrives on day 2 |

Summing every arriving row gives 100 + 100 + 50 + 75 = 325. The correct total across unique events is 100 + 50 + 75 = 225.

The day-1 report published on the morning of day 2 contains only e1, so it shows 100. e2 arrives on day 3, two days after its event day, which is still within the allowed lateness. The publication on the morning of day 4 therefore corrects day 1 to 150, and day 2 stays at 75. The platform publishes a revision that explains the correction. Adding e2 to day 3 simply because it arrived then would be wrong, and so would calling the first day-1 figure final. Lab 10 implements this example, including a later correction of e3.

Money needs care beyond the arithmetic. The lab uses integer minor units (cents) so that binary floating point does not distort the example. A production model also handles currency, rounding policy, tax, the timing of exchange rates and the source's accounting rules. A correct sum of incompatible currencies is still meaningless.

## Quality gates and reconciliation

Use layered checks:

- **Structural** checks find missing fields and wrong types.
- **Relational** checks find orphan identifiers.
- **Domain** checks find impossible transitions or forbidden negative values.
- **Distribution** checks flag unusual shifts for investigation, since an unusual value can still be correct.
- **Reconciliation** connects the published model back to the source within a declared extraction boundary.

Counts alone are weak. A missing row and a duplicate cancel out in a total count, so compare distinct key sets, version coverage, totals by meaningful groups and a sample of record values. A checksum helps only when both sides use the same canonical representation and scope. Keep sensitive raw values out of reconciliation logs.

For every daily run, keep:

- the source position, so the run can be repeated from the same input;
- the transformation revision and schema version;
- the quality results and the input and output counts;
- the publication identifier and any exceptions.

When a run fails, keep the last valid publication available with a visible freshness warning. Whether a partial publication is acceptable for a given report is the report owner's decision, made in advance.

## Security and lineage through derived data

Access rules must follow data into extracts, caches, notebooks, dashboard exports and any search or vector index. Removing direct identifiers does not anonymise a dataset by itself: aggregates over very small groups can reveal individuals, and joins with outside data can re-identify records. Apply the approved disclosure controls for each audience.

**Lineage** connects a displayed measure to its source fields, transformations and publication run. Operational lineage lets the team reproduce a number; business lineage explains what it means. Both matter when a manager challenges an indicator. A catalogue label saying "trusted" is weak support unless the calculation and its checks can be traced.

If an AI assistant explains a dashboard, give it the metric definition, the data timestamp, the user's permitted scope and the source lineage. A rise in service time may reflect a changed case mix, a delayed source or a real operational problem, and correlation alone cannot tell them apart. A human analyst evaluates those alternatives before anyone recommends action.

## Operate the analytical service

Measure freshness, completeness, quality-rule failures, correction volume, recovery time and cost per published dataset. These measure what report users experience, which CPU usage cannot show. Runbooks cover source outages, schema changes, duplicate replays, late corrections and accidental exposure.

Make reprocessing intentional and repeatable. Version the transformation logic and run a replay in isolation before it replaces published data. A changed definition may need a restatement of history, a new metric version, or both. Tell report users which, so that they do not mistake a modelling change for a sudden business improvement.

## Key points

- Start from the decision and define each metric's population, events, time zone and exclusions before building pipelines.
- Declare the grain of every fact; aggregate to a common grain before comparing facts, and respect additive, semi-additive and ratio measures.
- Event time, processing time and ingestion time differ; watermarks estimate progress, and triggers and revisions handle the data that arrives behind them.
- Deduplicate by event identity and version, assign events by event time, and publish corrections as numbered revisions.
- Quality gates, reconciliation, lineage and access rules must cover every derived copy of the data.
