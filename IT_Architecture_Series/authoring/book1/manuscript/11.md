- Evaluate a proposed decomposition by its transaction boundaries, ownership and release independence.

## Patterns describe recurring arrangements

An **architectural pattern** describes a recurring organisation of responsibilities and interactions. A named style helps a team reason about a design, but quality comes from how the style is applied. “Microservices” does not tell you whether the services are secure, coherent, observable or independently deployable.

Two basic patterns appear inside almost every style:

- **Client–server** separates a client that requests work from a server that provides it.
- **Layered architecture** groups responsibilities such as presentation, application logic, domain logic and persistence.

Styles differ mainly in how they divide and deploy the system:

- A **monolith** is deployed as one main application unit.
- A **modular monolith** keeps clear internal module boundaries while keeping that single deployment.
- **Service-oriented architecture** organises capabilities behind service interfaces, often across a whole enterprise.
- **Microservices** are small, independently deployable services, each owned by one team.

Boundaries and operational independence matter far more than lines of code per service.

## Start from the domain

A **domain** is an area of business activity with its own language. Domain-driven design introduces the **bounded context**: a boundary inside which one model, and one meaning for each term, applies consistently {REF:ddd}. “Service case” in the CRM and “work order” in ERP describe related work, but they have different lifecycles, attributes and owners.

Meridian has three main contexts:

- the **portal context** owns submissions, receipts and the status customers see;
- the **CRM context** owns service cases once the handoff is confirmed;
- the **ERP context** owns assets, contracts, entitlements, inventory and work orders.

Do not force them into one universal model. Define each relationship and the translation it needs. An **anti-corruption layer** translates between contexts so that one is not shaped by another's terminology or legacy interface {REF:ddd}. Meridian's work-order integration plays this role. It consumes the CRM's `ServiceCaseApproved` event, translates it into ERP's work-order format and creates the work order, using the service case ID as the idempotency key. The CRM never needs to know how ERP schedules technicians, and ERP never needs to know the CRM's case model.

## Teams shape systems

In 1968 Melvin Conway observed that organisations which design systems are constrained to produce designs that copy their own communication structures {REF:conway}. This observation became known as **Conway's law**. Two teams that rarely talk will build components joined by a narrow, formal interface. One team working closely together tends to build tightly connected parts.

MacCormack, Baldwin and Rusnak tested this idea, which they call the **mirroring hypothesis** {REF:mirroring}. They compared pairs of software products with similar functions. Products built by loosely coupled organisations, such as distributed open-source communities, were significantly more modular than their counterparts built by tightly coupled commercial teams.

For architects, the lesson cuts both ways:

- Draw service boundaries where team boundaries are, or plan to change one to match the other.
- Expect every boundary between teams to need a contract, an owner and a way to resolve disagreements.

For example, Meridian has one portal team of six engineers. It owns the portal and the work-order integration, so a single deployable application with clear internal modules matches its communication structure. The interfaces that cross team boundaries are the CRM handoff, the CRM events and the ERP APIs, and each of these is a formal contract with another team. Splitting the portal into ten microservices would give six people ten deployment pipelines and no team boundary for them to follow.

> **Check your understanding.** Two teams share one database table and both change its schema. What does Conway's law predict? Frequent coordination meetings, or failures when one team changes the table without the other: the table has become an unmanaged interface between teams.

## The modular monolith

A modular monolith can separate submission, assets, attachments and integration workflows inside one deployable application. Modules communicate through defined interfaces and own their data access, and the team can test most behaviour without a distributed environment.

Its advantages are simpler deployment, easy local transactions and fewer network failures. Its risks are accidental access across modules, a broad impact from each deployment, and contention for shared resources. Enforce the boundaries with code organisation, interface reviews, tests and explicit rules about which module may touch which tables.

For six engineers and a moderate workload, this is a strong starting point. Extract a service when a demonstrated need outweighs the extra operational work: independent scaling, separate ownership, security isolation or a different release cadence.

## Microservices and distributed coupling

A service boundary brings a network boundary with it. That means data consistency questions, authentication between services, independent deployment and more monitoring. A request that synchronously calls six services inherits all their latency and failure modes.

Independent deployment depends on compatible contracts and clear data ownership. If every release needs coordinated changes to five services, the system is operationally coupled, even with five repositories. A shared database that every service may modify couples them just as tightly.

Choose a service boundary around a cohesive capability with a clear owner and a real reason to change independently. Small endpoints alone are a poor reason; one service per create, read, update or delete operation multiplies the coupling.

## Event-driven architecture

**Event-driven architecture** uses events as a main means of coordination. Consumers can evolve independently and process at different rates. The costs are a workflow that is harder to see, state that is temporarily inconsistent, and event schemas that need governance.

Two coordination styles exist:

- **Choreography**: each participant reacts to events, with no central controller. It reduces central coupling but spreads the process logic across many consumers.
- **Orchestration**: a coordinator holds the sequence and the state. A long workflow becomes easier to understand, but the coordinator becomes an important component.

Use an explicit process model when the business needs to audit a multi-step outcome. An event history alone rarely tells a support worker what to do next.

## CQRS and event sourcing

Two further patterns change how a system stores and reads its state. They are often mentioned together with event-driven architecture, but each answers a different question.

### Separate models for commands and queries

**Command query responsibility segregation** (CQRS) uses one model to change data and a different model to read it {REF:fowlercqrs}. Commands go to a write model that enforces the business rules. Queries read from one or more **read models**, each shaped for a particular screen or report and updated from the write model's changes.

For example, Meridian's agents need a work queue: every open request in their business unit across all their customer-organisation scopes, sorted by age, with the asset name and the entitlement status. Built from the normalised tables of Chapter 6, each page view joins requests, assets, entitlements and handoff records. A read model instead keeps one row per open request with exactly the fields the queue shows. The portal updates the row in the same transaction that stores a request, and again when a handoff is confirmed or a CRM status event arrives. The queue then becomes a simple indexed query.

It brings three costs:

- The read model lags the write model, so the queue may show a request a few seconds after its receipt.
- There are now two models to keep consistent, and a procedure is needed to rebuild the read model when it is wrong.
- The read model is another copy of tenant data, and every query on it must apply the agent's scope.

Meridian's status projection and its ERP read model are already read models in this sense. Fowler, who first heard the idea from Greg Young, warns that CQRS adds risky complexity and suits specific bounded contexts, rarely a whole system {REF:fowlercqrs}.

### Events as the system of record

**Event sourcing** stores every change to an entity as an event in an append-only log and derives the current state by replaying those events {REF:eventsourcing}. A request's state would then be the result of `RequestSubmitted`, `HandoffConfirmed` and `CaseStatusChanged` events, rather than a row that is updated in place.

The benefits follow from keeping the whole history:

- an exact record of how each state was reached, useful for audit;
- temporal queries, such as "what did the portal show this customer at 09:00?";
- the ability to rebuild a read model, or build a new one, by replaying the log.

So do the costs. Old events must stay readable for as long as the log exists, so every event schema becomes a permanent contract. Replaying events must not repeat external side effects, such as a second CRM call, so replay runs with external gateways switched off {REF:eventsourcing}. Long histories need snapshots to keep replay fast. Personal data in an immutable log conflicts with deletion duties, unless the design keeps that data outside the events or can make it unreadable.

Event sourcing suits domains where the history is itself the business record, such as a ledger, stock movements or payment events. It differs from event-driven architecture: there, events carry information between components; here, events are the stored state. A transactional outbox records messages to send and leaves the business tables as the system of record, so it is not event sourcing either.

For Meridian, the CRM owns the case history after handoff, and the portal needs only a status history table and an audit trail. Read models earn their place in the portal; event sourcing would add permanent schema and replay obligations for little benefit.

## Serverless functions, ports and adapters

**Serverless** platforms shift more runtime management to the provider. Functions suit short, event-triggered tasks and variable demand. They still have execution limits, start-up delays, concurrency controls, monitoring needs and downstream dependencies. For example, a burst that scales a function to 500 concurrent instances can open 500 database connections and overwhelm the database.

**Ports and adapters**, also called hexagonal architecture, separates the core behaviour from external mechanisms {REF:hexagonal}. The business logic depends on a port, an interface it defines, and an adapter implements that port for a particular database or vendor. Chapter 5's CRM adapter is an example. The pattern works with any deployment style. It pays off when it protects a real source of change or enables meaningful tests; an interface around every trivial function only makes the code harder to follow.

## A comparison for Meridian

| Option | Main benefit | Main cost or risk | What would justify it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Modular monolith and worker | Simple delivery, local transactions | Shared deployment impact | Small team and coherent release needs |
| Several services | Independent ownership and scaling | Distributed failures and more operations | Measured differences in scaling or team autonomy |
| Event-driven workflow | Tolerates delay; new consumers are easy to add | Event governance and reconciliation | Several consumers and acceptable eventual completion |
| Managed functions | Little runtime management for bounded tasks | Limits and pressure on downstream systems | Bursty short tasks and tested limits |
| Vendor portal with integration | Less custom software to own | Vendor constraints and lock-in | Required experience and controls supported |

The options combine. A modular portal can publish events, and a managed function can run one integration. Treat the labels as building blocks rather than mutually exclusive packages.

## Technical debt and fitness functions

**Technical debt** is the future cost of design or implementation choices that make change harder. Some debt is deliberate, such as a temporary adapter built to meet a deadline. Some comes from weak understanding or neglected maintenance.

Record each debt's consequence, owner and repayment trigger. “Refactor later” is not a plan. A temporary polling integration may be acceptable if it meets the current freshness target and has a review scheduled for when volume crosses a measured threshold.

Turn important rules into automated **fitness functions**. For example:

- a dependency test fails the build if the reporting module imports the submission module's persistence code;
- a contract test fails if a change breaks the published API;
- a query test fails if any table holding tenant data is read without a tenant condition.

These checks make design intent enforceable long after the original authors move on.

## Key points

- Judge a design by its boundaries, ownership and operational independence rather than by its style name.
- Bounded contexts keep models consistent inside a boundary, and anti-corruption layers translate between them.
- Conway's law and the mirroring hypothesis link system structure to team communication; every team boundary needs a contract.
- A modular monolith with a worker suits a small team; extract services only for demonstrated needs.
- CQRS read models shape data for particular queries at the cost of lag and a second model; event sourcing keeps the full history at the cost of permanent event schemas and careful replay.
- Record technical debt with owners and triggers, and enforce key design rules with automated fitness functions.
