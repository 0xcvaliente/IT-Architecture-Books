# 17 Observability, service objectives and operations

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain what metrics, logs and traces each contribute, and choose telemetry for a business outcome.
- Calculate an error budget, the share of it consumed and the current burn rate.
- Design multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts for an SLO, and adapt them for a low-traffic service.
- Design runbooks and incident reviews that turn signals into owned actions.

## Observe a service through outcomes

**Monitoring** checks known conditions, such as disk space or error counts. **Observability** is the ability to understand what a system is doing from the outputs it produces. In practice the two overlap. The design question is concrete: can the team explain why this customer did not receive a valid receipt?

OpenTelemetry provides vendor-neutral APIs and instrumentation for several kinds of telemetry, which it calls signals. It currently supports traces, metrics, logs and baggage, and lists events and profiles as under development or proposed {REF:otel}. Three signals carry most operational evidence:

- **Metrics** summarise measurements over time, such as requests per minute.
- **Logs** record individual events with context, such as one failed handoff attempt.
- **Traces** follow one request or workflow across components.

**Baggage** is different in kind: it carries context, such as the tenant of the current request, from one service to the next, so that the other signals can be labelled with it. It travels in request headers, so treat it as visible to every downstream service, keep secrets and personal data out of it, and never base an access decision on it, because nothing protects its integrity {REF:otelbaggage}.

Give every step a **correlation identifier** that links the API request, the stored submission, the outbox record, each worker attempt and the vendor interaction. An identifier should help investigation without acting as a secret or revealing personal data.

Request, event and attempt identifiers have different meanings. One accepted submission can lead to several delivery attempts, so dashboards must count business outcomes separately from attempts. Otherwise retries inflate the apparent success rate.

To investigate a complaint, support staff first find the receipt and its tenant scope through authorised access. They then inspect the handoff state, the last attempt's error category and any vendor reference. Finally they check the CRM result or the reconciliation record. A useful event says "submission 7f3a9c2e confirmed by attempt 3". It does not need the customer's description or attachment. Trace sampling may drop some traces, so the durable handoff record remains the source that accounts for every accepted request.

## Instrument what matters

For an HTTP service, measure traffic, latency, errors and saturation. Google's SRE book calls these the four golden signals {REF:golden}. Add measures for the business process: accepted submissions, the age of the oldest `pending` handoff, the number in `manual_review`, and propagation delay to the CRM.

Keep telemetry bounded and safe:

- Log structured fields with controlled access and retention, and leave out full request content by default.
- Keep high-cardinality values, such as a service request ID, out of metric labels. Every distinct label value creates a new time series, so cost and query time grow quickly. Put individual IDs in logs and traces instead.
- Measure success at the point where responsibility is accepted. For example, a load balancer's 200 response shows only that a response was sent; the receipt API's commit shows that the submission is durable.

## Service level indicators, objectives and error budgets

A **service level indicator** (SLI) is a measured property of the service, such as the proportion of valid submissions that receive a durable receipt. A **service level objective** (SLO) is a target for an SLI over a period, such as 99.9 percent over 30 days {REF:slo}. The **error budget** is the amount of failure the SLO allows.

### Worked example: an error budget

Consider a busy API that serves 2,000,000 eligible requests in a 30-day month with a 99.9 percent success objective.

- The permitted bad fraction is 1 − 0.999 = 0.001.
- The error budget is 0.001 × 2,000,000 = 2,000 failed requests.
- If 500 requests fail, the service has used 500 / 2,000 = 25 percent of its budget.

This budget counts requests. A time-based budget for the same percentage counts bad minutes instead: 0.1 percent of a 30-day month is 43.2 minutes. Keep one denominator per SLI. Before reporting, define how invalid requests, planned maintenance, dependency failures and monitoring gaps are treated.

### Burn rate

The **burn rate** says how fast the service consumes its error budget compared with the rate the SLO allows. It is the observed bad fraction divided by the permitted bad fraction. If 1 percent of requests are failing and the SLO permits 0.1 percent, the burn rate is 0.01 / 0.001 = 10. At that rate the 30-day budget lasts three days.

The share of budget consumed in a window follows directly: budget consumed = burn rate × window length / SLO period. For example, a burn rate of 14.4 sustained for one hour of a 30-day (720-hour) period consumes 14.4 / 720 = 2 percent of the budget.

## Alerting on the error budget

A good page means "users are being hurt fast enough that a person must act now". Alerts on raw thresholds, such as "CPU above 70 percent", often fail that test. The Google SRE Workbook recommends alerting on burn rate over two windows at once, at several severities {REF:sloalert}.

For a 99.9 percent SLO over 30 days, it suggests these starting points:

| Severity | Long window | Short window | Burn rate | Budget consumed in the long window |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Page | 1 hour | 5 minutes | 14.4 | 2 percent |
| Page | 6 hours | 30 minutes | 6 | 5 percent |
| Ticket | 3 days | 6 hours | 1 | 10 percent |

An alert fires only when both windows exceed the burn rate. The **long window** shows that enough budget has gone to matter. The **short window**, one twelfth of the long one, shows that the problem is still happening, so the alert clears soon after recovery. The fast rule catches severe incidents; the slower rules catch smaller, sustained ones that a single fast rule would miss.

### Worked example: time to detection

Suppose the busy API starts failing 2 percent of requests. The burn rate is 0.02 / 0.001 = 20, above 14.4. The one-hour window's bad fraction rises steadily from zero, and it crosses the threshold of 14.4 × 0.001 = 1.44 percent after 60 × 0.0144 / 0.02 = 43.2 minutes. The five-minute window passed its threshold within the first five minutes, so the page fires at about 43 minutes, when 2 percent of the monthly budget is gone.

If the API fails completely, the one-hour window reaches 1.44 percent after 0.0144 × 60 ≈ 0.86 minutes, so the page fires in under a minute. Severe incidents page quickly and mild ones page later, but every page means the same amount of budget is at stake.

### Low-traffic services

Meridian's receipt API receives about 100,000 / 8,760 ≈ 11 submissions an hour on average. One failure among 11 requests gives a bad fraction of about 9 percent, a burn rate near 90, and an immediate page for a single failure. The SRE Workbook discusses this problem and lists remedies: generate synthetic traffic, combine related services into one alerting group, add client retries or fallbacks that capture failed requests, or page only on a longer window or a lower objective {REF:sloalert}.

For Meridian, a reasonable design combines three alerts:

1. A synthetic submission every minute in a dedicated test tenant, with a page when several consecutive probes fail.
2. Burn-rate tickets on real traffic over the 3-day window, for slow degradation.
3. An absolute rule on the business promise: page when any accepted submission has stayed `pending` or `retry_due` longer than the agreed handoff deadline.

## Alert on action

Every alert needs an owner, a meaningful condition and an expected first response. Include the service, scope, severity, observation time, a dashboard link and the runbook link, and leave out secrets and customer content. Test that the notification reaches the right team and that the runbook can be followed.

Use dashboards to support diagnosis. A first dashboard shows user impact, workload, dependencies, recent deployments and capacity; deeper views follow from there.

## Runbooks and recovery

A **runbook** describes a repeatable operational task. A CRM outage runbook for Meridian might read:

1. Confirm the CRM's status through the vendor's status page and a direct test call.
2. Check the handoff backlog: count by state and the age of the oldest `pending` item.
3. Pause automatic retries that would only add load, and stop any manual replays.
4. Tell support staff that customers whose handoffs pass the agreed delay will see "Received, awaiting service team".
5. After recovery, resume processing at a controlled rate within the vendor's limit.
6. Reconcile every handoff that ended in an uncertain state before closing the incident.

Include conditions for escalation and for stopping an action. Replaying all failed messages at once can overwhelm a recovering dependency. Any repair script should be reviewed, bounded and auditable.

Test backups and restores, credential rotation, queue replay and failover on a schedule. Record the measured recovery time and the manual steps that slowed it down, then feed those findings into architecture priorities.

## Incident learning

During an incident, separate coordination, technical investigation and communication, and keep a timeline of observations and decisions. Afterwards, examine the conditions that let the failure reach users and the reasons detection or recovery took as long as it did. Focus on the system rather than on one person's mistake. Give each action an owner and a follow-up date. "Be more careful" is a weak corrective action; "add a shared rate limiter that the worker cannot exceed" is a strong one.

For example, repeated CRM throttling may reveal that the worker's scaling policy ignores the vendor's limit. The improvement is a shared rate limiter and backpressure, rather than more retries.

## Capacity and the cost of telemetry

Watch trends before hard limits are reached. Database storage, connection counts, queue retention, certificate expiry and log volume all become operational risks. Forecast from real demand and planned changes, and record the uncertainty.

Telemetry has a cost too. Choose retention, sampling and detail according to investigation and assurance needs. A saving that removes the only record of an important failure is a poor trade.

## Key points

- Metrics, logs and traces answer different questions; correlation identifiers connect a receipt to its later handoff.
- An SLO turns into an error budget, and the burn rate shows how fast that budget is being spent.
- Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerts page quickly for severe incidents, later for mild ones, and clear soon after recovery.
- Low-traffic services need synthetic probes, grouped alerts or absolute business rules, because one failure can look like a large burn.
- Alerts need owners and runbooks, and incident reviews should produce owned changes to the system.
