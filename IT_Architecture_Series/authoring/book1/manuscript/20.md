# 20 Architecture evaluation and evidence

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain the steps of the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method and the risks, sensitivity points and trade-off points it produces.
- Build a quality attribute utility tree with scenarios ranked by importance and difficulty.
- Design an experiment that could challenge an architectural claim, and state the limits of its validity.
- Calculate observed failure rates and the upper bound that a failure-free test supports.
- Critique a review conclusion and write a bounded decision.

## Evaluation is an argument about a particular design

An architecture is good or bad for a purpose. A design may suit one workload, team and business goal and fail another. Evaluation therefore asks whether a proposed design can satisfy its important requirements under stated conditions, and what further evidence would reduce the remaining doubt. A checklist helps find omissions; it cannot supply missing evidence.

Begin with the decision to be made. For Meridian, the immediate question is whether durable local acceptance plus a handoff worker is acceptable for the first release. Define the boundary of the review: receipt, CRM handoff, status projection and operational recovery. Record the dependencies that cross that boundary, such as the identity provider and ERP, and leave unrelated enterprise systems out.

## The ATAM in brief

The **Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method** (ATAM) was developed at the Software Engineering Institute to expose how architectural decisions affect quality attributes and how those attributes trade off against each other {REF:atam}. Bass, Clements and Kazman's textbook describes the ATAM alongside a lightweight evaluation method for smaller reviews {REF:bass}.

The method has nine steps:

1. **Present the ATAM** to the participants.
2. **Present the business drivers**: goals, constraints and the most important quality attributes.
3. **Present the architecture**, at a level of detail that supports analysis.
4. **Identify architectural approaches**, such as an outbox, a cache or a zone-redundant database.
5. **Generate the quality attribute utility tree**, refining qualities into prioritised scenarios.
6. **Analyse architectural approaches** against the high-priority scenarios.
7. **Brainstorm and prioritise scenarios** with the wider stakeholder group.
8. **Analyse architectural approaches again**, using any new high-priority scenarios.
9. **Present the results**.

The steps sit inside four phases {REF:bass}:

- **Phase 0, partnership and preparation.** The evaluation leaders and the project's decision makers agree the scope, the participants and the logistics.
- **Phase 1, evaluation.** A small group with the architect and project leaders runs steps 1 to 6 and checks that the architecture is documented well enough to evaluate.
- **Phase 2, evaluation continued.** The wider stakeholders join, the team recaps steps 1 to 6, and steps 7 to 9 follow.
- **Phase 3, follow-up.** The evaluation team writes and delivers the final report.

The original technical report describes the same split of the nine steps between a first, architecture-centred meeting and a later, stakeholder-centred one {REF:atam}.

The analysis produces four kinds of output:

- **Risks** are important decisions not yet made, or made with consequences not fully understood.
- **Non-risks** are decisions judged sound, with the reasoning recorded.
- A **sensitivity point** is a parameter of the architecture that strongly affects a measurable quality response.
- A **trade-off point** is a parameter that is a sensitivity point for more than one quality, affecting them in different directions.

The report's own example is a communication channel whose speed drives throughput: if raising the speed also reduces reliability, that speed is a trade-off point {REF:atam}.

## Building a utility tree

A **utility tree** turns vague goals into concrete scenarios. The root, "utility", stands for the overall goodness of the system. Under it come quality attributes, then refinements, then specific scenarios at the leaves. Each leaf gets two ratings, usually High, Medium or Low: its importance to the business, and how difficult or risky the architecture team expects it to be {REF:atam}.

Read the ratings as (importance, difficulty). The analysis starts with leaves rated (H, H) and (H, M), because they matter most and carry the most uncertainty. In Meridian's tree, the CRM-outage scenario (H, M) and the peak-latency scenario (H, M) come first. Tenant isolation is (H, L): essential, but the team considers the mechanism well understood, so it needs tests more than analysis. Replacing the CRM vendor (M, H) is difficult but less urgent, so it informs the adapter boundary without driving the first release.

Each leaf is a full quality scenario in the sense of Chapter 2. For example, the CRM-outage leaf expands to:

| --- | --- |
| Source | The CRM vendor's platform |
| Stimulus | The CRM is unavailable for thirty minutes during business hours |
| Environment | Normal load, with an average of about 11 submissions an hour |
| Artefact | Submission path, outbox and handoff worker |
| Response | Receipts continue; handoffs wait as `pending` or `retry_due` and are delivered after recovery |
| Response measure | Zero accepted requests lost; backlog cleared within fifteen minutes of CRM recovery |

## Claims, mechanisms and evidence

{FIG:evaluation} separates three things that reviews often blur. The **claim** states the promised behaviour. The **mechanism** explains how the design produces it. The **evidence** investigates whether the mechanism works within the declared scope. A test result must name the build, configuration, workload, injected failure and observation point. An arrow from "test passed" to "release" is unjustified when the test never exercised the risky boundary.

FIGURE evaluation | A requirement leads through a design argument to evidence and a decision.

For the CRM-outage scenario, follow the path from API validation through the local transaction, the growing outbox, the paused worker, controlled recovery and reconciliation. Ask where data could be lost and how each accepted receipt will be accounted for. A receipt latency histogram shows how fast receipts were issued, while the recovery claim needs a count of delayed handoffs that reached the CRM, so the two tests need different observations.

## Sensitivity points, trade-offs and options

Meridian's analysis finds concrete parameters with consequences:

- **Worker concurrency** is a sensitivity point for backlog drain time. It is also a trade-off point: higher concurrency shortens recovery when the CRM has spare capacity, but increases throttling when it does not.
- **Retention of idempotency keys** affects storage cost and the risk of accepting a very late duplicate.
- **Token-validation caching** affects dependence on the identity provider and the delay before a revoked permission takes effect.

Three proposals are eligible: direct synchronous creation in the CRM, local durable acceptance with a worker, and the vendor's own portal. First remove any option that fails a mandatory requirement under its verified contract. Then compare the rest on operability, user experience, cost and reversibility. A high weighted score must never compensate for a failed mandatory requirement, such as tenant isolation.

| Review item | Claim or question | Evidence needed | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E01 | One local record per scoped submission key | Concurrent duplicate and payload-conflict tests | Blocks release if false |
| E02 | CRM outcome can be resolved after a timeout | Vendor contract plus an uncertain-outcome test | Decides automatic versus manual reconciliation |
| E03 | Backlog recovers within the agreed window | Measured service rate under the vendor quota | Sets intake and recovery limits |
| E04 | Staff cannot read outside their authorised scopes | Negative API, export, search and attachment tests | Blocks release if false |
| E05 | Purchased portal reduces operating effort | Demonstration, operating trial and cost assumptions | Informs the option comparison |

Treat E05 differently from E01. A concurrency test can expose a specific defect. A multi-year estimate of operating effort depends on adoption, staffing, incidents and vendor changes, so report a range and its assumptions.

## Design an experiment that can challenge the claim

A useful performance question is: can this configuration meet the receipt target during the ten-minute peak of 20 submissions per second while the CRM is unavailable? Set out the variables:

- **Independent variables** you change: arrival rate, payload sizes, database size and worker policy.
- **Dependent variables** you measure: receipt latency, failure fraction, database saturation and backlog.
- **Controlled conditions** you hold fixed: build, infrastructure, data seed and competing workload.

Use arrivals that match the scenario. In a **closed-loop** load generator, each simulated user waits for a response before sending again, so offered load falls when the system slows. In an **open-loop** (arrival-driven) test, requests keep arriving at the intended rate however slow the responses are. Real incident peaks behave like open-loop arrivals. State which model you used and check that the generator itself kept up; otherwise a system can look stable because the test stopped demanding work.

Separate warm-up from the measurement interval, and say whether cold starts are in scope. Keep failed and timed-out requests in the latency data at the time the client gave up, so that the worst outcomes stay visible. Repeat important runs and report the variation, instead of selecting the best run. A 99th percentile from a few hundred samples rests on a handful of observations, so report the sample size and the percentile method.

Three kinds of validity limit any result:

- **Internal validity**: can the observed difference be attributed to the change, rather than to something uncontrolled?
- **Construct validity**: does the measurement capture the property you meant to study?
- **External validity**: does the result transfer to the intended environment? For example, a test with 100,000 records, a warm cache and one tenant cannot predict behaviour with ten million records and uneven tenants.

## Quantitative evidence with uncertainty

For example, suppose a test sends 12,000 valid submissions and 18 fail. The observed bad fraction is 18 / 12,000 = 0.0015, or 0.15 percent, which exceeds a 0.1 percent target in that interval. This shows a problem under the tested conditions, and it is still not the monthly production SLO result. Investigate how the failures were distributed, how the generator behaved and whether the result repeats.

A run with no failures needs care too. If n independent requests all succeed, the failure probability could still be above zero. A useful approximation is the **rule of three**: with zero failures in n trials, the 95 percent upper bound on the failure probability is about 3/n. It follows from solving (1 − p)ⁿ = 0.05, since the natural logarithm of 0.05 is about −3.

- Zero failures in 12,000 requests supports a bound of 3 / 12,000 = 0.025 percent, comfortably below 0.1 percent.
- Zero failures in 1,000 requests supports only 0.3 percent, so it cannot demonstrate a 99.9 percent objective.

The calculation assumes independent requests. Requests that share infrastructure often fail together, which makes the true uncertainty larger. In the same way, multiplying component availabilities is a model with assumptions; keep model output separate from observed data.

Cost comparisons need the same discipline. Separate uncertain estimates from contractual charges, show which assumptions reverse the choice, and avoid precise probabilities that exist only to make a spreadsheet produce one answer.

## Read research as an architect

For a paper, identify the research question, the model or method, the main result, the assumptions and the limitations. Then write a transfer statement: what does this result change in your design, and what stays outside its scope? For example, Parnas helps you choose module boundaries but does not choose your cloud product. A consensus paper explains replicated agreement but does not settle a customer-facing compensation policy.

For a standard, find the normative requirement that applies and separate mandatory behaviour from options. For product documentation, record the version, configuration and scope. For a practitioner pattern, identify the problem it addresses and the extra responsibilities it brings.

## Report a decision that can be reviewed

End the evaluation with a bounded conclusion: proceed, proceed with conditions, investigate further, or reject. Record the architecture version, the scenarios reviewed, the evidence, the material findings, their owners and the closure criteria. Close a finding only when its evidence exists; "team aware" does not repair a violated invariant.

For Meridian, a defensible conclusion is conditional approval of the local receipt architecture. Production release waits until the CRM uncertain-outcome behaviour, the tenant access rules and backlog recovery have been verified. The conclusion matches the evidence available and names what would change it.

## Key points

- Evaluation is an argument that a specific design meets specific, prioritised scenarios under stated conditions.
- The ATAM's nine steps move from business drivers through a utility tree to analysis that records risks, non-risks, sensitivity points and trade-off points.
- A utility tree rates each scenario by importance and difficulty, so analysis starts where both are high.
- Experiments need realistic arrivals, retained failures, repeated runs and stated validity limits; zero failures in n trials bounds the failure rate at about 3/n.
- A good conclusion is bounded: it names conditions, owners and the evidence that would change it.
