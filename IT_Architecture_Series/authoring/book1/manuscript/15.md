# 15 Security, privacy and threat modelling

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain the six STRIDE threat categories and give a Meridian example of each.
- Model threats from a data flow with trust boundaries and record them in a threat register.
- Compare proxy and direct-capability designs for delivering sensitive files.
- Calculate the revocation bound of a cached access decision and choose a failure policy.
- Design privacy lifecycle controls and tests that could disprove a security claim.

## Security properties and assets

Security protects a small set of properties:

- **Confidentiality** prevents unauthorised disclosure.
- **Integrity** prevents unauthorised or incorrect change.
- **Availability** keeps the service usable by authorised users.
- **Accountability** records who or what performed each action, so that events can be reconstructed and attributed.

Start by naming what you protect. Meridian's assets include request content and attachments, customer and staff credentials, access policies, audit records, source code, deployment permissions and the continuity of a time-sensitive service process. Each threat affects particular assets, so the asset list guides where controls go.

Classify data by its real sensitivity in context. A record that looks harmless alone can identify a person when combined with another dataset. For example, an equipment serial number plus a site address and an incident date may point to one named engineer. Removing names is therefore only a first step towards anonymous data.

## Threat modelling

**Threat modelling** is a structured way to find what can go wrong before it happens. Shostack summarises it in four questions: what are we building, what can go wrong, what will we do about it, and did we do a good enough job {REF:shostack}? OWASP's guidance describes the resulting threat model as a structured record of the system being modelled, the assumptions that may need challenging later, the potential threats, the mitigations for each threat and a way to validate the model and the actions taken {REF:threat}.

Start with a current data flow. Mark the actors, entry points, data stores and the **trust boundaries** where assumptions about identity or data change. {FIG:threat} shows Meridian's main boundaries.

### STRIDE as a question generator

Loren Kohnfelder and Praerit Garg introduced the STRIDE categories at Microsoft in 1999 {REF:stride}. Shostack's book explains how to apply STRIDE in practice, alongside other techniques {REF:shostack}. Each letter names a kind of threat and the property it attacks:

| Category | Property attacked | Meridian example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Spoofing** | Authentication | An attacker replays a stolen session cookie to act as a customer |
| **Tampering** | Integrity | A caller changes the asset ID to attach a request to another tenant's asset |
| **Repudiation** | Accountability | A staff member later denies having closed a request, and no record can show otherwise |
| **Information disclosure** | Confidentiality | A log export contains customers' request text |
| **Denial of service** | Availability | Repeated 10 MB uploads exhaust the scanning workers |
| **Elevation of privilege** | Authorisation | A customer calls a staff-only status-change endpoint |

**Repudiation** deserves a note because it is easy to misread. It is the threat that a principal can perform an action and later deny having done it, while the system cannot prove otherwise. The 1999 paper describes users who "can deny a wrongdoing without any way to prove otherwise". Controls include individual identities, tamper-evident audit records and signed messages where needed.

Walk each element and flow in the diagram and ask the six STRIDE questions. Treat the categories as prompts. They help you find threats systematically, and the threat list still depends on the people asking.

### The threat register

For each credible threat, record the affected asset, the conditions for the attack, the impact, the control, an owner and how the control will be verified. Prioritise by consequence and likelihood under the real exposure. A numerical score helps discussion; keep it coarse so it does not suggest false precision.

| Threat | Main control | Verification | Residual concern |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agent reads a request outside their customer-organisation scopes | Check role, business unit and scope on every resource | Cross-scope denial tests on API, export and search | Wrong scope assignments in the staff directory |
| Malicious attachment reaches staff | Quarantine and isolated scanning before release | Safe test files and state-transition tests | Malware the scanner misses |
| Leaked worker credential exposes all data | Narrow workload identity with short-lived credentials | Permission review and denied-action tests | Misuse of the worker's legitimate permissions |
| Replayed `ServiceCaseApproved` event creates a second work order | Service case ID as idempotency key; durable deduplication | Replay and crash-recovery tests | ERP behaviour outside its documented contract |
| Log export discloses request text | Data minimisation and log access control | Inspection of log content | Careless future instrumentation |
| Staff member denies closing a request | Individual accounts and an append-only audit trail | Audit-record test for every status change | Shared accounts in other systems |

Controls reduce risk and leave a residual. The register therefore stays alive: incidents, new features and changed suppliers all add rows.

## Foundational protection principles

Saltzer and Schroeder set out design principles that still guide access control {REF:protection}. Three matter most here:

- **Least privilege** gives each principal only the authority its task needs.
- **Fail-safe defaults** base access on explicit permission, so anything not granted is denied.
- **Complete mediation** checks authority on every access to a protected object.

Applied to Meridian, a new API route denies access until its policy is defined, the worker receives only the permissions its handoff needs, and an attachment download checks the actual relationship between the user and that object. These principles explain why a broad global administrator role or a bare login check falls short.

## Defence in depth and Zero Trust

**Defence in depth** places several independent controls around an important property. For an attachment, the layers might be resource authorisation, a private storage policy, encryption, malware scanning and audit records. Independence matters: five checks that share one wrong assumption fail together.

NIST's Zero Trust architecture removes implicit trust based on network location or asset ownership. Each access decision considers the identity, the resource and the context. NIST separates a policy decision point, which decides, from a policy enforcement point, which applies the decision. It also treats disruption of those components as a threat to plan for {REF:zerotrust}. Zero Trust is a design approach, and no single product delivers it.

### Policy freshness and failure

Separating decision from enforcement creates a choice. An online decision for every request adds a dependency; a cached decision adds staleness. Both are acceptable when the bound is stated and measured.

For example, suppose the enforcement point caches decisions for at most 60 seconds, and the design allows 5 seconds of clock and evaluation tolerance. A decision made just before an entitlement was removed can then remain in use for up to 65 seconds. An event that invalidates the cache usually arrives within a second or two, which shortens the typical delay. Events can be delayed, however, so the guaranteed bound stays at 65 seconds unless the cache lifetime itself is shortened.

Decide what happens when fresh policy is unavailable, operation by operation:

- For new access to restricted attachments, deny once the cached decision has expired.
- For a public status page, serve cached content and label it.
- For emergencies, use a separately authorised and audited break-glass process with a named owner.

## Encryption and key management

Encrypt data in transit and at rest, and know which threat each control addresses. Storage encryption protects against some kinds of storage exposure, such as a lost disk or a misdirected backup. An authorised application still reads plaintext, so encryption at rest leaves application-level disclosure to other controls.

Key management covers creation, access control, rotation, backup or recovery where appropriate, and revocation. Very sensitive data may need separation of duties between key administrators and data administrators. Losing access to a key can make data unrecoverable even when backups exist.

Hashing differs from encryption. Passwords need a dedicated password-hashing function, never a fast general-purpose hash or reversible encryption. For most systems, an established identity provider is the better choice than building a password store.

## Worked design: delivering attachments safely

Consider an attachment that holds a customer's equipment incident details. The required property is that only an authorised principal can obtain its bytes through supported paths. Define the adversary first. An ordinary customer can change request parameters and replay an old download link. A stolen worker credential is a separate scenario. A compromised customer device after a legitimate download is out of scope, because no server can recall bytes already delivered.

List every path to the bytes: the API, a storage URL, a preview service, a support export, a log that contains a link and a backup restored by operators. A control on the visible download button alone misses the other five. Two designs then compete, shown in {FIG:attachment_paths}.

FIGURE attachment_paths | Proxy delivery and direct capability delivery place enforcement at different points.

**Alternative A: an application proxy.** The browser asks for an attachment by ID. The API looks up the attachment's owner and tenant, checks the user's current entitlement, confirms that scanning has approved the file, and streams the bytes using its workload identity. The storage account accepts only that identity and blocks public access. Previews and exports apply the same checks.

- Benefits: one place for request-time authorisation, and an audit trail tied to the user.
- Costs: all bytes flow through the application, which needs proxy capacity and becomes part of every download's availability.

**Alternative B: a short-lived storage capability.** After the same checks, the API issues a narrowly scoped download grant, such as a signed URL, for one object and a short lifetime. The browser downloads directly from storage.

- Benefits: less load on the application and faster large downloads.
- Costs: whoever holds the grant can use it until it expires, unless the storage service supports and enforces earlier revocation. Grants must stay out of analytics events and routine logs.

In the figure, solid arrows are requests or byte transfers. The dashed arrow carries the scoped capability, without any file bytes. Both designs keep storage private, and both still authorise the user before anything is released.

Choose using the revocation and throughput requirements. Suppose the requirement is that new access stops within ten seconds of an entitlement being removed. The proxy can meet it if each request checks fresh policy, and the design states what happens to a download already in progress. A signed URL meets it only if its lifetime, plus clock tolerance, stays within ten seconds. For large files and a looser bound, Alternative B may be the better trade. Record the chosen bound, the threat, the owner and the test that shows the bound holds.

## Privacy through the lifecycle

The GDPR sets principles including purpose limitation, data minimisation, storage limitation and accountability. Article 25 requires data protection by design and by default, Article 32 requires appropriate security, and Article 35 requires an impact assessment for processing likely to result in high risk. The exact obligations depend on the processing and on each organisation's role {REF:gdpr}.

Translate the principles into design questions:

- Why is each field needed, and who may see it?
- How long is it kept, and which copies exist?
- How does a correction or deletion reach every copy?
- Which processors and locations are involved?

For Meridian, the retention period for closed service requests comes from the privacy and business owners. The architecture provides scheduled lifecycle actions, exceptions such as legal holds, audit records and checks that the actions ran. Backups need explicit treatment: a restore must not silently bring back deleted data. A deletion request may also meet a lawful duty to retain, so the workflow applies the approved decision for each record and system.

## Verification: tests that could disprove the claim

Use a recognised baseline for application security requirements. OWASP ASVS provides versioned verification requirements; cite the exact version and requirement identifiers in a real assurance plan {REF:asvs}. Combine design review, dependency management, code review, automated checks, configuration review and targeted tests. A scanner finds known vulnerability patterns, while business authorisation rules need tests written from the policy.

Write authorisation tests as a table of principal, resource, action, policy state and expected outcome. Include the allowed cases too, for the reason given in Chapter 9.

| Principal | Resource | Policy state | Expected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Customer in tenant T2 | Request in tenant T1 | Normal | Deny |
| Customer in T1, not the submitter | Request in T1 | No delegation | Deny |
| Customer in T1, not the submitter | Request in T1 | Organisation-wide delegation | Allow |
| Agent in the right business unit | Request outside agent's scopes | Normal | Deny |
| Agent with matching unit and scope | Request in scope | Normal | Allow |
| Agent whose scope was removed | Request in the removed scope | 70 s after removal; bound 65 s | Deny |
| Customer whose delegation was revoked | Colleague's request | 70 s after revocation | Deny |
| Customer | Unscanned attachment | Normal | Deny |

Run each case through every route: API, preview, export and search. If the design permits a bounded stale decision, test just inside and just outside the bound. Use synthetic data only.

Test a compromised workload credential separately. Remove permissions the worker does not need, then show that it cannot list unrelated objects, change policies or grant itself more authority. A denied call shows what that identity and configuration could do at that time, so record the build, policy version and environment with each result.

Finally, check that the evidence itself is safe. Logs needed for accountability should hold identifiers and decision reasons, and leave out request content and credentials. When a full payload is needed for one investigation, treat its capture, access and deletion as an approved exception.

## Incident readiness

Prepare for credential exposure, data disclosure, destructive change and service disruption. Define detection, triage, containment, recovery, communication and preservation of evidence, and assign roles before an incident starts.

A runbook tells an operator how to identify the affected service, restrict a compromised identity, preserve relevant logs and restore a known-good state. Rehearse it with invented scenarios. Legal reporting decisions follow the organisation's current incident and privacy procedures.

> **In practice.** Review the threat register whenever a trust boundary moves: a new supplier, a new export, a new identity provider or a new path to stored files. Most serious gaps appear at boundaries added after the first review.

## Key points

- Threat modelling asks what we are building, what can go wrong, what we will do about it and whether we did enough.
- STRIDE prompts for spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service and elevation of privilege; repudiation means an actor can deny an action that the system cannot prove.
- Least privilege, fail-safe defaults and complete mediation turn into concrete checks on every route to every object.
- Cached or delegated access decisions need a stated revocation bound, a failure policy per operation and tests on both sides of the bound.
- Privacy controls follow data through every copy, including analytics, logs, indexes and backups.
