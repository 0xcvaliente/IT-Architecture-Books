# How to use this course

This course connects technical foundations with the decisions an IT architect makes. It is written for graduates in IT or information systems who can program and have studied business information systems. The level runs from upper undergraduate to introductory postgraduate study. At that level you explain a mechanism, test its assumptions, compare alternatives, design a solution and judge the case for it.

## What you will be able to do

By the end of the course you will have a portfolio of architecture work that you can explain and defend. You will be able to:

- turn business needs into measurable quality scenarios;
- draw consistent architecture views and record decisions in architecture decision records (ADRs);
- define who owns each kind of data and how it moves between systems;
- analyse concurrency, partial failure and uncertain outcomes;
- assess security, estimate capacity and cost, and plan a safe transition from old systems to new ones.

The course refreshes fundamentals while building professional judgement. Real delivery experience and feedback from practising architects remain the best way to complete that preparation.

## Four cases

Four organisations run through the book. Each puts a different kind of pressure on the architecture. The organisations, workloads, budgets and datasets are fictional teaching examples. The sources cited in the text are real, and the references list gives them in full.

### Meridian Service Portal

Meridian is the connecting case for Chapters 1 to 19, and Chapter 21 gives it a complete design. Meridian supports equipment for 600 customer organisations. Each customer organisation is a **tenant**. Inside Meridian, three service business units employ 40 service agents.

Customers report problems with their registered assets through a new portal. The planning workload is 100,000 service requests per year, which is about 0.0032 requests per second on average. During a major equipment incident the portal must absorb an exceptional peak of 20 valid submissions per second for ten minutes. Attachments can be up to 10 MB. Six engineers build and operate the first release, with shared identity and security support. The existing CRM stays in place for two years.

The submission path works like this:

1. The portal API validates the request and checks that the caller may submit it.
2. In one database transaction, it stores the submission and an outbox record for the CRM handoff.
3. It returns a receipt with the status `received`.
4. A background worker delivers the handoff to the CRM, using the submission ID as the idempotency key.

Each system owns different facts. The portal owns the receipt and the status that customers see. The CRM owns the service case once it has confirmed the handoff. ERP owns assets, contracts, entitlements, inventory and work orders. When a service agent approves a case in the CRM, the CRM emits a `ServiceCaseApproved` event. A work-order integration, run by the portal team, consumes that event and creates the ERP work order. The portal itself never creates work orders.

The service targets are:

- the receipt API answers 95 percent of valid submissions within one second, excluding file upload;
- at least 99.9 percent of eligible requests in a month succeed;
- CRM status changes reach the portal within one minute in normal operation;
- a thirty-minute CRM outage loses no accepted request.

These are different promises and need different measures. Access rules matter as much as speed. Customers read the requests they submitted; reading every request for their organisation needs an explicit delegation. A member of staff needs the service-agent role, an assigned business unit and an authorised set of customer organisations, because one agent can serve several tenants.

### Aster, Beacon and Orion

The later case chapters add pressures that Meridian does not have:

- **Aster Retail** (Chapter 22) handles orders, scarce inventory and payments whose outcome is sometimes unknown.
- **Beacon Analytics** (Chapter 23) deals with analytical grain, lineage, late corrections and access to shared data.
- **Orion Modernisation** (Chapter 24) replaces a legacy system while both run side by side, with cutover and rollback decisions.

Each case's assumptions apply only within its own chapter.

## How each chapter works

Every chapter follows the same pattern:

- **Learning objectives** say what you should be able to do afterwards.
- The sections build each idea from a small concrete example, usually from Meridian, before generalising. For example, Chapter 2 turns “fast submission” into a scenario with a stated load, a time limit and a point of measurement.
- **Exercises** mix recall, calculation, design and critique. Attempt them before reading the answers.
- **Key points** summarise the chapter for revision.

The answers chapter gives full working for calculations. For design questions it gives a model answer and the criteria for judging alternatives. A different design earns full credit when it meets the requirements and explains its costs and failure behaviour.

Citations appear as numbers in square brackets, such as [1], and the references list at the end of the book gives each source in IEEE style. Research papers support foundational ideas, standards support protocol and security claims, and official documentation supports product behaviour. Product documentation changes, so check it again before relying on it in a real deployment.

## Practical labs

Twelve labs turn the chapters into observable work. Nine of them include short Python listings, and the other three are design workshops with printed worksheets; either way the book contains everything a lab needs. The listings use Python 3.10 or newer and the standard library only. They run locally and contact no external service. Each lab lists the chapters it builds on; the study route below schedules every lab after those chapters.

## Read sources critically

Learn the reasoning, as well as the terms. For each important claim in a chapter or a source, ask five questions:

1. What exactly is claimed?
2. Which assumptions make it true?
3. What supports it: a proof, a measurement, a standard or practitioner experience?
4. What counterexample would limit it?
5. How would it change this design?

For example, a proof that a replicated log keeps its entries in agreement covers the log; whether an external payment happened exactly once needs evidence of its own. The research guide near the end of the book lists the papers that introduced the main ideas and shows how to critique them. You do not need to reproduce every proof, but you should be able to state where a guarantee stops.

## A twelve-week study route

A study day of 4.5 hours works well for this material:

- 20 minutes of retrieval practice on earlier chapters;
- 70 minutes of reading and annotation;
- 90 minutes of exercises or labs;
- 50 minutes of portfolio work;
- 40 minutes of source reading and reviewing your solutions.

Take breaks between blocks and shift time towards a difficult lab when you need to. Six study days per week for twelve weeks gives about 324 hours (4.5 × 6 × 12). That includes source reading, labs, design revisions and assessment; reading the prose alone takes far less. Keep the seventh day light, for rest or optional review.

Keep your portfolio under version control: the requirement register, editable diagrams, ADRs, lab notes, cost assumptions, threat model and case dossiers.

## Diagnose before you start

Before reading Chapter 1, answer these questions without searching. Write down where you are unsure.

1. Trace a web request from the browser to a database row and back.
2. Explain the difference between authentication and authorisation.
3. Sketch the entities customer, asset and service request, with their relationships.
4. An API call times out after the server committed its transaction. What does the caller know?
5. Define how you would measure the availability of a web API.
6. Explain why a technically elegant design might still be a poor business choice.

Repeat the diagnostic after week 6 and week 12. Progress shows up as more precise explanations and better-supported claims, not as more jargon.
