# 2 Business requirements and quality scenarios

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Distinguish outcomes, functional requirements, quality requirements, constraints, assumptions and dependencies.
- Write a quality attribute scenario with all six parts and a measurable response.
- Calculate the failure allowance implied by an availability or success objective.
- Design a compact requirement register that traces requirements to decisions and tests.
- Evaluate competing priorities by the consequence of failing each requirement.

## Understand the work before automating it

Begin by asking people to describe a recent, real instance of the process:

- Who started it?
- What information did they have?
- Where did work wait?
- Which exceptions needed someone's judgement?

A process diagram drawn from the official procedure can miss the spreadsheets, email approvals and manual corrections that make the actual process work.

For Meridian, interview a customer, a service agent, ERP operations staff, the CRM system owner, security and the support team. Each group sees different failures. For example, customers may receive no confirmation, service agents may find duplicate cases, and support may have no identifier for tracing a complaint. These problems are related, but each needs its own fix.

A **business capability** is an ability the organisation needs, such as managing service requests or scheduling field maintenance. A **process** describes how work flows to deliver that capability. An application is one way of supporting it. Keeping the three apart stops you from assuming that today's application defines the business.

Measure the baseline before promising an improvement. Suppose the outcome is a shorter time from case approval to a scheduled ERP work order. Agree the start event, the end event and which cases are excluded. Report the distribution as well as the average: a mean of two days can hide a group of cases that take three weeks.

## Classify what you hear

Sort each statement from the interviews into one of five types.

| Type | Meaning | Meridian example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Functional requirement | Behaviour the system provides | Record a service request against a registered asset |
| Quality requirement | A measurable property of that behaviour | 95 percent of receipts within one second at 20 submissions per second |
| Constraint | A fixed limit on the design | The existing CRM stays for two years |
| Assumption | Believed but not yet verified | The CRM honours the submission ID as an idempotency key |
| Dependency | Something another party must provide | The identity team supplies the sign-in service |

Keep assumptions out of the constraint column. “We must use a queue because the CRM is slow” mixes a proposed solution with an unmeasured claim. Rewrite it as a measured problem, such as “CRM case creation takes up to eight seconds at peak”. Then decide whether a queue, a bulk API, a better query or a process change addresses the cause.

Every important requirement needs an owner who can clarify its intent and accept the result. The technical team can propose a success measure, but the business decides whether that measure describes a satisfactory outcome.

## Quality attribute scenarios

A vague expectation such as “fast” or “highly available” cannot be tested. Bass, Clements and Kazman turn it into a **quality attribute scenario** with six parts {REF:bass}:

- **Source of stimulus**: who or what generates the event, such as customers, an operator or a failing dependency.
- **Stimulus**: the event that arrives, such as a submission or a CRM outage.
- **Environment**: the conditions at the time, such as normal operation, peak load or degraded mode.
- **Artefact**: the part of the system that is affected, such as the receipt API or the handoff worker.
- **Response**: what the system does.
- **Response measure**: a threshold that shows whether the response succeeded.

{FIG:quality_scenario} shows how the six parts connect for Meridian's peak submission requirement. The same scenario as a table:

| Part | Peak submission scenario |
| --- | --- |
| Source of stimulus | Customers reporting a widespread equipment fault |
| Stimulus | Valid submissions arriving at 20 per second for ten minutes |
| Environment | Incident peak, with the CRM available or unavailable |
| Artefact | Portal receipt API, excluding file upload |
| Response | Stores each submission with its handoff record and returns a receipt |
| Response measure | 95 percent of receipts within one second; no accepted submission lost |

> **Check your understanding.** Suppose the CRM fails during the peak. Does the scenario need a different response measure? No. Only the environment differs, and the receipt API must meet the same measure, because it never waits for the CRM.

Notice the boundary in the response. Returning a receipt for a stored request is a different promise from creating the CRM case. A separate scenario covers status propagation: in normal operation, a CRM status change reaches the portal within one minute.

### Availability and service levels

Availability needs the same care. “Highly available” becomes: in a calendar month, at least 99.9 percent of eligible submission requests receive a successful receipt within the agreed timeout. The requirement must then say which requests are eligible, where the measurement is taken, and how client errors, maintenance and dependency outages are treated. A percentage without a defined denominator is incomplete.

For example, at 100,000 requests a year Meridian handles about 8,333 requests in an average month. A 99.9 percent objective therefore allows about 0.001 × 8,333 ≈ 8 failed eligible requests in that month. A time-based objective gives a different budget: 0.1 percent of a 30-day month (43,200 minutes) is 43.2 minutes. The two measures can disagree sharply. Ten minutes of failure during an incident peak at 20 submissions per second affects up to 20 × 600 = 12,000 submissions, while the same ten minutes at night may affect none.

Three related terms come from site reliability engineering {REF:slo}:

- A **service level indicator** (SLI) is the measurement, such as the proportion of successful receipts.
- A **service level objective** (SLO) is the target for that measurement, such as 99.9 percent per month.
- A **service level agreement** (SLA) is a contract with consequences, such as service credits. It may use different measurement rules from the internal SLO.

## A compact requirement register

A register records each requirement with the check that will show it is met and the person who accepts it.

| ID | Requirement or constraint | Acceptance check | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R01 | One submission key from one submitter creates at most one service request, even under retries | Retry and concurrency test | Product owner |
| R02 | 95 percent of receipts within one second at 20 submissions per second | Load test report stating the workload | Engineering lead |
| R03 | Agents access only their business unit's requests for authorised customer organisations | Positive and negative access tests | Business owner |
| R04 | CRM status changes visible in the portal within one minute in normal operation | End-to-end lag measurement | Service operations |
| R05 | A thirty-minute CRM outage loses no accepted request | Outage rehearsal with backlog count | Engineering lead |
| C01 | Existing CRM retained for two years | Approved programme brief | Sponsor |
| A01 | CRM honours the submission ID as an idempotency key | Contract review and sandbox duplicate-key test | Integration owner |

The entries are illustrative. R02 covers only part of the customer experience; file upload, accessibility and confirmation messages need their own entries. A01 stays an assumption until the contract review and the sandbox test confirm it; Chapter 8 records the result.

## Prioritise by consequence

Ask what happens if a requirement is not met:

- A missing visual preference may be tolerable in a first release.
- A customer who cannot submit during a critical equipment outage is unacceptable.
- Showing one customer another organisation's service request is a security failure, even if it affects only a few requests.

When you compare options, separate mandatory requirements from weighted preferences. An option that fails a mandatory requirement is out, however cheap it is. For the remaining options, weighted scores support the discussion. Explain each score, and check how the ranking changes when a weight changes.

A score of four out of five means little on its own. Link it to a measured result, a documented capability or a named expert's judgement, and record which scores are tested facts and which are estimates.

## Traceability without bureaucracy

A requirement register becomes useful when it links to the work. R01, for example, might link to:

- the idempotency ADR;
- the unique constraint on the request table;
- the API contract;
- a concurrency test;
- an operational report of detected duplicates.

If R01 changes, these links show what to revisit. Keep the chain short enough to maintain. A small project can use one table and a few ADRs; a regulated programme may need more formal records. In both cases the purpose is to preserve the reasoning as people and systems change.

## Worked example: “real-time reporting”

The sponsor asks for real-time reporting. Interviews reveal two different needs:

- Managers review service trends each morning.
- Service agents need near-current status for a request while they are on a call with the customer.

The design meets the second need through the service request workflow, with its one-minute status target. It refreshes management analytics on a nightly schedule and shows the data timestamp on every report. Meridian avoids paying for streaming infrastructure just because “real time” sounded desirable.

Validate both schedules with the users. If the business later adds urgent capacity routing, the reporting requirement changes, and a faster pipeline may then be justified.

## Exercises

2.1 Write a testable requirement to replace “the system must be secure”. Choose one specific risk.

2.2 A 30-day month contains 43,200 minutes. (a) Calculate the downtime allowance for a 99.95 percent time-based availability objective. (b) In the same month Meridian receives 8,000 ordinary submissions, all successful, plus a ten-minute incident peak at 20 submissions per second during which 2 percent of submissions fail. Calculate the request-based success ratio and say whether it meets a 99.95 percent request-based objective, the same threshold as in (a). (c) Explain why the two measures give different verdicts at the same threshold.

2.3 Meridian asks for zero data loss and a five-minute recovery time. List the questions you must answer before estimating the solution.

2.4 Write a six-part quality attribute scenario for R05, the requirement that a thirty-minute CRM outage loses no accepted request. Then describe a test that checks its response measure.

2.5 A vendor comparison gives option A a weighted score of 4.2 and option B a score of 3.9. Option A cannot restrict agents to authorised customer organisations (R03). Critique the recommendation to choose A, and explain how the comparison should be run.

## Key points

- Understand the actual work and measure a baseline before proposing a system.
- Keep outcomes, functional requirements, quality requirements, constraints, assumptions and dependencies distinct.
- A quality attribute scenario names a source, stimulus, environment, artefact, response and response measure.
- Availability and success objectives need a defined denominator; time-based and request-based measures can disagree.
- Trace each important requirement to its decisions and tests, and prioritise by the consequence of failure.
