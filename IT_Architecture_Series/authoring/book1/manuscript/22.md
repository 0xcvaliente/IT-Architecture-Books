# 22 Worked architecture: commerce, inventory and payments

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Define business invariants that span orders, inventory and payments.
- Design an atomic stock reservation and a safe reservation-expiry transition.
- Explain why a payment timeout differs from a rejection, and design its reconciliation.
- Design a saga whose compensations are explicit business actions and whose terminal states cannot be reopened by late messages.
- Evaluate a modular design against a distributed alternative.

## A different problem from the portal

Aster Retail sells physical goods through its website. The planning assumptions are 2,000 orders a day, a promotion peak of 50 checkout attempts per second for five minutes, two warehouses and an external payment provider. The first release serves one fulfilment region and is built by eight engineers. The customer journey covers browsing, pricing, stock reservation, payment authorisation, order confirmation, capture, shipment and, sometimes, refund.

The business states three critical rules:

1. A confirmed order has an accountable path to fulfilment.
2. The system never knowingly sells more units than its available-to-promise policy allows.
3. Repeating the same checkout operation never causes an unintended second charge.

Each rule needs a precise definition before services are chosen. For example, "no overselling" is incomplete until the business decides whether stock is shared with physical stores, whether backorders are allowed and which system owns reservations.

Meridian's receipt only promised durable storage. An Aster order confirmation creates financial and fulfilment commitments, so it must mean more. "Checkout received", "payment pending" and "confirmed" are separate states because they carry different obligations, and the website shows the one that is true.

## Boundaries and the first architecture

The first release in {FIG:commerce} is a modular application that handles catalogue access, pricing, orders and reservations, with clear internal interfaces and separate table ownership. A payment adapter isolates the external provider, and a fulfilment worker talks to the warehouse system. Analytical feeds stay off the checkout path. A cache may speed up catalogue reads, but it is never the authority for scarce stock.

Keeping orders and reservations inside one relational database makes the stock invariant a local transaction. Should separate teams, regions or warehouse owners later need an independent inventory service, the design must replace that lost atomicity with an explicit reservation protocol and recovery model. Extracting a service changes guarantees as well as packaging.

The inventory module tracks stock on hand, active reservations and committed allocations. **Available-to-promise** (ATP) is the quantity the business may still promise to new customers. For example, it might be usable stock minus active reservations minus committed allocations, taking care never to subtract the same units twice. Returns, damaged stock and other sales channels must update the same figures through authorised paths.

## Protect the stock invariant atomically

Reserve stock with a single conditional update on the authoritative row, so that the check and the change happen together:

~~~sql
UPDATE stock
   SET available = available - :qty
 WHERE sku = :sku AND available >= :qty
~~~

In the same transaction, insert the reservation with a unique operation ID. If the update changes no row, the item is missing or short of stock, and the transaction records no reservation. Never read the stock level, decide in the application or browser, and then write an unconditional new balance; two customers would both pass the check.

For example, one lamp is in stock and two customers press "Buy" at the same moment:

| Order of events | Customer A | Customer B | Stock afterwards |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A's update commits first | Reservation created | Update changes 0 rows; told "out of stock" | 0 |
| B's update commits first | Update changes 0 rows; told "out of stock" | Reservation created | 0 |

Exactly one reservation succeeds in every interleaving. If the business allows backorders, model them as a separate state with their own promise, rather than letting the stock count go negative.

For baskets with several lines, state the policy. An all-or-nothing basket reserves every line in one local transaction, locks rows in a consistent order (for example, sorted by SKU) to avoid deadlocks, and retries on a serialisation failure. Partial fulfilment needs a different state model and the customer's agreement. A distributed inventory reserves each line separately and must release the successful ones if a later line fails; that is extra workflow, never the same as one atomic transaction.

## Payment authorisation is an external side effect

Payment terms vary by provider contract, so define them for your design:

- **Authorisation** reserves funds on the customer's account without moving them.
- **Capture** takes the authorised funds.
- **Void** cancels an authorisation before capture.
- **Refund** returns captured funds, as a new financial event.
- **Settlement** is the later movement of money between banks.

This design assumes a provider with documented authorise, capture and void operations, stable operation identifiers, a query API and a stated idempotency window. These are requirements to verify for any real provider.

The customer pays through a provider-hosted flow, and Aster stores only the token or reference its contract allows, never raw card details. The server sets the amount and currency from the agreed price snapshot. A client-supplied total is never authoritative.

An authorisation timeout creates uncertainty: the provider may have authorised the funds even though Aster never received the answer. Keep the operation ID of the first attempt, then query the provider or retry with the same ID, as its contract allows. A new random ID on each retry defeats duplicate protection. Hold the order in a visible "payment pending" state until the outcome is known; releasing the stock and cancelling on a timeout would be guessing.

## A saga and its compensation policy

Garcia-Molina and Salem introduced the **saga** for long-lived transactions. A saga is a sequence of smaller transactions T1, T2, …, Tn, each paired with a **compensating transaction** that semantically undoes it. If the saga cannot finish, the system runs the compensations for the steps already completed, in reverse order. A compensation need not restore the exact earlier state, and other transactions may see the partial results in the meantime {REF:saga}. Their setting was a single database. Across an external payment provider and a warehouse, the same idea has an important consequence: compensation is a new business action with its own failure modes, never a rollback that erases history.

FIGURE saga | Aster's order saga as a state machine; confirmed, rejected and cancelled are terminal, and a failed void keeps the order in compensation_pending.

{FIG:saga} shows Aster's order saga as a state machine. On the normal path, reserving stock moves the order from `received` to `payment_pending`, and a verified authorisation with the reservation still active moves it to `confirmed`, after which fulfilment is requested. An unknown payment outcome keeps the order in `payment_pending` while Aster reconciles with the provider. Capture timing is a business and provider decision. After shipment, returns and refunds start a separate lifecycle.

The compensating actions follow the outcome:

- **Authorisation rejected**: release the reservation once and close the order as `rejected`.
- **Authorisation unknown**: hold a bounded pending state and reconcile with the provider.
- **Authorised, but the order cannot proceed** (for example, the reservation expired): request a void, then release stock when policy allows, and close the order as `cancelled`.
- **Compensation fails**: keep the order in `compensation_pending`, visible to an owner, with automatic retries or a manual process.

### Terminal states and late messages

Payment providers retry their notifications, so the same callback can arrive twice, late or out of order. Some states must therefore be **terminal**: once reached, no message can move the order out of them.

| State | Terminal? | A late `authorised` callback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `payment_pending` | No | Moves the order towards confirmation |
| `compensation_pending` | No | Recorded; the void continues |
| `confirmed` | Yes | Recorded as a duplicate; no change |
| `rejected` | Yes | Raised for investigation; no change |
| `cancelled` (after a void) | Yes | Recorded as a duplicate; no change |

Suppose Aster voids an authorisation, releases the lamp to another customer and marks the order `cancelled`. Then the provider's delayed retry of the earlier "authorised" callback arrives. If the code simply applied every "authorised" message, it would revive a cancelled order and confirm it against stock that now belongs to someone else. Recording the late callback while refusing the transition keeps both stock and money accountable. Lab 9 tests exactly this case.

Each step needs a durable identity, an expected predecessor state, an outcome, an attempt record and a next action. The coordinator can be a database-backed state machine inside the modular application. A workflow engine can help when processes become long and complex, but the payment and inventory contracts still decide which actions are safe.

## Reservation expiry is a concurrency problem

Suppose a reservation expires at 12:05 and the payment confirmation arrives at the same instant that the expiry job runs. The system needs one atomic transition that picks a single winner:

- If expiry wins, the reservation is `expired` and the stock is back on sale. The late authorisation cannot confirm the order against released stock; the order tries a fresh reservation or enters compensation and customer contact.
- If confirmation wins, the reservation is `committed`, and the expiry job finds nothing left to release.

Use a version number or a compare-and-set condition on the reservation's state, such as `UPDATE ... WHERE state = 'active'`. A timestamp alone cannot settle simultaneous actions, because background jobs run late, clients retry and provider messages arrive out of order.

The reservation invariant is simple to state: each reservation is in exactly one of `active`, `committed`, `released` or `expired`, with only the allowed transitions between them, and the stock balance changes exactly once per applicable transition. A duplicate release message returns the recorded outcome without adding the stock back a second time.

## Events, facts and customer messages

Emit `OrderConfirmed` only when the confirmation conditions are met, never when the HTTP handler merely starts. The warehouse consumer can record the event in an inbox with its local work item, yet the warehouse API may still need its own idempotency key or reconciliation. The external side-effect problem repeats at every new boundary.

Order events by a key aligned with the aggregate, such as the order ID. A single key for all customers' events would create a bottleneck without a business need. Refund events add to the history; they do not overwrite the order's paid amount.

Customer messages follow the verified state. "We received your checkout and are confirming payment" differs from "Your order is confirmed". While an outcome is unknown, support staff need the order ID, the payment operation reference, the reservation state and the last reconciliation result. Asking the customer to pay again is safe only when the provider's flow guarantees that the first attempt cannot also succeed.

## Performance and failure evaluation

At 50 attempts per second, a five-minute promotion brings 50 × 300 = 15,000 checkout attempts, far more than the 2,000 orders of an ordinary day. Separate catalogue traffic from checkout traffic: caching the catalogue does nothing for contention on one popular item. Measure hot-row behaviour, transaction retries, payment latency and the ratio of attempts to confirmed orders.

If the payment provider fails, browsing can continue while checkout shows "payment pending" or is paused. Decide how long reservations last and when to stop taking new checkouts; otherwise unpaid reservations can make all stock look sold. That fallback is a business policy, set with the business, as well as a circuit-breaker setting.

Test the risky interleavings:

- two customers buying the last unit at once;
- a repeated checkout key;
- a timeout after the provider has authorised the payment;
- duplicate provider callbacks;
- an authorisation that arrives after expiry or a void;
- a compensation that fails;
- a worker restart before it acknowledges its work.

A fast checkout that charges twice fails the design.

## Compare a distributed alternative

Split inventory, orders and payments into separate services when independent ownership, scale, security or release needs justify the extra interfaces. The split then needs explicit reservation expiry, idempotency, correlation, event contracts and shared operational responsibility. Shared identifiers do not create a shared transaction, and a synchronous chain of services adds their latencies and multiplies their failure points.

A sound decision record can keep the modular design now and name the conditions for extraction: measured contention on inventory beyond a stated limit, a change in warehouse ownership, or a team that needs its own release cadence. Clear internal boundaries keep that later extraction possible, though never free.

> **Common mistake.** Treating every provider callback as a command. Callbacks are reports that may arrive late, twice or out of order; the order's state machine decides whether a report changes anything.

## Key points

- Commerce rules such as "no overselling" and "no double charge" are invariants that need precise definitions before any technology choice.
- Reserve scarce stock with one conditional update and a unique operation ID inside a local transaction.
- A payment timeout means "unknown"; reconcile with the same operation ID before releasing stock or retrying.
- Sagas, in Garcia-Molina and Salem's sense, pair each step with a compensating business action; terminal states such as `cancelled` must ignore late or duplicate callbacks.
- Keep a modular design until measured needs justify distribution, and record the conditions for extracting services.
