# 25 Architect practice and the final capstone

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- Plan architecture work through discovery, design, delivery and operation, spending effort where uncertainty is greatest.
- Design a complete, traceable architecture dossier for the Meridian capstone.
- Evaluate a dossier against the scoring rubric and its pass conditions.
- Defend a design under questioning, separating knowledge from assumption.

## The architect's work cycle

Architecture work usually begins with discovery, moves through requirements and options to a decision and an implementation direction, and then returns to evidence during delivery and operation. The architect stays involved when assumptions meet real constraints.

Each meeting type has its own job:

- In a **discovery** meeting, ask for examples and measures.
- In a **design** session, make responsibilities and trade-offs explicit.
- In a **delivery review**, check that the implementation preserves the important decisions.
- During an **incident**, help connect symptoms to dependencies, and revisit any assumption that proved wrong.

Use time deliberately. For example, a detailed diagram is premature while the business outcome is unclear, and a long options paper is wasted when one option fails a mandatory constraint. Spend effort on the uncertainty that could change the design.

## The capstone brief

Design the first release of Meridian's service portal from the facts below. They consolidate the case used throughout the book; record any change you make in your assumption register.

**Organisation and workload.** Meridian supports equipment for 600 customer organisations, each a tenant. Three internal business units employ 40 service agents. The portal receives 100,000 service requests a year. A widespread equipment incident may produce 20 valid submissions per second for ten minutes. Attachments can be up to 10 MB.

**Systems.** The CRM stays in place for two years and provides a test environment, a service request API that honours an idempotency key for 24 hours, and a lookup by external reference; the vendor has not confirmed that the external reference is unique. ERP owns assets, contracts, entitlements, inventory and work orders, and offers a read API. When an agent approves a case, the CRM emits a `ServiceCaseApproved` event; the solution must turn it into an ERP work order without the portal creating work orders itself.

**Service targets.** The business wants a durable receipt within one second for 95 percent of valid submissions at the stated peak, excluding file upload, and proposes a 99.9 percent monthly success SLO for that receipt. Status changes should normally reach the portal within one minute. A thirty-minute CRM outage must not lose any accepted request.

**Access.** A customer may read the requests they submitted, and colleagues' requests only through an explicit organisation-wide delegation. A service agent needs the service-agent role, an assigned business unit and an authorised set of customer-organisation scopes; one agent may serve several tenants. A privacy owner will supply retention and deletion rules before production, so the architecture must support configurable, auditable lifecycle actions. Management analytics can refresh each morning.

**Team and constraints.** Six engineers will build and operate the first release, supported by shared identity and security teams. The sponsor asks for a vendor-neutral design, one Azure deployment option, one AWS option and a three-year cost comparison. You may use invented planning figures if you label them clearly; never present them as vendor prices.

## Your deliverables

Produce an architecture dossier that a reviewer can follow from the problem to the release evidence:

1. A two-page problem and scope brief, with outcomes, exclusions, stakeholders and assumptions.
2. A requirement register with at least twelve entries, including quality scenarios and owners.
3. A context view, a container view, a deployment view, a sign-in sequence and a submission or handoff sequence.
4. A relational data model and a data flow covering attachments, the ERP read model, analytics and lifecycle actions.
5. An API contract for submission, status retrieval and the CRM handoff, including errors and duplicate handling.
6. Four ADRs covering the initial application style, data ownership, asynchronous integration and the cloud operating model.
7. A threat model with at least six prioritised threats, controls, owners and verification methods.
8. An SLO worksheet, a capacity estimate, a disaster-recovery plan and two operational runbooks.
9. A three-year cost model with sensitivity analysis and a vendor evaluation.
10. A phased delivery and migration roadmap with release criteria and unresolved questions.

Use consistent identifiers throughout. A reviewer should be able to trace each critical requirement to a decision, an implementation responsibility, a test and an operational measure.

## Approach the work in stages

1. **Requirements and boundary.** Establish the requirements and the scope of the first release. Resolve or isolate the unknowns that could invalidate the design, especially the CRM's duplicate behaviour and rate limits. Draw a simple context view before choosing any product.
2. **Data and contract.** Design the data model and the submission contract. Decide exactly when the portal accepts responsibility and how that responsibility survives a failure. Add the handoff states, the ERP work-order path, the identity policy and the data lifecycle.
3. **Platform mapping.** Map the neutral responsibilities onto each cloud. Compare real differences, instead of drawing the same boxes with different logos. Include identity, network, telemetry, backups, recovery and operator access.
4. **Challenge.** Remove the CRM for thirty minutes. Restart a worker at an awkward point. Attempt access outside an agent's scopes. Reuse a submission key with different content. Ask how support finds the outcome of one complaint. Strengthen the design wherever the evidence is weak.

## Extend the portfolio across domains

After completing the Meridian dossier, choose one case from Chapters 22 to 24 and write a five-page comparative review. Explain which assumptions and decisions transfer, which do not, and why. Include one additional figure and a failure scenario. For commerce, focus on stock and payment invariants; for analytics, grain and correction policy; for migration, write authority and recovery. This synthesis prepares you for the design-studio work in Book 2.

## Scoring rubric

| Dimension | Points | Evidence expected |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Requirements and traceability | 15 | Measurable scenarios and a clear chain from requirement to test |
| Coherent boundaries and data ownership | 15 | Consistent views and protected invariants |
| Integration and failure handling | 20 | Retry, duplicate, outage and reconciliation behaviour |
| Security and privacy design | 15 | Resource checks, lifecycle, threats and verification |
| Operability and delivery | 15 | SLOs, recovery, telemetry and safe change |
| Economics and transition | 10 | Transparent assumptions and feasible intermediate states |
| Communication and judgement | 10 | Clear trade-offs, honest uncertainty and good answers under challenge |
| **Total** | **100** | |

A dossier passes with at least 75 points and no unresolved critical security or data-loss flaw. Both conditions apply: a high score cannot buy back a design that can lose accepted requests. Use Chapter 21 as a worked baseline and the exercise answers as assessment guidance, and defend every assumption you reuse rather than copying its diagrams.

## Present the design

Prepare a ten-minute presentation followed by fifteen minutes of questions. Spend the first minute on the problem and the outcome, then explain the main responsibilities and three consequential decisions. Leave time for residual risks, cost assumptions and the next evidence you need.

Practise answering these questions:

- Why this starting architecture?
- What fails when the CRM fails, and what does the customer see?
- How do you prevent access outside an agent's scopes?
- What exactly does a successful response guarantee?
- Why do you need each piece of personal data?
- What would make you choose a different cloud service?
- Which requirement is the most expensive?
- How would you reverse this decision?

When you do not know an answer, say so and describe the investigation that would settle it. An architect is expected to separate knowledge from assumption, rather than improvise a confident answer to every detail.

## Preparing for role discussions

Describe your background as a base for architecture work: software and data literacy, systems analysis, business processes and analytical research. Support each claim with your course portfolio and with real work you have done. Present the capstone as coursework, never as a client deployment.

In an interview case, clarify the scope before drawing. Ask who the users are and what load they bring, how sensitive the data is, which systems it integrates with, what availability and recovery the business needs, and which constraints and team skills apply. If answers are unavailable, state a small set of assumptions, then design and explain the trade-offs.

Read the position's actual responsibilities, because "IT architect" may mean infrastructure, solution integration, software design or enterprise planning. For example, an integration architect role will probe your handoff and reconciliation design more than your cost model. Use your portfolio to show transferable reasoning, and name the areas where supervised delivery experience would help you most.

> **In practice.** Reviewers remember a design whose limits are stated plainly. "We have not yet confirmed the CRM's uniqueness guarantee; until we do, uncertain handoffs go to manual review" earns more trust than a claim of exactly-once delivery.

## Exercises

25.1 Give a two-minute explanation of Meridian's proposed architecture to a service operations manager without using cloud product names.

25.2 Ask a peer to challenge three assumptions in your capstone. Record which decisions changed and which remained justified.

25.3 Write a personal development plan with one technical skill, one delivery experience and one communication practice to pursue next. Define evidence of progress for each.

25.4 A dossier scores 13, 12, 15, 12, 11, 7 and 8 on the seven rubric dimensions, in the order of the table. Its reviewer also notes that the worker marks a handoff `confirmed` before calling the CRM, so a crash during the call loses the request. Calculate the score and decide whether the dossier passes. What is the smallest change that would make it pass?

25.5 A peer's dossier claims "exactly-once delivery to the CRM". Write three review questions and the evidence you would ask for.

## Key points

- Architecture work cycles from discovery through design to delivery and operation; spend effort where uncertainty could change the design.
- The capstone dossier must trace each critical requirement to a decision, a responsibility, a test and an operational measure.
- The rubric totals 100 points; passing needs at least 75 and no unresolved critical security or data-loss flaw.
- Under questioning, separate what you know from what you assume, and name the investigation that would close each gap.
